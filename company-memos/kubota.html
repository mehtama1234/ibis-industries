Advantaged
What's happening

Combine harvester manufacturing is caught in a vise: collapsing farmer profit margins squeeze equipment demand, while tariff costs and right-to-repair activism reshape who gets to service the installed base. Ag machinery demand remains weak in 2026, but small-equipment and aftermarket software monetization offer a lifeline.

The investor question

The bet is that Kubota can move from selling hardware into selling data and services wrapped around legacy machines. Proof points: Can it own the software layer on machines already in the field? Can it fend off John Deere's dealer network and DIY repair movements?

The operator playbook

Kubota must invest heavily in connected equipment and subscription services before farmers turn to simpler, older machines. Capital is constrained by weak retail demand, so speed to market and integration efficiency matter more than raw R&D spending.

The core tension

Pivoting to software and services works only if existing equipment owners adopt it—but farmers buying used machines and smaller operators buying import-reliant knockoffs have no incentive to pay for Kubota's digital layer. If autonomous harvesting accelerates, the entire equipment cycle compresses.

Bottom line: Kubota survives the commodity squeeze only if it can sell recurring software revenue to existing owners faster than the installed base shrinks.
