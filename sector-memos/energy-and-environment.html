Coal & Natural Gas Power in the US

Coal & Ore Wholesaling in the US

The US Coal & Ore Wholesaling industry remains a mature, low-concentration distribution market whose 2025 revenue base is still near $19 billion, but 2026 demand indicators point lower as utility coal consumption retreats while nonfuel mineral demand stays supported by metals, critical-mineral and industrial supply-chain needs.

Tensions
  • AI productivity gains benefit capital-rich early adopters and scale players; workers in automation-vulnerable roles face wage pressure or displacement, and smaller firms cannot afford the compute or licensing costs that scale players absorb.
  • Power demand from data centers collides with grid capacity, environmental limits, and local political resistance; buildout is necessary but faces delays and will concentrate infrastructure investment geographically.
  • Scale players and capital-rich firms can afford reshoring and power-adjacent land; smaller manufacturers and regional players face margin pressure from tariffs without the resources to relocate.
  • Power buildout is massive but geographically lumpy—some regions and corridors win spectacularly while others remain peripheral, creating new winners and losers rather than uniform growth.

Power-Centric Geography Reorders Industrial Value

Data-center demand changing land value maps and generation and transmission access determining site viability are no longer isolated category quirks. They are showing up across technology & digital, energy & environment, and adjacent sectors, where the compute super-cycle and the real-estate reckoning are changing the operating baseline. That pushes the category toward a world where power access should be treated as a first-order go-to-market variable. The pattern is already visible in example operators such as CoreWeave, NextEra Energy, and Kinder Morgan Inc.

Power, Water, and Heat Become AI Externalities

Grid stress spilling into broader commercial and residential economics and cooling-water demand creating local permitting friction are no longer isolated category quirks. They are showing up across energy & environment, where the compute super-cycle is changing the operating baseline. That pushes the category toward a world where digital growth has real physical and political costs. The pattern is already visible in example operators such as NextEra Energy, Kinder Morgan Inc, and Baker Hughes.

The Compute Super-Cycle

  • Access to computing capacity and reliable electricity are now strategic constraints, not commodities.
