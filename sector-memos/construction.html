Construction memo · US · 2025-2026

Construction

Construction is a tale of two economies. Data-center buildout exploded 190% in 2025 starts, driven by AI compute demand, while traditional office construction hit a 10-year low. Residential builders are trapped in a $166–170B flat equilibrium, squeezed by tariff shocks, elevated rates, labor scarcity, and margin compression that is consolidating the industry around top-tier players (D.R. Horton, Lennar, PulteGroup). Remodeling and repair work (painting, insulation retrofit, plumbing supplies) stay stronger than new construction, but materials inflation and labor costs cut both ways. Infrastructure spending supports tunnel, transit, and bridge work, though volatility remains high. The bifurcation is brutal: scale players capture cash flow; smaller operators commoditize.

Tensions
  • Office-heavy cities and regions have stranded assets and weakened tax bases, while places with logistics corridors and power access attract capital; but infrastructure builds slowly, and new buildout faces political resistance and environmental scrutiny.
  • Housing lock-in reduces labor flexibility and pushes up service costs in high-cost areas, but policy options (zoning reform, construction acceleration) remain politically fraught and regionally contested.
  • Companies gain flexibility and margin defense through fractional staffing but lose depth of institutional knowledge and investment in junior development; long-term competitive capability may erode.
  • Workers gain autonomy in skill selection and portfolio construction but lose stability, benefits continuity, and access to subsidized training; inequality between credential-holders and non-specialists widens.
Signals
  • Adaptive reuse, housing lock-in, and power-adjacent land become recurring decision points.
  • Advisory retainers, project staffing, and portable admin layers gain relevance.
  • Operators lean harder on pricing architecture, pack-size changes, and cost takeout.
  • Domestic capability, logistics resilience, and buildout bottlenecks matter more in valuation.

Work Without the Old Firm

Skills and Credentials Replace Slow Career Ladders

Micro-credentials as employability insurance and shorter training loops replacing long formal pathways are no longer isolated category quirks. They are showing up across technology & digital, business services, and adjacent sectors, where the fractional worker and the labor squeeze are changing the operating baseline. That pushes the category toward a world where training providers need clearer links to wage gain and employability. The pattern is already visible in example operators such as Kumon, ADP, and TriNet.

The Fractional WorkerThe Labor Squeeze
Pressure points
  • Technology & digital operators have to absorb this shift in a market where the US business certification and IT schools industry is bifurcating: high-margin enterprise and compliance-linked credentials (cloud, cybersecurity) are growing at 12-16% annually, while commodity bootcamps and general IT courses face bankruptcies and commoditization as AI and free learning platforms make basic skills training structurally uncompetitive.
  • Business services operators have to absorb this shift in a market where educational services in the US—spanning K-12, higher ed, and training—remains the largest in the sector, but enrollment is fleeing public schools for private/charter alternatives, while AI and online platforms squeeze margins and force consolidation among smaller players.
Signals and consequences
  • Automation, scheduling discipline, retention tactics, and selective simplification accelerate.
