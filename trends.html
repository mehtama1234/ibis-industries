Cross-cutting trends

Read 25 industries side by side and the same forces keep reappearing — societal, cultural, technological, industrial, economic. These are the trends that show up across many industries at once, each linked to the ones it touches.
The through-line

Business is consolidating around scale, automation, and direct customer access, while middle-market players and traditional retail are hollowed out by technology and polarizing consumer demand.

11 industries Economic

Consolidation and scale economics

Smaller players are being squeezed out by larger ones that can absorb technology costs, negotiate better supplier deals, and spread fixed costs. This shows up as M&A waves, franchise rollups, and private-equity consolidation across sectors.

Who wins
Large incumbents, private-equity firms, and regional leaders that can acquire and integrate smaller competitors.
Who's squeezed
Independent operators, local players, and mid-market firms that can't achieve scale on their own.

7 industries Technological

E-commerce and direct-to-consumer cannibalization

Digital channels are replacing traditional retail and service middlemen. Companies that can't compete on speed, convenience, or price to the end-user are losing share to online alternatives and brand-direct channels. This flattens the economics of retail stores, call centers, and local service providers.

Who wins
Large online platforms, companies with direct-to-consumer brands, and businesses that bundle digital tools with physical experience.
Who's squeezed
Traditional retail stores, local service providers, and middlemen who don't own customer relationships.

6 industries Cultural

Premiumization and the hollow middle

The middle market is hollowing out. Consumers are either trading up to premium, branded, or experiential products, or cutting to the cheapest option. Mass-market generic offerings are becoming commodities with thin margins.

Who wins
Premium brands, luxury goods, high-end experiences, and budget retailers.
Who's squeezed
Mid-market retailers, mainstream brands without strong positioning, and businesses selling undifferentiated middle-price goods.

6 industries Cultural

Health-conscious consumption shift

Consumers are choosing healthier, more transparent, and more sustainable products. Soda is losing ground to energy drinks and functional beverages. Processed foods are seen as risky. This reshuffles winners and losers within categories and creates premium segments.

Who wins
Natural and organic brands, health-focused companies, functional beverage makers, and fitness and wellness services.
Who's squeezed
Sugary soda makers, fast food, and traditional vending and snack businesses.

Regulatory burden and compliance cost

Rules around emissions, healthcare, workplace safety, finance, and food are multiplying. Compliance is expensive and favors large companies with compliance teams. Small operators and new entrants get priced out.

Who wins
Large incumbents with compliance infrastructure, consultants, and companies in heavily regulated sectors.
Who's squeezed
Small operators, startups, and businesses without in-house compliance expertise.

5 industries Technological

Automation reducing labor and injury

Machines are replacing both routine jobs and the physical risks that come with them. Robots in manufacturing, chatbots in customer service, and automated systems in logistics are cutting headcount and reducing workplace claims. This lowers costs for winners but destroys jobs for losers.

Who wins
Capital-intensive producers, insurance companies, and businesses that own the automation tech.
Who's squeezed
Low-wage workers, labor-dependent service providers, and companies that can't afford to automate.

Subscription and recurring revenue

Businesses are shifting from one-time sales to recurring subscriptions and rentals. This creates predictable revenue but requires locking in customer retention. Platform companies bundle services to justify ongoing fees.

Who wins
SaaS companies, platform operators, and streaming and subscription service businesses.
Who's squeezed
Businesses with one-time-transaction models and companies that can't justify recurring fees.

Global trade volatility and supply-chain fragmentation

Tariffs, geopolitical tensions, and currency swings are making global supply chains riskier and more expensive. Companies are nearshoring, reshoring, or diversifying suppliers to reduce exposure. Export-dependent sectors are getting hit hardest.

Who wins
Domestic manufacturers, nearshoring beneficiaries, and companies with diversified supply chains.
Who's squeezed
Export-dependent farmers and commodity producers, companies with long global supply chains, and import-reliant retailers.

Vertical integration and bundling

Companies are integrating backward or forward, or bundling products and services, to lock in customers and justify pricing. Cable bundles broadband with TV. Banks bundle advice with wealth management. This raises switching costs and creates defensibility.

Who wins
Incumbent platforms with customer relationships, and companies that can afford to build suites of services.
Who's squeezed
Point-solution providers, unbundled specialists, and customers who want choice without lock-in.

Discretionary spending sensitivity

When the economy softens, consumers cut back on non-essentials: alcohol, dining, fitness, premium goods. These sectors have little pricing power in downturns and face cyclical demand.

Who's squeezed
Premium and discretionary sellers, lifestyle services, and cyclical industries.

4 industries Societal

Aging population reshaping demand and labor

Wealthier older customers are driving spending on healthcare, premium goods, and experiences. At the same time, fewer young workers are available to fill jobs. This creates both a bonanza for aging-focused services and acute labor-cost pressure everywhere else.

Who wins
Healthcare companies, luxury goods makers, financial advisors, and businesses offering personal services.
Who's squeezed
Low-wage employers, manufacturers relying on cheap labor, and physical-retail chains that depend on young foot traffic.

Commodity price volatility

Farmers and raw-material producers are buffeted by global price swings, weather, and demand shocks. Margins are thin and unpredictable. This makes the sector structurally weak and drives consolidation.

Who wins
Large commodity traders, hedging specialists, and consolidated producers that can absorb volatility.
Who's squeezed
Independent farmers, small commodity processors, and businesses with thin margins exposed to price swings.
