- 2022 baseline: $40.4 billion
- Takeaway: The US women's clothing retail sector is bifurcating into thriving luxury-and-specialty online and physical retail while mass-market brick-and-mortar chains face accelerating closures, squeezed by Shein and Temu from below and tariff-driven price pressure from all sides.
- Current dynamics: The industry is bifurcating sharply. Premium and specialty retailers—focused on affluent women, niche segments like plus-size and athletic wear—continue to grow, while mass-market and discount chains face accelerating closures. The tariff exemption's end in August 2025 has triggered broad price increases across imported goods, with estimated costs of $136 per family. E-commerce now accounts for nearly half of women's clothing sales (online $61.8B in 2025 vs brick-and-mortar $66.4B in 2026), but ultra-low-cost online platforms like Shein and Temu are eating share from traditional retailers. Store closures hit 7,323 in 2024 and roughly 6,000 in the first half of 2025 alone. Retailers are responding by expanding omnichannel offerings, developing localized warehousing (Temu and Shein), and focusing on higher-margin segments. Employment in online women's clothing grew 5.5% annually 2019-2024, while brick-and-mortar employment continues declining.

### Family Clothing Stores in the US (Retail)
- Market size: $226.5 billion USD (2026) | growth: 0.4% (2026); 2.3% (2025); 2.4% CAGR (2021-2026) | margin: 4.0% (2022 baseline); pressured lower by tariffs, inventory write-downs | businesses: Approximately 53,000 establishments (2022); declining through 2025-2026 via bankruptcies and closures | employees: 820,384 (2022 baseline); ongoing contraction from store closures
- 2022 baseline: $131.98 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: The US family clothing stores industry faces converging crises: tariffs doubling import costs in months, e-commerce capturing a quarter of fashion sales, and consumers slashing discretionary apparel spending amid inflation.
- Current dynamics: Tariff shocks have become the defining force. The December 2025 tariff rate of 35.1% on apparel imports is nearly 2.4 times the January 2025 rate of 14.7%, creating immediate cost pressure across inventory and pricing. Retailers are racing to nearshore production to USMCA-qualifying countries and exit China by H2 2026, but supply chain lag means Q1-Q2 2026 shipments will absorb the full tariff hit. Meanwhile, consumers are cutting apparel spending sharply—monthly spending fell 21.7% in early 2025 versus late 2024—with price-sensitive lower-income households pulling back furthest as apparel CPI jumped 5.1% year-over-year. E-commerce continues its relentless march, capturing 24% of fashion retail in 2026 (up from ~21% in 2024) and growing 4.9% annually, while traditional brick-and-mortar sales growth lags e-commerce by 62%. High-profile bankruptcies accelerated the contraction: Forever 21 liquidated all 364 stores (May 2025), Francesca's announced closure of 400 locations (2026), Torrid plans 180 store closures, and Liberated Brands liquidated 122 stores.
