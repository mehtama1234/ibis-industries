### Beer Wholesaling in the US (Business Services)
- Market size: $95.4 billion USD (2026) | growth: 1.0% (2025–2026); 1.1% CAGR (past 5 years) | margin: 4.8–5.0% on revenue | businesses: 1,393 beer distributors in US | employees: 115,900 (March 2026)
- 2022 baseline: $82.1 billion USD (2021)
- Takeaway: Beer wholesaling is a stagnant, heavily consolidated middle layer facing simultaneous commodity deflation (flat-to-negative volumes), tariff-driven cost shocks, and category cannibalization by spirits-based RTDs, with consolidation and labor scarcity as the only growth levers.
- Current dynamics: Wholesalers are trapped between shrinking beer demand and consolidation-driven labor displacement. Craft beer production fell 4% in H1 2026, overall beer volumes declined 5.7% in 2025, yet craft still holds 13.4% of market by volume (slightly up from 13.2%). Spirits-based RTD cocktails grew 16.4% in 2025 to $3.8 billion—a category that bypasses traditional beer wholesalers entirely. Employment actually ticked down 0.6% year-over-year (March 2026), even as wage pressure spiked 6.4% to $28.89/hour, signaling tight labor markets during consolidation disruption. Tariffs are fragmenting the import segment: canned beer faces 25% aluminum tariffs (April 2025 onward), but bottled imports (56.4% of 2024 volume) and draft (5.1%) escape, creating winners and losers by format. Direct-to-consumer shipping remains severely restricted—only 11 states allow interstate beer shipping—so the three-tier system still holds its gatekeeping power, but it is increasingly ornamental as category shift (not distribution model) becomes the real threat.

### Meat, Beef & Poultry Processing in the US (Manufacturing)
- Market size: $387.7 billion (2026) | growth: 4.2% CAGR (2021–2026); declining forecast next 5 years | margin: 5.1% (2026), up from 4.7% (2021) | businesses: 6,587 establishments (2026) | employees: 539,000+ workers (2021 baseline); employment growing at 0.65–0.7% annually
- 2022 baseline: $227.6 billion (2021, most recent in 2022 report)
- Takeaway: A $387.7B (2026) mature industry led by four consolidated firms, racing to automate away chronic labor shortages while navigating tariff shocks and China's lockout-and-opening cycle on exports.
- Current dynamics: The industry is in a rare squeeze between two pressures that normally don't collide. Input costs are soaring: beef cattle prices in 2026 are forecast to average $240 per cwt, up 7% from 2025, driven by tight cattle supplies and strong demand. But the labor market is unforgiving—plants can't fill meat-cutting and evisceration roles at any wage because the work is physically brutal, and immigration enforcement under Trump has tightened the labor supply. Processors are racing to automate de-hoofing, evisceration, and packaging roles using cobots and AI vision systems to escape this trap. Simultaneously, export markets blew up: China locked out US beef in 2025, wiping out 96% of sales, though the May 2026 trade deal restored some access. Plant-based competition, which was hyped as an existential threat in 2023–2024, has collapsed (2–3% retail share, down sharply) as consumers cited "lack of naturalness" and taste issues.
