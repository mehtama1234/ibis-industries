- Takeaway: Two halves of one industry: utility-scale solar is booming into a transmission crisis, while residential solar is collapsing after the 30% federal tax credit expired on December 31, 2025.

The US interconnection queue now contains 2,600 GW of projects with a median wait time of five years; transmission infrastructure takes 10-15 years to build while solar farms take 1-5 years. - 2022 baseline: $8.1 billion
- Takeaway: The US hydroelectric sector is caught between chronic western drought cutting output and aging infrastructure on one side, and resurgent private capital, government funding, and small-project innovation on the other—with climate volatility now the dominant operational risk.
- Current dynamics: Western drought is the defining constraint. Lake Powell, the Colorado River's second-largest reservoir, could hit minimum power pool by December 2026, potentially halting generation at a critical moment. The Colorado River Basin is 100% in drought as of 2025, having lost 27.8 million acre-feet of groundwater since 2002. Hydropower generation in 2025 reached 245 billion kWh—a recovery from 2024's record low, but still constrained. The EIA projects 259 billion kWh in 2026 (a 5% gain), yet that's 1.8% below the 10-year average. Snow drought—record warmth causing early snowmelt—has replaced traditional drought as the operational enemy.
