- 2022 baseline: $1.36 billion (2021, IBISWorld baseline)
- Takeaway: The US waste-to-energy plant operation industry—a $1.4 billion, nine-company sector operating 64 plants across 21 states—is at a turning point: data center demand for renewable baseload power and updated methane regulations (2027) create growth opportunities, but direct competition from landfill gas capture and advanced pyrolysis technologies, combined with electricity price volatility and construction slowdowns, constrain margins to 18-25% EBITDA and employment to 1,870 workers (down 5% over five years).
- Current dynamics: The industry stands at an inflection point. On the demand side, data center electricity consumption surged 17% in 2025 and AI-focused data centers are tripling power use, creating new contract opportunities for reliable baseload power; the tech sector signed 40% of all corporate renewable energy PPAs in 2025. On the supply side, landfill gas capture is intensifying competition—598 landfill gas systems now produce 64% of US renewable natural gas (2025), with 20 new projects launched in 2025 adding 39.9 Bcf biogas capacity; an updated methane rule (effective 2027) will push 705 additional landfills toward capture. Reworld's 2024 rebrand signals industry consolidation and shift away from pure incineration toward materials recovery and alternative fuels; the company processes 20.8 million tons annually and targets 25% more recycling/reuse by 2025. However, electricity price volatility persists—EBITDA margins range 18-25% when indexed (vs. landfill margins of 25-30%), and rising interest rates have slowed construction waste flows. ### Hydroelectric Power in the US (Energy & Environment)
- Market size: $6.7 billion in 2026 (US-specific); $12.5 billion in 2024 (broader market valuation) | growth: Up 3.1% in 2026; declined -4.8% CAGR from 2021–2026 | margin: 14.5% (2022 baseline); pressured by rising maintenance costs and lower utilization | businesses: 1,458 utility-scale plants; 2,300+ small hydro facilities (2025); 215 enterprises (2022) | employees: 9,000+ (2022 baseline); projected to grow 1.8% annually through 2027
- 2022 baseline: $8.1 billion
- Takeaway: The US hydroelectric sector is caught between chronic western drought cutting output and aging infrastructure on one side, and resurgent private capital, government funding, and small-project innovation on the other—with climate volatility now the dominant operational risk.
- Current dynamics: Western drought is the defining constraint. Lake Powell, the Colorado River's second-largest reservoir, could hit minimum power pool by December 2026, potentially halting generation at a critical moment. The Colorado River Basin is 100% in drought as of 2025, having lost 27.8 million acre-feet of groundwater since 2002. Hydropower generation in 2025 reached 245 billion kWh—a recovery from 2024's record low, but still constrained. The EIA projects 259 billion kWh in 2026 (a 5% gain), yet that's 1.8% below the 10-year average. Snow drought—record warmth causing early snowmelt—has replaced traditional drought as the operational enemy. Meanwhile, the industry is investing heavily in small hydro, with the DOE identifying nearly 97,000 non-powered dams suitable for retrofitting. Private equity interest surged: hydropower companies raised $718 million in equity in 2025 (up from $167 million in 2024).
