### Auto Parts Manufacturing in the US (Manufacturing)
- Market size: $74.9 billion (2026) | growth: 5.4% annual (2026) | margin: 5.0% (2026) | businesses: 1,091 (2026) | employees: 140,405 to 587,500 (2025-2026, depending on scope)
- 2022 baseline: $66.9 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: US auto parts makers are caught between 25% tariffs that make imports expensive, an accelerating EV transition that voids traditional component demand, and rapid nearshoring to Mexico that is now the only viable way to stay competitive.
- Current dynamics: The 25% tariff on imported auto parts (effective April-May 2025) has hit the sector hard, forcing suppliers to absorb costs or risk losing customers to rivals. Manufacturers are rapidly reorienting supply chains toward Mexico rather than reshoring to the US; in the first nine months of 2025, new Mexican investment surged 200%. Electric vehicle adoption is slowing demand for traditional exhaust systems, radiators, and engine components, while simultaneously driving demand for battery thermal management, electronics, and lightweight materials. The aftermarket remains a bright spot as the average US vehicle ages and needs repair. Bankruptcy filings among auto parts suppliers hit decade highs in 2025, and employment fell despite stable production, indicating aggressive automation. The industry is consolidating as larger players with R&D muscle acquire smaller specialists unable to invest in EV transition technology.
- Recent: April 3, 2025: Trump administration imposes 25% Section 232 tariffs on auto parts, covering over 150 categories (engines, transmissions, batteries, electronics, brakes, tires); takes effect May 3, 2025 for parts. | H1 2025: Stellantis reports €2.3bn ($2.7bn) net loss partly due to early tariff impacts; JLR profits nearly halve with 4% EBIT margin amid tariff pressure. | H1 2025: BMW, Mercedes, Volkswagen combined $6 billion loss attributed to US tariffs. | 2025: Mexican auto parts investment surges 200% in first nine months as suppliers nearshore production to avoid tariffs.

### Iron & Steel Manufacturing in the US (Manufacturing)
- Market size: $112.9 billion (2026) | growth: 3.3% in 2026 (vs 4.4% in 2025); declining 4.0% CAGR over 2021-2026 | margin: 12.3% EBITDA (Q2 2026 TTM); sub-5% net margins | businesses: 196 establishments (2026); down from 332 in 2022 | employees: 72,600 (2026); down 2.3% annually since 2021
- 2022 baseline: $124.1 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: US steel bounced from tariffs in 2025-2026 but faces structural headwinds: global oversupply, flat demand, and the permanent shift to lighter vehicles—making recovery unlikely despite short-term margin gains.
- Current dynamics: Tariffs imposed in February-June 2025 created a 50% import wall (with later refinements), triggering a price spike and domestic production surge—the US became third-largest steel producer in 2025. However, this windfall is eroding. Raw material costs (iron ore +9%, coking coal +17%, scrap +2% for 2025) are rising, and the margin between input and selling prices has tightened since spring 2025. Demand from construction (flat volume despite 2.6% spending growth) and automotive (10.2M units, steady but shrinking per-vehicle steel content as EVs grow to 42% of output by 2031) is not rallying. Global oversupply is the immovable problem: China exported a record 131 million tonnes of steel in 2025 (up 153% since 2020), and the OECD warns global excess capacity will grow from 640 to 745 million tonnes by 2028 as producers add 139 million tonnes of capacity while demand grows only 34 million tonnes. The industry is consolidating rapidly—Cleveland-Cliffs acquired ArcelorMittal and Stelco, and Nippon Steel completed its $14.2 billion buyout of US Steel in June 2025 under government oversight. E

### 3D Printer Manufacturing (Manufacturing)
- Market size: $30.5 billion (global 2025) | growth: 20.5% year-over-year (2025-2026) | margin: 0.8-2.8% (industry average) | businesses: 182 US manufacturers (2025) | employees: 5,569 (2025)
- 2022 baseline: $5.181 billion (2021, last IBISWorld figure)
- Takeaway: US 3D printer makers are racing to master metal printing and secure defense contracts as reshoring mandates drive domestic demand, while low-cost Chinese competitors erode margins in commodity machines.
- Current dynamics: Metal additive manufacturing has emerged as the fastest-growing segment, projected to reach $102 billion globally by 2035 at 24% CAGR. Aerospace and defense demand is the primary driver, with military orders and reshoring mandates (especially post-FY2026 NDAA restrictions on foreign 3D systems for DoD) creating new requirements for domestic supply. Industry consolidation is accelerating; Stratasys/Desktop Metal merger integrates polymer and metal capabilities into one platform. Simultaneously, low-cost Chinese systems (Bambu Lab, Creality) are gaining industrial relevance despite import restrictions, putting pressure on pricing for entry-level machines. Profitability remains under pressure as new entrants proliferate and material costs fluctuate, though aerospace aftermarket parts are creating profitable margin opportunities. Reshoring investments and supply chain redundancy are now strategic priorities for manufacturers, positioning 3D printing as a de facto reshoring tool.

### Soybean Processing (Agriculture)
- Market size: $54.1 billion (2025) | growth: -3.2% (2024); +0.5% (2025) | margin: ~5–7% (2025, down from 8–10% in 2023) | businesses: 143–150 processors (2025, mix of large and small operators) | employees: 7,725 (2025)
- 2022 baseline: $33.4 billion (2021, last IBISWorld reported)
- Takeaway: Record soybean crushing driven by renewable diesel mandates is overwhelming the market with cheap meal and collapsing margins, forcing consolidation and efficiency plays among processors while China tariffs and global oversupply cap near-term profits.
- Current dynamics: The renewable diesel boom is the defining force in 2025–26. The EPA's RVO mandates renewable fuel blending, creating nearly unlimited demand for soybean oil in renewable diesel production. This has triggered a 23% capacity expansion over three years, with over $6 billion invested in new crush facilities. Crush volumes hit record 2.54 billion bushels forecast for 2025–26, but the surge in meal output (as a byproduct of oil extraction) is flooding the market and crushing margins. Crush margins fell from $2–$3/bushel in 2023 to $1.10–$1.50/bushel in 2025 as four new major plants came online in North Dakota, Kansas, and elsewhere. China's 13% tariff (imposed 2018, still in place 2025) has reduced US soybean exports to China by 33% versus 2024, forcing reliance on domestic crush rather than global markets. Soybean prices themselves are volatile: peaked at $12.30/bu in early 2024, fell to $9.55 mid-August 2024, recovered to $10.30 end-of-2025, and remain around $11.60/bu in August 2026 despite oversupply concerns.
- Recent: 2024: Louis Dreyfus broke ground on new Ohio soybean-processing plant; North Dakota Soybean Processors opened Casselton facility; Incobrasa opened $250M Gilman, Illinois plant. | 2025: ADM, AGP, SD Soybean Processors brought new facilities online; EPA finalized RVO mandates for 2026–27 mandating maximum-capacity renewable diesel production; 75M bushels/year new capacity came online. | August 2026: ADM announced upgrades to four soybean crush plants unlocking ~700,000 metric tons additional annual capacity; crush margins remained compressed at $1.10–$1.50/bu despite record volumes.
