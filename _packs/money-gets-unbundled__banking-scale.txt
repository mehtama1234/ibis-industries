- Market size: $1.5 trillion (2026) | growth: 9.2% CAGR (2021-2026) | margin: 19.7% (2025) | businesses: 3,712 (2026) | employees: 1.85 million (2026)
- 2022 baseline: $963.4 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: US commercial banking is compressing margins as Fed rate cuts ease deposit costs, struggling with a looming $1.26 trillion commercial real estate maturity wall, and consolidating rapidly as smaller banks fall behind on technology and regulatory costs.
- Current dynamics: The industry is balancing three major pressures. First, the Fed's rate cuts (1.75 percentage points from September 2024 through Q4 2025) have compressed net interest margins even as they recovered to 3.39% by Q4 2025—highest since 2019—but the path forward is uncertain. Second, commercial real estate remains a latent risk: $1.26 trillion in CRE loans will mature by 2027, many originated at 4.3% now refinancing at 6.2%, while delinquencies hover near decade highs; banks are holding $2.7 trillion in CRE exposure. Third, deposit competition is fierce and deposit costs remain sticky—uninsured deposits surged $214.7 billion in Q4 2025 alone, suggesting clients are shopping rates. The 2023 failures (SVB in March, First Republic to JPMorgan in May, Signature in March) killed the mid-size bank consolidation wave temporarily but did not trigger systemic contagion, leaving the big four stronger relative to community banks.

### Industrial Banks in the US (Finance & Insurance)

- 2022 baseline: $11.6 billion in revenue (2021 baseline from 2022 report)
- Takeaway: Industrial banks—niche FDIC-insured lenders once dominated by Sallie Mae and Comenity—are experiencing a structural shift as major automakers and fintech firms win charter approvals to build embedded lending platforms, challenging the old incumbent model.
- Current dynamics: The industry is in the throes of a structural transformation driven by three overlapping forces. First, interest rate cuts (Federal Reserve cut 75 basis points in 2025, bringing rates to 3.50–3.75% by December 2025) are compressing net interest margins across all lenders, but ILCs are particularly vulnerable because interest income is 75% of their revenue. Second, regulatory policy flipped: the 2022 report warned that the FDIC moratorium on new charters was tight; in reality, as of July 2025, the FDIC opened the door by issuing a formal request for comment on its ILC supervision framework, effectively inviting large technology and automotive firms to apply—Ford and GM won conditional approvals within months. Third, a wave of de novo banking is reversing a decade-long drought; PayPal is mid-application, and automakers are embedding finance directly into dealer platforms. The old incumbent ILCs (Sallie Mae, Comenity) face shrinking deposit bases and margin squeeze; smaller, unprofitable banks are being shut down or acquired.
