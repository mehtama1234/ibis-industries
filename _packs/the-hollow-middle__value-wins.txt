### Dollar & Variety Stores in the US (Retail)
- Market size: $123.5 billion (2026) | growth: 1.4% annually 2021-2026 | margin: 4.7-5% (2026) | businesses: 9,400 companies operating ~39,000 stores | employees: Approximately 600,000+ workers
- 2022 baseline: $101.3 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: Dollar stores remain a resilient middle-tier retail format attracting consumers across income levels in an inflation-stricken economy, but growth is slowing as tariffs, organized theft, and Walmart competition compress margins and saturate the market.
- Current dynamics: The industry is locked between two opposing forces. On the demand side, persistent inflation and wage stagnation have made dollar stores the default destination for low-income households and an attractive "trade-down" venue for middle-income shoppers—a behavioral shift that proved structural in 2024-2025. On the supply side, tariffs (especially on Asian imports), organized retail crime (ORC), and labor-cost inflation are eating margins. Simultaneously, Walmart and Target have weaponized their size to undercut dollar stores on price while offering fresher produce and prepared foods, pulling away both price-sensitive and aspirational shoppers. Dollar stores are responding by remodeling stores, expanding fresh produce (DG Fresh now in ~7,000 locations, 2025), and ramping up omnichannel (e-commerce partnerships, retail media). Yet consumer sentiment collapsed to 48.2 in May 2026 (a record low), signaling that shoppers' financial strain is worsening rather than improving. The industry is stable but no longer growing—it's optimizing for a stagnant or slowly declining consumer base.
- Recent: May 2026: Consumer sentiment index hit record low of 48.2; Dollar General CEO reports customers say their financial situation has worsened. | May 2026: 87% of Dollar Tree stores are within five miles of a Walmart; tariffs and inflation eroding value perception. | Q1 2025: Dollar General reports 5.2% sales growth and +61 basis point gross margin expansion from $150 million labor and store remodeling investment. | 2025-2026: DG Fresh produce rollout continues to ~7,000 stores total (slow pace relative to 2023 momentum); 450 new DG stores and 4,250 remodels planned for 2026.

- Market size: $43.7 billion in 2026 (IBISWorld definition) | growth: 0.2% CAGR 2021-2026 | margin: Approximately 1.5-2% (from 1.8% in 2022; tightening due to theft and wage pressure) | businesses: 58,033 stores in 2026 | employees: approximately 175,000+ (2025 data suggests continued hiring)
- 2022 baseline: $37.3 billion in 2022
- Takeaway: The US convenience store industry is a mature, consolidating $43.7 billion sector where profit comes increasingly from prepared foodservice (39% of in-store profit) while fuel margins compress and organized retail crime eats away at profitability.
- Current dynamics: The industry faces a pivotal tension: margins are eroding at scale while consolidation is accelerating among smaller players. Organized retail crime—coordinated theft rings moving across multiple regions—cost retailers $47.8 billion in 2025 and is expected to hit $55 billion by 2028; convenience stores, with high cash flow and 24/7 operations, are prime targets (6% of all robberies occur at c-stores, with 70% going unresolved). Rising wages mandate consolidation to survive, but labor remains the biggest barrier to hiring despite 70% wage growth over a decade. Foodservice is the only high-margin growth lever—prepared food sales were flat in 2025, yet 80% of operators expect it to improve, betting on differentiation over channel-wide lift. Fuel margins compressed sharply as gas prices fell 5.9% from $3.30 to $3.11 per gallon in 2025; operators who automated fuel pricing using AI report 3.4% profit gains. ### Ethnic Supermarkets (Retail)
- Market size: $58.9 billion (2025) | growth: 0.1% (2025); 1.1% CAGR (2020-2025) | margin: ~3.3% (2022 baseline; 2025 data not yet published) | businesses: 16,042 (2025; down 1.3% from 2024) | employees: ~200,000+ (2022 baseline: 204,368; 2025 figures not yet published)
- 2022 baseline: $49.5 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: The US ethnic supermarkets industry is stalling at near-zero growth (0.1% in 2025) as mainstream grocers capture demand with private-label ethnic foods and online delivery scales.
- Current dynamics: Growth has collapsed to 0.1% in 2025 from 1.9% in 2024, despite favorable demographics. Tariff shock—647% increase in collections on food/ag in early 2025 vs. 2024—is squeezing margins from already-thin 3% levels; ethnic supermarkets source more specialty imports (Asian noodles, spices, fish sauce) than mainstream competitors, making them vulnerable. Business count is falling 11.3% annually, a hidden crisis: small independents cannot compete with large chains on price or with Amazon/Walmart on delivery. Mainstream supermarkets (Kroger, Albertsons) have successfully captured ethnic demand through private-label expansion, narrowing the unique value ethnic specialists once held. Immigration enforcement crackdowns in early 2025-2026 dented foot traffic in LA and other immigrant-heavy cities, adding labor scarcity to cost pressures. Large regional chains (Vallarta, H Mart, 99 Ranch) are expanding with flagships and food halls, pursuing scale and destination appeal, but they cannot build delivery networks at Amazon/Walmart pace.
- Recent: October 2024: PepsiCo acquires Siete Foods (Mexican-American CPG) for $1.2 billion, Big Food's bet on ethnic foods | Late 2023 onwards: Kroger launches Hispanic-inspired private label line; expands 2026 with Korean bulgogi, Chinese mandarin chicken, global meals | January 2026: Vallarta Supermarkets opens first Arizona store (Glendale, 49k sq ft); plans up to 15 stores over next decade | July 2025: 99 Ranch Market opens flagship in Flushing, Queens (37k sq ft, 23-vendor food hall); plans Las Vegas expansion
