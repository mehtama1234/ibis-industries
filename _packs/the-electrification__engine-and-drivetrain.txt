### Automobile Engine & Parts Manufacturing in the US (Manufacturing)
- Market size: $43.8 billion in 2026 | growth: 2.5% in 2026, 1.5% CAGR 2021–2026 | margin: 8.1% (2022) | businesses: 774 establishments, 633 enterprises (2022) | employees: Approximately 62,000–63,000 workers (2022 baseline: 62,106; 2027 projection: 63,419)
- 2022 baseline: $37.3 billion
- Takeaway: Gasoline engine manufacturing is no longer dying; it is evolving into a three-fuel ecosystem (gasoline, hybrid-electric, mild-hybrid 48V) while tariffs and supplier bankruptcies force consolidation and nearshoring.
- Current dynamics: The sector faces a paradox: electric vehicles are growing (hybrids up 10+ percent year-on-year), yet gasoline engine demand has *not* collapsed. Instead, OEMs are stretching ICE platform life by investing in turbocharging, variable compression, and 48-volt mild-hybrid architecture. Tariff regimes—US 25% on auto parts effective April 2025, Mexico 25–50% tariffs on non-FTA parts starting January 2026—are crimping margins at suppliers and forcing nearshoring decisions. CAFE standards have been reset in June 2025 to exclude EV credits from compliance calculations, giving OEMs more breathing room to sell gasoline vehicles without penalty. Bankruptcies and restructurings among smaller suppliers (First Brands, Accuride, Tayco) signal consolidation pressure. Denso, Bosch, and Continental are doubling down on EV electronics and thermal systems, while traditional engine makers are repositioning as powertrain integrators—producing not just gasoline blocks but hybrid modules and control systems.
- Recent: First Brands (FBG) files Chapter 11 bankruptcy (September 2025); portfolio included Raybestos, Centric, Cardone, Autolite, FRAM engines and parts | US imposes 25% tariffs on autos and auto parts, effective April 2025 | Mexico introduces 25–50% tariffs on auto parts from non-FTA countries, effective January 2026 | Stellantis announces $13 billion US investment plan (October 2025); Kokomo engine plant to produce new four-cylinder GMET4 EVO starting 2026 with 100+ new jobs

### Automobile Transmission Manufacturing in the US (Manufacturing)
- Market size: $44.6 billion (2026) | growth: -1.7% CAGR 2021-2026; declining 2.9% in 2026 | margin: Compressed; specific 2026 margin not disclosed but deteriorating from cost pressures | businesses: 338 manufacturing establishments and ~435 enterprises (down 1.5% CAGR 2021-2026) | employees: Approximately 93,000 workers (2022); manufacturing sector lost 68,000 jobs during 2025
- 2022 baseline: $54.7 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: Transmission manufacturers are shrinking as EVs eliminate transmission demand, hybrid sales offer only temporary relief, vehicle demand falters, and rising tariffs and input costs compress profitability.
- Current dynamics: The industry is caught between two opposing forces. On one side, hybrid vehicles are booming—hybrid sales surged 20.5% through mid-2026—and hybrids need efficient multi-speed transmissions, creating a temporary lifeline for traditional transmission makers. On the other side, battery electric vehicles do not need conventional transmissions at all; BEVs dropped to 5.9% of market share by July 2026 after federal tax credits expired in September 2025, but long-term EV adoption remains a systemic threat. Light vehicle sales are skittish, hitting 16.3 million units annualized in July 2026, down 1.4% year over year, and tariffs, geopolitical tensions, and volatile input prices (steel, crude oil) have squeezed margins and triggered manufacturing job losses. Consolidation is accelerating: Allison Transmission acquired Dana's off-highway business for $2.7 billion in early 2026, and the closure of TransAxle (a Pennsylvania-based drivetrain remanufacturer) in 2025 signaled weakness among smaller suppliers. ### Engine Rebuilding & Remanufacturing (Manufacturing)
- Market size: $14.59 billion USD (2025) | growth: 3.8% CAGR (2025-2035); 4.2% CAGR for pure remanufactured engines (2025-2033) | margin: Estimated 5–7% (2025), pressured by labor costs, metal scrap prices, and sourcing difficulty for complex engine cores | businesses: Approximately 90–95 establishments; consolidation ongoing; major players include Jasper Engines and Transmissions, ATK Engines, and Remanufactured Engines | employees: Estimated 5,700–6,000 (2025), up modestly from 5,545 (2019)
- 2022 baseline: $2.226 billion USD (2019 data in the report; 2022 not explicitly given)
- Takeaway: A mature but now revived repair-vs.-replace niche where engine cores are restored cheaper than new ones and sold to keep aging vehicles on the road, turbocharged by tariffs, vehicle age, and fleet maintenance demand.
- Current dynamics: The industry is in a rare inflection point. For decades it was countercyclical to prosperity—good when consumers were broke, bad when they had money. The 2022 report predicted slow decline as new engines improved and lasted longer. But 2025 rewrote the playbook. New-vehicle prices spiked (tariffs added $2,000–$4,000 to many models), interest rates stayed elevated (financing a $35,000 vehicle is painful), and supply of used cars dried up. Vehicle owners, facing $10,000–$15,000 to replace an engine, are staying with their aging cars—average fleet age jumped from 12.4 years (2024) to 12.8 years (2025). That's the industry's tailwind. Simultaneously, supply-chain costs surge: 44% of repair parts enter the US from abroad; tariffs made sourcing engine cores and specialized components 15–25% more expensive year-over-year. Labor costs rise as technicians require certification for hybrid/diesel work. Metal commodity prices (steel, aluminum) remain elevated.
