### Diagnostic & Medical Laboratories in the US (Healthcare)
- Market size: $90.9 billion (2026) | growth: 3.6% in 2026; 0.9% CAGR from 2021-2026 | margin: ~24-25% EBIT margin at the largest players
- Takeaway: The US diagnostic lab industry is consolidating under reimbursement pressure, losing volume to decentralized testing, and struggling to hire enough technicians.
- Current dynamics: The big story is a two-front squeeze. On the payer side, Medicare payments are locked flat through 2026, but PAMA legislation will permit up to 15% annual cuts beginning 2027 based on 2025 private-payer data—labs are already bracing for revenue hits. On the supply side, the technician shortage remains acute: only about half the needed lab professionals are being trained annually, forcing wage increases to compete for workers who are in short supply. On the customer side, hospitals and health systems are increasingly building in-house labs or acquiring their own diagnostic capabilities, cutting into the traditional outside lab model; simultaneously, point-of-care testing in clinics is growing (68,000 healthcare facilities now run POCT programs, up from 42,000 in 2020), and at-home testing is exploding at 8-14% per year for direct-to-consumer players like Everlywell and LetsGetChecked. ### Cardiologists (Healthcare)
- Market size: $67.2 billion (2025) | growth: 2.5% CAGR (2020-2025); 2.0% growth in 2025 | margin: 13.9% of revenue (2025)
- Takeaway: The US cardiologists industry—worth $67.2 billion in 2025 with 2.5% CAGR—is collapsing private practice, absorbing private equity deals, grappling with severe workforce shortages and reimbursement cuts, while AI and remote monitoring transform clinical workflows.
- Current dynamics: The cardiology industry is caught between soaring demand and a shrinking supply of cardiologists. Heart disease remains the leading cause of death in the US (1 in 4 deaths), with obesity prevalence now at 41.9% (2025-2026) and heart failure cases growing from 6.7 to 7.7 million adults in under five years. Yet the profession is in crisis: the cardiologist-to-patient ratio will deteriorate from 1 per 1,087 patients (2025) to 1 per 1,700 patients (2035), while burnout and early retirements are projected to cost the field 547 cardiologists annually by 2031. Private practice has virtually disappeared—90% of cardiologists now work for hospitals or PE-backed consolidators—driven by unsustainable reimbursement (Medicare payments fell 29% from 2005-2021 while hospital payments rose 70%) and administrative burden.
