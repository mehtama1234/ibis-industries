- Takeaway: AI infrastructure demand has turned the colocation industry from a physical space problem into a power-grid problem, making electricity access and regulatory tariffs the new competitive moats.
- Current dynamics: The colocation market is experiencing a historic inversion: until 2020, supply constraints were physical (not enough real estate). Now they are energy constraints (grid cannot deliver enough power). AI workloads—training at 30-100+ kW per rack, inference at 10-20 kW—demand liquid cooling, purpose-built infrastructure, and direct power from generators or renewable sources. ### Data Processing & Hosting Services in the US (Technology & Digital)
- Market size: $485.7 billion in 2026 | growth: 5.7% in 2026; forecast 16.8% CAGR through 2032 | margin: 6.7% profit margin in 2021; likely lower now due to high capex-to-revenue ratio and commodity pricing pressure | businesses: Approximately 70,000 enterprises; consolidation among the largest players is accelerating, but the number of solo-operator data processing specialists is also rising | employees: 636,786 employees in 2021; growth forecast at 3.4% annually to 2026, reaching 753,671
- 2022 baseline: $196.5 billion in 2021
- Takeaway: AI is reshaping the data processing and hosting industry from a growth-stage, outsourcing-driven market into a power-constrained, hyperscaler-dominated infrastructure arms race where talent and electrical capacity are more scarce than capital.
- Current dynamics: AI demand is the defining force: the five largest hyperscalers will spend $630 billion on infrastructure in 2026, up 36% from 2025, with $450 billion explicitly targeting AI (chips, GPUs, accelerators). This spending has created a brutal constraint on power—US data center electricity demand surged from 23 GW in 2023 to 42 GW in 2026, wholesale prices near major data centers have risen 267%, and PJM capacity prices jumped 833% in one year. The workforce shortage is acute: 90% of operators cite staffing constraints as a bottleneck, with demand for AI infrastructure specialists, high-voltage engineers, and cooling technicians far outpacing supply. Consolidation is intensifying in the tier-one space—CoreWeave acquired Core Scientific, Equinix closed a $15 billion joint venture with GIC to build hyperscale facilities, and Digital Realty is expanding into new geographies—while smaller operators are either specializing (e.g., edge compute, liquid cooling) or disappearing.
