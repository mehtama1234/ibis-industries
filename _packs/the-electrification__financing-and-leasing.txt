### Fleet Car Leasing (Transport & Logistics)
- Market size: $10.6 billion US in 2026 | growth: 3.7% CAGR 2021-2026 | margin: 13.5% (2022); trending toward 14.1% (2022-2027 projection) | businesses: 238 active fleet leasing businesses | employees: 6,021 workers (2022 baseline); 2026 data not yet public
- 2022 baseline: $8.7 billion
- Takeaway: Fleet car leasing is a $10.6 billion, 3.7%-growth industry under pressure from tariffs, EV depreciation uncertainty, and a tidal wave of used-vehicle inventory that threatens to crack the residual-value economics underlying the business model.
- Current dynamics: Demand is picking up as e-commerce drives freight activity and corporate spending recovers from pandemic lows. However, tariffs have become the dominant cost driver—vehicle prices spiked mid-2025 when US weighted-average tariff rates hit 20%, forcing lease operators to choose between absorbing costs or passing them to already-price-sensitive customers. EV adoption remains the industry's Achilles heel. Lessees are hesitant to lock in three-year EV leases because residual values are uncertain and far-field (early 2026 data shows steeper EV depreciation than traditional vehicles). A looming inventory cliff complicates returns: off-lease volumes are expected to surge 25.7% in 2026, flooding wholesale markets with used vehicles and pressuring residual values downward. Meanwhile, interest rates have begun declining, easing borrowing costs for operators but not yet translating to pricing relief for end customers.

### Car & Automobile Manufacturing in the US (Manufacturing)
- Market size: $393.3 billion (2026) | growth: −1.0% (2026); +2.3% CAGR (2021–2026) | margin: 4.4% (2026) | businesses: 190 establishments; Big Three (Ford, GM, Stellantis) dominate; 45 major competitors | employees: ~151,000 direct (2026 estimates)
- 2022 baseline: $104.2 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: Tariff shocks, an EV recount, and labor-cost misalignment have flattened 2026 margins despite stable demand.
- Current dynamics: Tariffs are the immediate shock. A 25% import tariff on all vehicles (April 2025) raised consumer car prices by $5,000–$8,900 for imports and $1,600–$2,000 for domestic cars due to steel/aluminum costs. The tariff added $108 billion in costs to the industry, with Ford, GM, and Stellantis absorbing $41.7 billion collectively. China slapped retaliatory 10–15% tariffs on US vehicles, closing that market to Detroit. Meanwhile, EV momentum has stalled—EVs and hybrids were only 20% of new US sales in 2024, and 2025 EV sales actually declined 4% versus 2024, with Tesla's market share slipping from 49% (2024) to 45% (2026). Hybrid demand is robust, but sedans and compact cars—the industry's defined core—remain unprofitable. UAW contracts signed in 2023 cost GM $9.3 billion and Ford $8.8 billion through 2028, locking in labor rates that are uncompetitive versus non-union plants (Toyota, Honda, Hyundai facilities average $52/hour). About 29,000 parts manufacturing jobs disappeared in 2025, though assembly plants held steady.
