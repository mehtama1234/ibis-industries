- 2022 baseline: $903 million (2021 estimate from IBISWorld; report dated Jul 2021)
- Takeaway: Expert networks are shifting from pay-per-call consulting to AI-driven transcript synthesis and agentic research workflows, with consolidation accelerating around players who control large, curated call libraries.
- Current dynamics: The expert networks industry is at an inflection point where AI is rapidly redefining the business model itself. Historically, revenues came from scheduling one-off phone consultations between clients and experts at $1,200–$5,000 per call. Today, the profit engine is shifting toward platforms that ingest thousands of past call transcripts, apply large language models to synthesize insights on demand, and automate the sourcing and vetting of new experts. AlphaSense's acquisition of Tegus in mid-2024 was a watershed moment: by merging the two largest transcript libraries, the combined entity can now generate synthetic research from existing data at near-zero marginal cost, undercutting traditional pay-per-call models. This threatens the historical revenue stream but is offset by massive efficiency gains—a typical research project that took 2–5 days now takes 2 hours. Meanwhile, AI voice agents and automated channel checks (costing $100–$300 per data point instead of $2,000 per expert call) are emerging as a parallel threat from outside the industry. ### Management Consulting in the US (Business Services)
- Market size: $370 billion (2026) | growth: 4-5% CAGR (2025-2031) | margin: 12.6% (2022 baseline); now under pressure as wage costs climb and AI automation reduces billable hours per project | businesses: 1.6 million+ (2022 baseline); now fragmented across mega-firms, mid-market, and boutique specialists | employees: 2.2+ million (2022 baseline); shifting mix—entry-level flat or declining, senior roles +55% since 2020, AI roles soaring
- 2022 baseline: $329.1 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: The US consulting industry is growing but hollowing: AI is replacing junior work, federal spending is collapsing, and clients are demanding outcomes not hours.
- Current dynamics: AI has become the industry's existential event. By 2025, AI roles outnumber entry-level consultants at top firms for the first time—a reversal from 2015 when there were nearly four junior consultants per AI role. Hiring overall is down 20% from 2023 peaks; consultant-specific roles are down 40%. Simultaneously, federal agencies cut roughly $5 billion in consulting contracts in early 2025 as part of austerity measures, forcing layoffs at Deloitte, Accenture, and IBM. Clients have also grown hostile to traditional hourly billing: CEOs now demand measurable ROI, pushing firms toward outcome-based pricing, subscription models, and contingency fees. Compensation practices are inverting—AI specialists command 56% wage premiums over traditional software roles, while junior consultant salaries have stalled. The industry is rapidly stratifying: the Big Four control scale and federal access; boutique specialists win on depth and agility; mid-market firms are squeezed.
