- 2022 baseline: $50.9 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: Chain restaurants are caught between structural wage inflation, tariff pressures, and a consumer base choosing value-driven casual dining over premium sit-down experiences.
- Current dynamics: Chain restaurants are caught in a structural cost-margin trap. Labor costs have jumped 39% since pre-pandemic levels, with minimum wages now $16-$20 per hour in major states (California FAST Act $20 for national chains; Washington $16.66; Florida heading to $15 by September 2026). Food inflation sits 34% above pre-pandemic, and tariffs have forced 62% of operators to raise menu prices. Consumer spending at restaurants is growing only 1.3% in real terms, while 60% of operators report deteriorated business conditions in 2025 and 42% are unprofitable. Fast-casual's weakness has paradoxically strengthened casual dining: Chili's and Texas Roadhouse thrive by offering $12 sit-down deals that undercut $15 limited-service entrees. However, consumers are also defecting to grocery and convenience stores, takeout apps, and at-home experiences, shrinking traffic across all formats.

- 2022 baseline: $296.5 billion (2021)
- Takeaway: A mature, fragmented $424B industry growing below 1% in 2026 as traffic deflates from consumer retrenchment and GLP-1 adoption, labor costs rise structurally (California $20/hour minimum now core cost floor), tariffs push food prices relentlessly higher, and chains make desperate bets on AI automation to replace payroll.
- Current dynamics: Fast food has entered a structural squeeze. Traffic declined 0.3% in 2025, accelerating to -2% in Q4 2025 vs Q4 2024, driven by softer consumer demand and the GLP-1 appetite-suppression effect. Chains are trying to offset lower visit frequency by raising average check (+3.3% year-over-year in 2025) through bundling and upsells, but this only works until customers stop coming entirely. Labor scarcity and rising wages—especially California's sector-specific $20/hour minimum that took effect April 2024—forced many chains to cut hours (overtime largely eliminated) and boost prices 8-12%. Tariff-driven food inflation is real: low beef inventories from avian flu pressure on poultry, combined with 15% of US food imports now tariffed, created a two-year climb in commodity costs. Operators absorbed much of this in margin, unable to pass full costs to price-sensitive consumers without losing traffic.
