### Wheat, Barley & Sorghum Farming in the US (Agriculture)
- Market size: $16.2 billion (2025) | growth: -0.2% projected 2026 | margin: ~10-12% (2025, down from 13.1% in 2022) | businesses: 73,932 (2025) | employees: 93,733 (2025)
- 2022 baseline: $15.7 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: Wheat, barley, and sorghum farming—a mature, export-driven, family-farm-dominated commodity sector—is reeling from a perfect storm of severe Plains drought (production down 29%), China's retaliatory sorghum tariffs, and six-year-low prices, pushing net farm income into structural decline despite federal support.
- Current dynamics: The industry is in acute structural stress. Wheat production fell 29% from 2025 to 2026 (1.402B to 990M bushels), driven by the worst Plains drought since records began, with 63% of winter wheat in drought areas as of June 2026. Yield plummeted to 46.7 bu/acre from 54.9 in 2025—the lowest in over a decade. China's 44-49% tariffs imposed April 2025 decimated sorghum exports (down 97% to China by July 2025), though modest relief arrived in November 2025 when Beijing paused some tariffs. Wheat prices have crashed from the 2022 Ukraine-war spike ($219/ton baseline) to $5.00-5.12 per bushel in early 2026—a 6-year low—because global supplies are bloated and demand is soft. Farm income is collapsing: wheat receipts fell from $11.2B (2024) to $9.86B (2025) to $9.5B (2026). Subsidies, once a reliable safety net, face congressional pressure for reductions. The industry remains heavily export-dependent, making it vulnerable to currency fluctuations and geopolitical trade wars.
- Recent: June 2026: USDA forecasts US winter wheat production at 1.048 billion bushels (down 25% from 2025), the lowest since 1965/66; drought devastates Plains with Kansas yields projected at 37 bu/acre vs. 51 in 2025. | April 2025: China imposes 44-49% tariffs on US sorghum, wheat, corn, and soybeans; US sorghum exports to China collapse 97% by July. | February 2026: Wheat farm price rises to $5.12/bu (up from $5.01 in January), but remains a 6-year low as global stocks swell and US ending stocks hit 6-year highs. | November 2025: China pauses 24% retaliatory tariff and rolls back duties of up to 15% on some US ag shipments, retaining 10% base levy; modest sorghum and wheat purchases resume but remain well below pre-tariff levels.

### Soybean Processing (Agriculture)
- Market size: $54.1 billion (2025) | growth: -3.2% (2024); +0.5% (2025) | margin: ~5–7% (2025, down from 8–10% in 2023) | businesses: 143–150 processors (2025, mix of large and small operators) | employees: 7,725 (2025)
- 2022 baseline: $33.4 billion (2021, last IBISWorld reported)
- Takeaway: Record soybean crushing driven by renewable diesel mandates is overwhelming the market with cheap meal and collapsing margins, forcing consolidation and efficiency plays among processors while China tariffs and global oversupply cap near-term profits.
- Current dynamics: The renewable diesel boom is the defining force in 2025–26. The EPA's RVO mandates renewable fuel blending, creating nearly unlimited demand for soybean oil in renewable diesel production. This has triggered a 23% capacity expansion over three years, with over $6 billion invested in new crush facilities. Crush volumes hit record 2.54 billion bushels forecast for 2025–26, but the surge in meal output (as a byproduct of oil extraction) is flooding the market and crushing margins. Crush margins fell from $2–$3/bushel in 2023 to $1.10–$1.50/bushel in 2025 as four new major plants came online in North Dakota, Kansas, and elsewhere. China's 13% tariff (imposed 2018, still in place 2025) has reduced US soybean exports to China by 33% versus 2024, forcing reliance on domestic crush rather than global markets. Soybean prices themselves are volatile: peaked at $12.30/bu in early 2024, fell to $9.55 mid-August 2024, recovered to $10.30 end-of-2025, and remain around $11.60/bu in August 2026 despite oversupply concerns.
- Recent: 2024: Louis Dreyfus broke ground on new Ohio soybean-processing plant; North Dakota Soybean Processors opened Casselton facility; Incobrasa opened $250M Gilman, Illinois plant. | 2025: ADM, AGP, SD Soybean Processors brought new facilities online; EPA finalized RVO mandates for 2026–27 mandating maximum-capacity renewable diesel production; 75M bushels/year new capacity came online. | August 2026: ADM announced upgrades to four soybean crush plants unlocking ~700,000 metric tons additional annual capacity; crush margins remained compressed at $1.10–$1.50/bu despite record volumes.
