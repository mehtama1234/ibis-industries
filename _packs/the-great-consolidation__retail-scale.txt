### Auto Parts Stores in the US (Retail)
- Market size: $77.1 billion (2026) | growth: 1.6% (2026) | margin: 8.8% (2022) | businesses: 44,600+ establishments (2022 baseline) | employees: 417,098 (2022 baseline)
- 2022 baseline: $73.6 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: The $77 billion US auto parts stores industry (2026) faces slowing growth (1.6% in 2026) as electric vehicles require fewer traditional repairs, e-commerce takes online sales, and tariffs on imported parts raise costs.
- Current dynamics: The industry is being remade by three colliding forces. First, electric vehicles are structurally shrinking the parts basket per vehicle: EVs need no oil changes, fewer spark plugs, simpler drivetrains, and less frequent brake service due to regenerative braking. Second, e-commerce—led by Amazon ($13.7 billion 2026) but also RockAuto, eBay Motors, and Walmart—has captured the price-sensitive DIY buyer; U.S. online auto parts sales (excluding marketplaces) reached ~$23 billion in 2025 and are forecast at 4.6% annual growth, while including Amazon/eBay/Walmart the total channel grows 6.7% annually. Third, tariff volatility (25% on Chinese parts from April 2025, fluctuating with trade negotiations) has raised wholesale costs for retailers and made inventory planning unpredictable. Meanwhile, the average vehicle age climbed to 12.8 years in 2025 (a record), which theoretically should drive parts demand for aging fleets, but this benefit is offset by EV adoption and fewer DIY repairs as modern cars become too complex for amateur mechanics. ### Sporting Goods Stores in the US (Retail)
- Market size: $120.6 billion (2026) | growth: 1.1% in 2026, -0.5% CAGR 2021-2026 | margin: 4.7% net margin (Dick's as of Q2 2026); gross margin ~37% | businesses: Dick's (10% market share, 850+ stores), REI, Academy, Big 5, independent specialists | employees: 465,543 (2026), 1.3% growth year-over-year
- 2022 baseline: $67.2 billion (2022)
- Takeaway: The US sporting goods retail industry is consolidating rapidly (led by Dick's $2.5 billion Foot Locker acquisition), but is constrained by weakening consumer discretionary spending, tariff cost headwinds, and structural pressure from e-commerce and brand direct-to-consumer strategies.
- Current dynamics: The industry is split between winners and losers. Dick's Sporting Goods controls roughly 10% of the fragmented market and is using scale to drive acquisition integration and margin recovery. Nike and other major brands are recalibrating their direct-to-consumer strategies, re-engaging key wholesale partners like Dick's and Foot Locker while also protecting brand positioning. Middle-income consumer retrenchment, exacerbated by tariff pass-through and exhausted pandemic savings, is squeezing transactions. Foot Locker's historical mall-based locations and brand direct distribution headwinds made the acquisition costly; Dick's is consolidating back-office functions and closing redundant locations. E-commerce continues to steal share, with online sporting goods sales reaching $39.2 billion in 2025 (2.3% growth), while brick-and-mortar stores compete through experiential formats, expert service, and omnichannel integration. Tariffs on imported footwear and athletic apparel are raising landed costs by over 10 percentage points in some categories, putting pressure on both margins and prices.
