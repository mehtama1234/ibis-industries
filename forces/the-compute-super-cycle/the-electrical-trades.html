C · The physical trades of the buildout

The Electrical Trades Cash In

Electricians, HVAC techs, and turbine makers are the picks-and-shovels of the compute buildout, and they're getting rich while labor scarce and tariffs squeeze competitors.

The compute super-cycle has turned the electrical and mechanical trades from boring infrastructure work into the bottleneck that limits how fast AI can be built. Electricians are in acute shortage. HVAC contractors are commanding premium rates. Turbine and engine makers face regulatory deadlines they can't dodge and tariff costs they can't escape. This is not a nice problem to have if you're in one of these trades—it's a license to print money, at least for now.

What changed

Electricians, historically caught between a dying residential market and flat commercial demand, have suddenly become the scarce labor holding back data-center construction. In 2026, data-center electrical work alone reached $50.7 billion annually, up 28 percent year-over-year. That surge is real and growing faster than the industry can supply labor. The US electricians market is $370.9 billion in 2026, but growth is modest overall—down 1.1 percent in 2026—because the work is concentrating in high-skill, high-margin specialties (solar, EV charging infrastructure, data-center power distribution) where experienced workers command $76,000 to $110,000+ per year. Tariffs have crushed margins for smaller contractors: copper wiring jumped 18 percent in price, electrical panels up 22 percent, because the tariff wave on imported materials hit steel, aluminum, and components at 25 percent rates starting in 2024. Smaller contractors with thin 5-6 percent margins can't absorb those shocks. The result is consolidation: private equity platforms are aggressively rolling up fragmented electrical firms, even as deal count dropped to 99 in 2025 from 140 in 2024, because those that survive need scale to negotiate tariff-driven material costs and capture the high-margin data-center and infrastructure work.\n\nHVAC contractors face a different squeeze—a clean bifurcation between a dying residential market and an exploding commercial one. Residential HVAC work, which was 46 percent of the industry's 2022 revenue, is collapsing because mortgage rates stuck near 6 percent killed new home construction; single-family starts fell 5 percent in 2025 to 909,000 units. But commercial HVAC is booming. Data centers, office buildings, and warehouses are on modernization binges driven by energy-efficiency mandates, indoor air quality rules, and AI-powered building automation. The market is $159.4 billion in 2026, growing just 2.6 percent overall, but commercial HVAC is expanding at 8.27 percent annually. On top of that, the EPA phased out R-410A refrigerant on January 1, 2025, and the sell-through deadline hit on January 1, 2026—every new residential unit now requires A2L refrigerants (R-454B, R-32). That forced technicians to retrain, systems to be redesigned, and installation costs to jump 10 percent. Heat pumps have hit an inflection point: they outsold central air conditioners and gas furnaces for the first time in 2025, and over 48 percent of US homes now use electrical heating. This is good for commercial contractors and bad for traditional residential shops. Labor is brutal: the industry has 590,000 employees with acute shortages pushing wages up 8 percent annually. Private equity has noticed—PE add-on acquisitions in HVAC jumped 88 percent year-over-year through mid-2025, with platforms like Apex Service Partners (Apollo-backed, ~$10 billion) rolling up over 100 brands and closing 60 add-ons in 2025 alone.\n\nTurbine and engine makers operate under a different kind of pressure. The US engine and turbine manufacturing industry is $55.0 billion in 2026, growing only 1.6 percent, but the real story is tariffs and regulatory deadlines. Tariffs on imported steel, aluminum, and engine components are running at 25 percent, and that alone cost major producers like Caterpillar an estimated $2.2 to $2.4 billion in 2026. Some firms are reshoring components and locking in long-term US supplier contracts, raising operating leverage but spreading execution risk across the supply chain. The EPA's 2027 heavy-duty engine rule is the hard deadline: NOx limits cut 82 to 90 percent across all new truck, locomotive, and marine engines. Detroit Diesel and Cummins are already shipping Gen-6 and 2027-compliant models, but the technology lift is steep. Truck prices are climbing. Warranty obligations extend 5 to 7 years. The long-haul diesel franchise is secure because electrification is still a fringe play, but the margin pressure is real. GE Vernova is pivoting hard to meet the grid's hunger for new generation capacity: turbine production capacity is moving from 55 to 70-80 heavy-duty turbines annually, with an 80-gigawatt backlog sold out through 2028-2030 and $300 million in capex for South Carolina and New York plants. Cummins launched a 13-liter, 2600 Series multi-fuel engine in 2026 available in diesel, biofuel, and hydrogen at 400 to 700 hp. These firms cannot duck regulation or tariffs. They have to absorb costs, retool fast, and lock in supply chains. The ones with domestic scale and capital to invest are winning. The ones betting on cheap imports are getting crushed.

The evidence

  • Electricians: Data-center construction spending hit $50.7 billion annualized in 2026, up 28% year-over-year, offsetting residential collapse (2026)
  • HVAC contractors: Commercial HVAC growing 8.27% annually while residential collapses; heat pumps now outsell furnaces for first time, marking structural shift (2025–2026)
  • HVAC consolidation: Private equity add-on acquisitions jumped 88% year-over-year through mid-2025; Apex Service Partners closed 60 deals in 2025 alone (2025)
  • Turbine makers: GE Vernova turbine capacity rising from 55 to 70–80 units annually with 80-GW backlog sold out through 2028–2030 and $300M capex investment (2025–2026)
  • Engine compliance: EPA 2027 NOx emissions rule forces all new diesel trucks and locomotives to redesign; Cummins launched multi-fuel X15 (diesel, biofuel, hydrogen) and Detroit Diesel shipping Gen-6 engines (2026)

Who rides it

Skilled electricians, HVAC technicians, and larger mechanical contractors with existing scale are riding a once-in-a-generation wealth creation moment. Journeymen electricians are commanding $76K to $110K+ and moving into data-center and grid infrastructure work at 28% year-over-year growth. HVAC technicians are scarce enough to push wages up 8 percent annually, and commercial shops serving data centers, modernized office buildings, and automated facilities are thriving. Electrical and HVAC contractors large enough to absorb 18-22% material cost increases and invest in retraining are consolidating smaller competitors and locking in high-margin work. Turbine and engine makers with domestic supply chains and capital to invest in 2027 emissions compliance are securing long-term backlog and locking in regulatory advantage. PE platforms are aggressively rolling up fragmented firms to create scale and capture the consolidation premium.

Who's squeezed

Residential electricians and HVAC contractors, small owner-ops with thin 5-6% margins who have no pricing power against tariff-driven material cost increases. Single-family housing starts fell 5% to 909,000 units in 2025, collapsing traditional residential HVAC and electrical work. Tariffs hit copper wiring 18% and electrical panels 22%, and small contractors cannot absorb those shocks or negotiate volume discounts. HVAC shops dependent on residential retrofit and new-build work are being squeezed out as commercial work bifurcates and PE platforms consolidate at scale. Engine and turbine makers without domestic supply chains and capital for rapid 2027 EPA compliance retooling face margin compression, warranty cost overruns, and competitive loss to better-capitalized rivals. Import-dependent manufacturers are getting crushed by 25% tariffs on steel, aluminum, and components.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The real bottleneck is labor, not money. Electricians and HVAC technicians are acutely scarce—Nvidia and Microsoft CEOs have publicly cited electrician shortages as a constraint on AI infrastructure expansion. Training pipelines are not growing fast enough to fill the gap, and wages are being bid up 8-9% annually. If the data-center buildout continues at its current $50.7 billion annualized rate, but electrician supply stays flat or shrinks, projects will slip. Tariff-driven inflation on materials and components is compressing margins for smaller contractors and raising prices across the board, which could trigger demand destruction or project delays if end customers hit budget caps. The 2027 EPA emissions deadline is a hard cliff: firms that miss retooling will be shut out of the truck and engine market entirely. Consolidation is accelerating, which means fewer independent shops and more leverage in the hands of PE platforms—that could squeeze margins downstream and reduce competitive pricing pressure, ultimately making the buildout more expensive.

In one sentence

The electrical trades cash in in a nutshell

Electricians and HVAC techs are the rarest and most expensive labor in the compute buildout, while tariffs and EPA deadlines reward scale over independence.
