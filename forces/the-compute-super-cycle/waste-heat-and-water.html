D · The tensions

Heat, water & the environment

AI's cooling needs are making water scarce and turning waste into assets — while exposing the limits of an aging hydroelectric grid.

The compute super-cycle didn't just create demand for electricity — it created an entirely new constraint: water and the heat that comes with it. Cooling a data center the size of a small town takes as much water as a mid-size city. That water has to come from somewhere. Rivers are drying out. Aquifers are dropping. And the waste heat pouring out of servers is too much to ignore anymore — nobody can just dump millions of tons of thermal energy into the air or water and pretend it's fine.

What changed

The waste-to-energy plant business — the US industry that burns garbage to make electricity — is caught at an inflection point. The sector pulled in $1.4 billion in 2025 and operates 64 plants across 21 states. These plants used to be about incineration and getting rid of waste. Now they're suddenly interesting to data center operators hunting for reliable baseload power to run AI clusters. Data center electricity consumption surged 17% in 2025. AI-focused data centers are tripling their power use. Tech companies signed 40% of all corporate renewable energy power-purchase agreements in 2025. That demand is real. But the WTE industry faces its own squeeze: landfill gas capture is eating its lunch. There are now 598 landfill gas systems in the US producing 64% of renewable natural gas as of 2025. Twenty new landfill gas projects launched in 2025 alone, adding 39.9 billion cubic feet of biogas capacity. An updated EPA methane rule that takes effect in 2027 is expected to push 705 additional landfills into capture projects. Reworld — the company that owns most of the major plants after buying Covanta in 2024 — processes 20.8 million tons of waste annually and is retargeting itself toward materials recovery, not just burning. The economics tell the story: WTE plants are pulling 18-25% EBITDA margins in 2025, down from 25-30% at landfill gas operators. That gap is where the pain is.

Hydroelectric power is in crisis, and it's a crisis that hits data centers directly. The US hydroelectric sector generated 245 billion kWh in 2025, but the EIA projects only 259 billion kWh in 2026 — that's still 1.8% below the 10-year average. The problem is simple: the West is drying out. Lake Powell, the Colorado River's second-largest reservoir, could hit minimum power pool by December 2026, potentially halting generation at the worst time. The Colorado River Basin is 100% in drought as of 2025 and has lost 27.8 million acre-feet of groundwater since 2002. Snow drought — unseasonably warm years causing early snowmelt — has replaced traditional drought as the real threat to generation. This isn't a cyclical downturn. This is structural. The sector is fighting back with capital. Private equity raised $718 million in equity for hydropower in 2025, up 433% from $167 million in 2024. The DOE announced $430 million in incentive payments to 293 projects at 212 facilities in 2026, and Congress appropriated $220 million annual funding for hydropower and marine research — the highest on record. The agency identified nearly 97,000 non-powered dams suitable for retrofitting. But retrofitting takes years. Licensing bottlenecks are still there. And the core problem — drought and climate volatility — can't be willed away with capital alone.

The waste heat coming out of data centers is starting to look like an asset. A single large AI data center can dump the thermal energy equivalent of a small power plant into the atmosphere or local water body. That heat, if captured and used, could heat homes, greenhouses, or industrial processes. Some operators are already experimenting with pipe-to-district-heating schemes, particularly in cold climates. The Reworld rebranding signals that the waste-to-energy industry is pivoting: instead of pure incineration, they're targeting materials recovery, alternative fuels, and now waste heat capture. Enerkem's Ecoplanta project in Spain received approval in January 2025 for waste-to-methanol conversion, signaling an industry-wide shift toward advanced chemical recycling and using waste as feedstock, not just fuel for burning. These shifts matter because they reframe waste from a liability into an input. Data centers are creating new markets for waste heat and biogas as baseload power. The constraint was always the grid's ability to absorb that power and the water needed to move the heat around. Now water scarcity is making the whole calculus more expensive and forcing operators to cluster data centers near the few reliable water sources — rivers with good flow, coastal areas, or regions with cheap recycled water.

The evidence

  • Waste-to-energy: US WTE sector generated $1.4 billion in 2025 with 64 plants across 21 states; EBITDA margins fell to 18-25% as landfill gas capture (598 systems producing 64% of US renewable natural gas in 2025) squeezed the legacy incineration model.
  • Hydroelectric: US hydropower generated 245 billion kWh in 2025 (down 1.8% from 10-year average in 2026 projection of 259 billion kWh); Lake Powell could hit minimum by December 2026; Colorado River Basin 100% in drought after losing 27.8 million acre-feet since 2002.
  • Data center demand: AI-focused data centers tripled power use in 2025; electricity consumption surged 17%; tech signed 40% of all corporate renewable energy PPAs, creating urgency for reliable baseload power.
  • Capital surge: Private equity invested $718 million in hydropower in 2025 (up 433% from 2024); DOE gave $430 million in incentive payments to 293 projects; Congress appropriated $220 million annual funding (record high).
  • Waste reuse: Reworld (Covanta rebrand, April 2024) processes 20.8 million tons annually and pivots toward materials recovery; Enerkem's waste-to-methanol Ecoplanta approved in January 2025; EPA methane rule (2027) will push 705 more landfills into capture.

Who rides it

Waste heat capture and biogas operators are suddenly valuable. Reworld and landfill gas companies are the chokepoint between data centers and reliable, renewable power. Private equity is flooding hydroelectric retrofitting with capital — $718 million flowed in 2025 alone. Operators who own land near both good water sources and data centers, or who can retrofit old dams quickly, have a once-in-a-generation advantage. The pivot from burning waste to capturing heat and biogas rewards companies that can move fast on chemical recycling and district heating schemes.

Who's squeezed

Regions dependent on Colorado River water are getting squeezed. Western municipalities, agriculture, and industry face drought-driven competition from data centers that need massive cooling flows. Aging hydroelectric plants without retrofit capital are stuck with declining output and rising maintenance costs. Traditional landfill operators face a future where new methane rules force them into capture projects they may not want or can afford. Old WTE plants without diversification into materials recovery and heat capture are watching margins compress as biogas and alternative fuels take the baseload power contracts.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Lake Powell could hit minimum power pool by December 2026, potentially halting hydroelectric generation at the exact moment when data centers need it most. The Colorado River Basin is 100% in drought and has lost 27.8 million acre-feet of groundwater since 2002. Climate volatility and snow drought are now structural risks, not cyclical ones. No amount of capital can fix a dry river. Data centers could accelerate westward migration away from water-scarce regions, or force brutal rationing of cooling, either of which cascades into energy prices and compute availability during the AI buildout.

In one sentence

Heat, water & the environment in a nutshell

Data centers are draining rivers and heating aquifers, but turning waste heat into renewable power and forcing utilities to rethink infrastructure for a hotter, thirstier future.
