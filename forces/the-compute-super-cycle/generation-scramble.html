B · The power scramble

The Generation Scramble

Data centers need firm power now—gas utilities are racing to expand, solar is splitting in two, and the grid is at a breaking point.

AI's power hunger flipped the electricity market overnight. Utilities that spent a decade preparing for coal to die and renewables to rise are now frantically building new natural gas plants. The grid's transmission is so clogged that solar farms can't connect for years. And residential solar, which relied on a federal tax credit for two decades, just got it cut off. Everything plugged into electricity is about to get more expensive and harder to find.

What changed

Natural gas just became the infrastructure play of the decade. In March 2026, NextEra Energy announced a historic 10 GW expansion of natural gas capacity in Texas and Pennsylvania—approved by the Trump administration and directly tied to AI data center demand. This wasn't a gradual forecast; it catalyzed a industry-wide pivot. Duke Energy committed $103 billion in capital spending (2026-2030) and is seeking 10 GW of new gas procurement in the Carolinas. Southern Company followed suit. The natural gas market is now $105.2 billion in the US (2026) with margins widening; utilities are treating data center customers as the new industrial anchor tenant. This is a genuine reversal. Natural gas had been declining as a share of electricity generation as renewables rose, but in 2026 it sits at 40% of generation, down from 42% in 2024 only because Henry Hub prices surged 60% in 2025 and are forecast to rise another 30% by 2027. Coal is now cheaper in the margin, so utilities are burning more of it—coal climbed to 17% of generation in 2025 before settling back to 15% in 2026—but coal plants are aging and retiring, so they can't scale. Natural gas is the only fuel that can ramp fast and meet long-term contracts. The paradox: data centers want zero-carbon power, but they're forcing a gas buildout because the grid can't connect renewables fast enough.\n\nSolar shows what happens when you split an industry in two. Utility-scale solar is in a construction frenzy. Developers safe-harbored 216-240 GW of capacity ahead of the July 4, 2026 federal deadline under the Inflation Reduction Act and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Texas alone will host 40% of new 2026 capacity. But here's the trap: the interconnection queue is jammed. The US grid now has 2,600 GW of projects waiting to connect, with a median wait time of five years. Solar farms take 1-5 years to build. Transmission takes 10-15 years. So massive solar capacity is being built and stuck offline, unable to feed the grid. Meanwhile, the PPA (Power Purchase Agreement) price for solar hit $64.49/MWh in Q1 2026, up sharply from the $40s range in prior years because tariffs (polysilicon tariffs doubled in January 2025) and shipping costs spiked. The residential half is collapsing. The 30% federal tax credit expired on December 31, 2025, after 20 years of dominating the financing model. Roughly 100 solar companies have gone bankrupt since 2023; Sunnova filed Chapter 11 in June 2025, and Sunrun entered distress. Financing froze—banks stopped investing in tax-credit instruments. Third-party ownership (TPO) models now capture 65% of residential deals, but even that can't offset the credit loss. Residential solar went from the driver of the industry to a clearance bin.\n\nWind is caught between boom and squeeze. Onshore wind added a remarkable 8.2 GW in 2025, a sharp reversal after a 2024 lull. Data centers are driving this—wind is now the cheapest way to meet long-term power contracts, so developers are racing to build. But tariffs and permitting are shredding margins. Steel tariffs went from 25% in March 2025 to 50% in June 2025, raising turbine component costs by 25-30% for imported parts. EBIT margins compressed from 21.5% in 2022 to 15-17% in 2026. GE Vernova invested $600 million in US factory capacity as a tariff hedge, signaling that manufacturing onshore is now cheaper than importing. The industry split between onshore (81.9% of revenue) and offshore (18%) is widening. Onshore is riding demand, but offshore entered crisis: Ørsted canceled projects, citing supply-chain inflation, rising interest rates, and construction risk. A December 2025 court victory temporarily blocked an executive order freezing federal wind development, but policy volatility remains the operational risk.\n\nHydroelectric power is the wild card nobody planned for. The western drought is relentless. Lake Powell could hit minimum power pool by December 2026, potentially halting generation at the worst moment. The Colorado River Basin is 100% in drought as of 2025, having lost 27.8 million acre-feet of groundwater since 2002. Hydropower generation reached 245 billion kWh in 2025, a recovery from 2024's record low, but the EIA projects only 259 billion kWh in 2026—that's 1.8% below the 10-year average. Snow drought—record warmth causing early snowmelt—has replaced traditional drought as the operational enemy. Yet private capital is flooding in. Hydropower companies raised $718 million in equity in 2025 (up from $167 million in 2024), and the DOE identified nearly 97,000 non-powered dams suitable for retrofitting. Government funding hit a record: $430 million in incentive payments to 293 projects plus $220 million annual congressional appropriation. The bet is on small hydro and distributed generation to replace what western drought is taking away. But that's years out, and the grid needs power now.

The evidence

  • Energy & Environment: Natural gas utilities secured historic 10 GW expansion approvals in 2026 (NextEra Texas/Pennsylvania March 2026; Duke Energy Carolinas; Southern Company) as data-center demand reversed a decade-long decline and pushed prices 60% higher in 2025.
  • Energy & Environment: Utility-scale solar safe-harbored 216-240 GW ahead of July 4, 2026 federal deadline, but interconnection gridlock (2,600 GW queue, 5-year median wait) trapped capacity offline while polysilicon tariffs doubled in January 2025, raising PPA prices to $64.49/MWh.
  • Energy & Environment: Residential solar collapsed after 30% federal tax credit expired December 31, 2025 (after 20 years); ~100 companies bankrupt since 2023; Sunnova Chapter 11 June 2025; TPO financing now captures 65% of deals as traditional models failed.
  • Energy & Environment: Onshore wind added 8.2 GW in 2025 driven by data-center demand, but steel tariffs (25% to 50%, March-June 2025) raised turbine costs 25-30%; GE Vernova invested $600M in US factory capacity; EBIT margins compressed from 21.5% (2022) to 15-17% (2026).
  • Energy & Environment: Western drought (Colorado River Basin 100% drought, 27.8M acre-feet lost since 2002) threatens hydropower; Lake Powell risks minimum power pool by December 2026; but private equity flooded in ($718M raised 2025, up from $167M 2024) betting on 97,000 non-powered dams for retrofitting.

Who rides it

Utilities with natural gas assets and land near data centers win the biggest prize. Natural gas plants are the only infrastructure that can ramp fast enough to meet multi-year power contracts with AI companies. Margin expansion and government backing (Trump administration approved NextEra's 10 GW expansion in March 2026) make this a once-in-a-decade capital story. Onshore wind developers win the second-place prize because data centers are locking in long-term PPAs, making wind cheaper than other options—but only if tariffs stabilize. Private hydroelectric companies are beginning to win on smaller scales as the DOE and Congress fund distributed generation ($430 million in 2026 alone) and retrofitted dams offer lower risk than massive greenfield plants.

Who's squeezed

Residential solar companies lost everything when the tax credit expired December 31, 2025. Over a hundred went bankrupt; Sunnova, Sunrun, and mid-market installers are in distress. Homeowners who needed the 30% credit to afford solar are priced out. Transmission developers and grid planners are squeezed because they have to connect 2,600 GW of projects with five-year wait times while solar and wind farms sit offline. Coal-dependent utilities are caught: coal's cheaper in the margin right now, but coal plants are aging, retirement orders are sticky, and they can't build new ones. The western US loses out if Lake Powell hits minimum power pool by December 2026—cities, agriculture, and existing hydro generation all face curtailment. Offshore wind is nearly dead, with Ørsted canceling projects and no recovery in sight.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is a cascade failure in the western grid. Lake Powell could hit minimum power pool by December 2026. If that happens, hydropower generation halts at the exact moment data centers are demanding maximum power. The grid's interconnection queue (2,600 GW, five-year median wait) means utilities can't respond fast. They'll turn to whatever power is dispatchable—natural gas. That drives gas prices higher, squeezes margins across electricity, and forces rolling demand destruction across all of customer. Meanwhile, solar capacity is being built but can't connect, so it's stranded offline. Transmission infrastructure takes 10-15 years to build, but solar farms and wind turbines take 1-5 years. This mismatch will persist through the 2030s, making blackout risk the shadow cost of the data-center boom.

In one sentence

The generation scramble in a nutshell

Data-center power demand forced utilities to reverse course and expand natural gas while solar split into a booming utility-scale half (stuck in grid gridlock) and a collapsing residential half (no more tax credit), leaving transmission as the scarcest resource on the grid.
