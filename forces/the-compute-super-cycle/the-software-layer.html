The software that runs on top

Where enterprise software vendors are fighting for survival as AI reshapes what their products are worth.

The data centers eating terawatts need software to run them. That software layer is in crisis. Not because it's broken—because AI is making it obsolete faster than vendors can pivot. Every SaaS company is now scrambling to prove AI makes them essential instead of replaceable.

What changed

AI has turned enterprise software into a cannibal game. Customer relationship management, business intelligence, workflow automation, customer support—entire product categories are getting gutted by AI agents that do the work these tools were designed for. In February 2026, $2 trillion in software sector market cap evaporated in what traders called the "SaaSpocalypse" as fear of disruption peaked. But that wasn't the bottom. By March 2026, Deutsche Bank declared "peak fear," signaling the bottom was in. The market bifurcated. Salesforce, recognizing the threat, went on an acquisition spree: Informatica in November 2025 for data integration and AI pipelines, Qualified.com in April 2026 for AI-driven sales, and Fin in June 2026—a pure AI customer support and sales agent. Total M&A activity for the year hit $440 billion, with 72% of SaaS acquisition targets in 2025 pitching AI as their sole value proposition. Acquiring companies no longer care about feature breadth; they're buying AI-first capabilities or not buying at all.

The database and storage layer shows the same pattern on steroids. Databricks reached $5.4 billion in ARR with 65% growth in February 2026, while Snowflake, the most-used data warehouse vendor, grew at 29% year-over-year to $4.68 billion in fiscal 2026 revenue. But here's the squeeze: Databricks is worth 2x Snowflake's valuation despite similar revenue. The market is paying for AI-native data platforms, not traditional warehouses. Every major cloud provider is embedding vector search and AI optimization directly into their databases. AWS upgraded Aurora Serverless on April 20, 2026, promising 30% faster scaling for AI workloads with vector search as standard. Microsoft added vector indexes to Azure SQL Hyperscale on March 18, 2026. Google AlloyDB gained auto-embedding functions on January 2, 2026. This is a systematic dismantling of the licensing model. Traditional databases—Oracle, SQL Server—face sustained pricing pressure as customers standardize on managed cloud services. Perpetual enterprise licensing is dead; consumption-based subscriptions with outcome-based pricing are taking over. By 2025, 61% of companies had already shifted to hybrid pricing with base fees plus usage metering. Gartner predicts 40% of enterprise SaaS will be outcome-based by 2026.

Complexity is the third killer. The average enterprise now manages 354 APIs, up from 200 two years earlier. Every time a company adds a new system—and AI is forcing them to add new systems constantly—the integration nightmare grows. SMB and mid-market vendors are getting trapped between cloud giants who can afford to be free or $1/month and specialized players who are moving upmarket into AI analytics, real-time processing, and agentic automation. The small vendor is dead. Regulatory complexity compounds the squeeze. GDPR fines exceeded $6.2 billion through August 2025. DORA enforcement went live in 2026 with mandatory database-layer change management requirements. These aren't one-time costs; they're structural. Every database vendor now has to build compliance infrastructure into the product, raising the engineering bar and making customer acquisition more expensive.

The enterprise software market crossed $317.5 billion in 2026 with a 13.8% year-over-year growth rate, and it's supposed to add 38,732 new workers to reach 179,869 employees by 2026 from 141,137 in 2021. But those growth numbers mask the carnage underneath: margin compression, consolidation, pricing model whiplash, and the existential dread that your product—no matter how much your engineers love it—could be replaced by an AI agent trained on a few hundred examples. Vendors are no longer selling software. They're selling data pipelines, AI model training infrastructure, and the promise that they'll survive long enough to stay compliant.

The evidence

  • Enterprise Software: Salesforce acquired Informatica (November 2025), Qualified.com (April 2026), and Fin (June 2026)—$440 billion in total M&A activity in 2025 with 72% of targets pitching AI as primary value
  • Database/Cloud: Databricks reached $5.4B ARR with 65% growth in February 2026; Snowflake grew 29% YoY to $4.68B FY2026 revenue but faces 2x valuation discount
  • Cloud Integration: AWS Aurora Serverless gained 30% faster scaling (April 2026), Microsoft added vector indexes (March 2026), Google embedded auto-embeddings (January 2026)
  • SaaS Pricing Shift: 61% of companies adopted hybrid pricing by 2025; Gartner projects 40% of enterprise SaaS will use outcome-based pricing by 2026
  • Compliance Burden: GDPR fines totaled $6.2B through August 2025; DORA enforcement live in 2026 mandating database-layer change management

Who rides it

Specialized, AI-native vendors in databases (Databricks, Snowflake), data integration (post-Informatica Salesforce), and cloud infrastructure (AWS, Microsoft, Google) capture the value. They own the layer that trains AI models and the infrastructure AI needs to run. Legacy perpetual licensing vendors get squeezed; acquisition targets get absorbed into larger platforms. Cloud giants win because they can bundle AI into databases for free, commoditizing competitors' core products. Vendors selling AI-first solutions—agent platforms, real-time analytics, agentic automation—command valuation premiums because they're perceived as solving the next problem, not the last one.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market and SMB software vendors are trapped. Too small to compete with cloud giants; too general-purpose to avoid cannibalization by AI agents. Traditional database licensing vendors (Oracle, SQL Server) face sustained pricing pressure as customers shift to managed cloud services. Customer support software, CRM features, workflow automation tools—any software that automates routine knowledge work—is now a liability on a company's product portfolio because customers know AI can do it better. Employees at traditional software companies watch market cap evaporate and acquisition multiples collapse while they're told to "add AI" to products that are fundamentally being made redundant.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The cascade: If AI can automate the software engineer's job tomorrow—writing code, debugging, shipping features—then every software company is a potential target for the same force squeezing their customers. A software engineer's productivity spike from AI tooling (Copilot, Claude, etc.) compresses the time to feature parity, which collapses the moat that once justified licensing fees. The worry is that the entire industry hits deflation in 2027 or 2028 as companies realize their software stack is 80% commodity + 20% differentiation, and 70% of that differentiation can now be built by a team of five instead of fifty. That's not disruption; that's structural contraction.

In one sentence

The software that runs on top in a nutshell

AI is cannibalizing enterprise software's value while forcing vendors to chase cloud giants into infrastructure and agents, leaving anyone selling traditional tools or mid-market SMB solutions in existential peril.
