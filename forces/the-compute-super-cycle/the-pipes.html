The Pipes to the Internet

AI's compute sprawl is forcing a reshuffling of who owns the last mile and who hosts the workloads.

The internet's plumbing was designed for browsing and video streaming. Now it's designed for machine learning. That simple shift—from millions of people pulling content to millions of GPUs pushing and pulling petabytes—is remaking the ISP and hosting industries faster than anyone thought possible. Fiber is suddenly critical. So is compute hosting. And everyone is scrambling to supply both.

What changed

Internet Service Providers have gone from defending their legacy video bundles to racing for fiber dominance. The US ISP market sits at $179.9 billion in 2026, growing 4.5% year-over-year, but the growth masks a brutal reshuffling underneath. Fiber-to-the-home availability jumped from 37% of US addresses in 2024 to 57% by early 2026—nearly a 20-point sprint in two years. That sprint is federally funded (the BEAD program, now in earnest construction across 46 states with NIST approval in 52 states with grant funds available) and corporate-driven (AT&T targets 60 million locations by 2030; Charter is investing $11.4 billion in capex for 2026 alone). The economics are clear: whoever lays fiber first captures the data center customer. But consolidation is the real story. The number of ISP establishments actually shrunk as the market expanded. Verizon closed a $20 billion Frontier acquisition in March 2026, leapfrogging to second-largest broadband provider. Charter is acquiring Cox Communications by mid-2026. DigitalBridge and Crestview scooped up WideOpenWest for $1.5 billion in 2025. North American ISP M&A hit $81 billion in 2024, doubling the prior year. Smaller ISPs are exiting or being absorbed. The margin squeeze is relentless. ISP profit margins sit around 33.82% (broader information services sector, 2025), but rising capex for fiber buildout and labor costs are eating into that. Meanwhile, legacy revenue streams are collapsing: Comcast lost 322,000 TV subscribers and 65,000 internet subscribers in Q1 2026 alone, forcing ISPs to chase data center customers just to offset the bleeding.\n\nInternet Hosting Services face a different but equally intense squeeze. The market was $98.1 billion in 2025, growing 4.9% year-over-year, but that's a sharp slowdown from the pandemic boom—2020-2022 saw 11.9% growth; now the industry is maturing and fragmenting. Cloud giants (AWS at 31% market share, Azure at 23-25%, Google Cloud at 11-12% in Q1 2026) have encroached on traditional hosting, offering managed infrastructure that undercuts pure hosting providers on price and flexibility. Within the pure hosting sector, consolidation and specialization accelerate. Newfold Digital consolidated Web.com into Network Solutions in June 2025, sunsetting Web.com as a standalone brand. The result: 13,817 hosting establishments as of 2022 are now fighting harder for niches. Price competition is brutal. Labor costs are rising 7% annually for hosting technicians. And compliance is becoming a permanent operational cost—GDPR, CPRA, LGPD are now table-stakes in every contract. The survival strategy is AI automation. In 2025-2026, 65% of hosting providers prioritized AI-powered malware detection, and 53% cited AI-driven automation as critical to 2026 competitiveness. AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud all published carbon-aware routing and per-region carbon intensity APIs in Q1 2026, embedding sustainability into hosting decisions. Google cut compute pricing 8% across all regions to fight for share.\n\nThe convergence is clear: fiber ISPs and hosting providers are now competing in the same game—capturing data center customers. ISP margins are solid but capex-intensive. Hosting margins are thin (around 5% of revenue in 2022, pressured downward since) but automation-intensive. The winners are the vertically integrated giants—Amazon, Google, Microsoft—who own or control both infrastructure and the pipes. The squeezed are the mid-market ISPs that lag on fiber deployment, and the thousands of small hosting providers that can't afford the AI tooling and compliance burden.

The evidence

  • ISP fiber expansion: AT&T targets 60 million locations by 2030; fiber availability surged to 57% of US addresses by early 2026 (from 37% in 2024), driven by BEAD construction across 46 states
  • ISP consolidation: Verizon acquired Frontier for $20 billion (March 2026); Charter acquired Cox Communications by mid-2026; M&A hit $81 billion in North America in 2024
  • Hosting market slowdown: Internet Hosting Services grew 4.9% in 2025 after 11.9% CAGR during 2020-2022 pandemic boom; now facing cloud encroachment and margin pressure
  • ISP customer losses: Comcast lost 322,000 TV subscribers and 65,000 internet subscribers in Q1 2026, forcing ISPs to chase data center workloads
  • AI becomes table-stakes: 65% of hosting providers prioritized AI-powered security (2025-2026); cloud giants (AWS, Azure, Google) all published carbon-aware APIs in Q1 2026 and cut pricing (Google down 8%)

Who rides it

Winners own both pipes and computing. Amazon Web Services ($37.59 billion revenue, up 28% year-over-year in Q1 2026), Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud control the market consolidation—they own or partner for fiber, sell hosting, and integrate AI tools (Azure with OpenAI GPT-5, AWS with proprietary ML services). The largest ISPs—AT&T, Verizon, Charter—that own both access and can strike data center partnerships also benefit. Smaller ISPs with fast fiber deployment and niche hosting providers that specialize in AI-powered compliance and security carve out defensible positions. BEAD beneficiaries—rural broadband expansions funded by the program—create new fiber assets for whoever builds them at scale.

Who's squeezed

Traditional mid-market ISPs and independent hosting providers are squeezed. ISPs that lag on fiber deployment (cable-only or older copper networks) lose to fiber-first competitors and BEAD-funded fiber cooperatives. Cable TV revenues collapse (Comcast's 322,000 TV sub losses prove the point), forcing ISPs to fight for data center share against bigger, better-capitalized competitors. Independent hosting providers face a margin trap: rising labor costs (technician wages up 7% annually), price competition from cloud giants cutting by 8%, and compliance burden (GDPR, CPRA, LGPD) that requires permanent engineering headcount. Smaller hosters can't afford the AI tooling that larger platforms offer.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is stranded infrastructure and regulatory capture. Fiber buildout is racing (46-52 states approved for BEAD), but if demand for data center connectivity outpaces ISP ability to build, prices will spike—attracting new competitors but also inviting antitrust scrutiny. Net neutrality rollback (2025) gives ISPs latitude for paid prioritization; AT&T already inked fast-lane deals with Disney+ and Xbox Cloud by Q2 2025. If large ISPs use paid prioritization to favor their own cloud hosting or content partners, smaller hosters and ISPs get locked out, and consolidation accelerates. The regulatory tug-of-war (five states—CA, WA, OR, CO, VT—maintained local net neutrality protections) could create fragmentation that raises costs for everyone.

In one sentence

The pipes to the internet in a nutshell

Data centers need fiber, bandwidth, and hosting stacks—and the companies that supply them are seeing unprecedented demand, consolidation, and margin squeeze all at once.
