The data-center land rush

AI compute demand turns land, power, and cooling into the scarce inputs of the decade.

For a hundred years, data meant a physical place: a building with machines inside. Now the building doesn't matter. The only thing that matters is whether the grid has enough power to run it. AI training workloads demand 30 to 100+ kilowatts per rack. That flipped the constraint from land to electricity—and from a problem utilities manage slowly to a problem companies must solve fast.

What changed

Until 2020, data-center companies competed on square footage. You leased space, filled it with servers, paid the power bill. The colocation industry was worth $13.2 billion in 2021. Today it is $72.37 billion (2026)—a fivefold jump in five years. But that growth is almost entirely about electricity, not space. North American colocation occupancy hit 1% vacancy by 2026. There is nowhere left to build. Operators have stopped trying to acquire land and started fighting for grid connections instead.

The numbers show the severity. US data center electricity demand jumped from 23 gigawatts in 2023 to 42 gigawatts in 2026. Wholesale electricity prices near major data centers climbed 267% in that window. Capacity prices in PJM—the grid operator serving the East Coast and industrial Midwest—jumped 833% in one year alone. These are not incremental changes. This is scarcity.

The real constraint is the queue. American grids are jammed. The interconnection queue—the waiting list for new power connections—exceeds 1,400 gigawatts of stuck requests. A company ordering a new high-capacity connection to the grid now waits four to five years. In saturated markets like Northern Virginia, Dallas, and Phoenix, there is no spare capacity at all. Seven major AI companies (Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Oracle, xAI) signed the Ratepayer Protection Pledge with the White House in March 2026 to directly fund grid infrastructure improvements, signaling a historic flip: they no longer wait for utilities to solve power problems. They solve them themselves. The data processing and hosting industry is spending $630 billion on infrastructure in 2026 (up 36% from 2025), with $450 billion explicitly targeted at AI—chips, GPUs, accelerators, and the power systems to run them. Consolidation is accelerating fast. CoreWeave acquired Core Scientific. Equinix closed a $15 billion joint venture with GIC to build hyperscale facilities. Digital Realty acquired Slough (July 2024) and is expanding into new geographies. The colocation market grew 11.5% annually from 2025 to 2026, and will grow another 11.5% through 2030, but only for operators who control power.

Regulation is tightening around the shortage. Oregon signed the POWER Act in August 2025, mandating ten-year power-purchase agreements for facilities over 20 megawatts. California and Virginia implemented large-load tariffs with 25-40 megawatt thresholds. FERC issued data-center-specific transmission tariffs for PJM in December 2025, effective January 2026. These rules acknowledge the new reality: data centers are now essential grid assets. They are no longer just tenants. They are infrastructure.

The evidence

  • Colocation market grew from $13.2B (2021) to $72.37B (2026), with 11.5% CAGR through 2030, driven entirely by AI electricity demand and power scarcity rather than physical space
  • US data center electricity demand surged 82% from 23 GW (2023) to 42 GW (2026), making power the binding constraint for all new deployments
  • Grid interconnection queue exceeds 1,400 GW of stuck requests with four-to-five-year lead times, particularly in saturated markets (Northern Virginia, Dallas, Phoenix)
  • Seven major AI companies signed the White House Ratepayer Protection Pledge (March 2026) to directly fund grid upgrades, signaling corporate takeover of infrastructure buildout from utilities
  • Oregon POWER Act (August 2025), California and Virginia large-load tariffs, and FERC data-center-specific transmission rules (December 2025) formalize data centers as grid-critical infrastructure with long-term power commitments

Who rides it

Power companies, transmission operators, and large landowners in high-capacity grid zones (Texas, Oregon, Northern Virginia) win decisively. Electricity becomes the new collateral. A parcel near a substation with spare grid capacity is now worth as much as a pharmaceutical patent. Hyperscalers (Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta) and specialized AI hosting platforms (CoreWeave) win because they can absorb $630 billion in capital spending and negotiate directly with utilities and governments. Smaller operators can still win if they specialize in edge compute, liquid cooling, or power-adjacent services—filling the gaps hyperscalers leave behind. Utility companies and grid operators win because they suddenly have leverage. They are no longer afterthoughts. They are dealmakers.

Who's squeezed

Regional carriers and small-to-mid-sized colocation companies get squeezed. If you don't own a power connection or land near one, you are out. Pricing power for colocation jumped 17-19% in the last two years after 15 years of decline. Occupancy is 1% vacancy nationwide. You cannot grow without electricity access, and electricity access is locked up or years away. Real estate developers who built data centers as speculative plays (betting on rising square-footage demand) lose. The constraint shifted from land to power; vacant facilities in low-capacity grid areas are stranded assets. Workers in areas without data-center buildout lose as well—talent and high-wage jobs concentrate in Texas, Northern Virginia, and other power-rich zones. The rural areas that thought they would get a data center boom get nothing.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The grid itself is not built for this. Interconnection queues exceed 1,400 gigawatts. That is more than the entire US generates on an average day. Utilities move slowly; AI companies move fast. The gap between their timelines is widening. Regulators are tightening rules (Oregon PPAs, FERC tariffs, state large-load regulations) to formalize data centers as infrastructure partners, but those rules can also slow projects or force them into unfavorable terms. The single biggest risk is grid failure—not a blackout, but rolling undersupply where demand overwhelms available generation and transmission in pockets of the country. If a hyperscaler cannot get new grid capacity for two more years, but its training workload is scheduled to double in eighteen months, it either fails to deliver or it pulls power from the rest of the grid, raising costs for everyone else. That is already happening in PJM, where data-center loads are forcing reliability discussions and new tariff rules. One bad grid event—a summer heat spike that triggers cascading line failures while data centers run at peak—could force rationing or brown-outs in major metro areas tied to the same grid. That would turn data-center siting from a competitive advantage into a regulatory and political liability.

In one sentence

The data-center land rush in a nutshell

The machines need electricity more than real estate, and whoever controls that grid connection owns the next decade of infrastructure.
