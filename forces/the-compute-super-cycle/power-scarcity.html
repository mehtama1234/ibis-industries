When Power Gets Scarce

AI data centers are hoarding the grid—and everyone else is learning to wait for electricity.

The grid just became a battlefield. AI data centers need 75.8 gigawatts of power by 2026, and they're grabbing it from the utilities that used to serve factories and neighborhoods. When one player can eat a tenth of a region's entire electricity supply, the rules change. Whoever can't outbid—homes, small manufacturers, regional ISPs—gets pushed to the back of the queue or pays more.

What changed

Power went from a regulated utility that everyone could count on to a scarce resource that favors scale. In 2025, wholesale power costs in PJM spiked 49%, and capacity costs jumped 262%—both driven by data center demand crashing into transmission limits. This isn't supply growing slower than demand. This is supply hitting a hard ceiling. The interconnection queue—where projects wait for permission to plug into the grid—became the chokepoint. FERC had to step in with an emergency directive in December 2025, and the US Secretary of Energy issued a new rulemaking just to stop project delays from tanking the whole system. Even then, the timeline for final rules isn't until April 2026. Meanwhile, utilities are doing something unthinkable: they're pivoting back to coal and natural gas after decades of moving toward renewables. Henry Hub natural gas prices surged 60% in 2025 and are forecast to rise another 30% by 2027. Coal climbed back to 17% of the generation mix in 2025 as utilities scrambled to add capacity. NextEra Energy announced a historic 10 GW natural gas expansion in March 2026, Duke Energy committed $103 billion in capex through 2030, and Southern Company followed suit—all to feed the data center buildout. This is "The Great Re-Gassing," and it's backwards. Renewables were supposed to dominate. Utility-scale solar grew 21.3% in 2025 and renewables hit 26% of generation in 2025. But the grid can't route it fast enough. Transmission—the wires that move power—is the real bottleneck. Utilities are raising rates aggressively. Profit margins climbed to 15% of revenue in 2025, double the historical 7-8% norm. Consolidation exploded: NextEra and Dominion announced a $66.8 billion merger in May 2026, the largest utility deal in decades, as firms race to own generation and transmission before the window closes. Smaller players got crushed. Pine Gate Renewables, a North Carolina-based independent power producer, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in November 2025, citing financing and policy pressures.

Who rides it

Tech giants and vertically integrated utilities with land, assets, and capital win. They're buying generation, transmission, and data centers in bundles. NextEra and Dominion's $66.8 billion merger puts one firm in control of generation, transmission, and customer service across the Southeast—exactly the leverage needed to land data center contracts. Duke Energy's $103 billion capex commitment through 2030 serves the same playbook: own the power plant, own the transmission, own the customer. Regional utilities and independent power producers get swallowed or go bankrupt. Whoever has dual assets—generation and transmission—can promise the grid stability that data centers demand. Smaller independents can't.

Who's squeezed

Homes and small businesses lose first. They're on the grid. They can't negotiate wholesale rates or demand transmission priority. When utilities raise rates 15% in profit margins, retail customers feel it. Small manufacturers and regional ISPs lose second. They need reliable power to run 24/7, but data centers get priority in the interconnection queue and better rates through long-term contracts. Independent power producers like Pine Gate get squeezed out entirely—they can't compete for grid access or financing when utilities are locking in generation and transmission assets. States and counties lose planning control. FERC's emergency rulemaking in October 2025 to fast-track data center interconnection overrides local transmission planning. Renewables lose momentum. Utilities are re-gassing with natural gas and coal to keep the lights on for data centers, not building transmission fast enough to absorb wind and solar cheaply. Coal plant retirements hit a 15-year low—only 2.6 GW retired in 2025 versus 8.5 GW planned—because utilities are now keeping them running as backup capacity.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that the grid cannot be built fast enough. Utilities need $1.4 trillion in transmission investment from 2025 to 2030. FERC can issue directives, but construction, permitting, and land rights take years. Meanwhile, data center capacity is being installed in months. If the transmission queue stays toxic—if projects still face approval delays and grid constraints—some data centers will never turn on, or they'll move to regions where power is cheaper (overseas, mostly). That kills the whole buildout story. The other risk: utilities are locking in natural gas and coal contracts that last decades, betting on their coal fleets running till 2045 or later. If renewables actually do scale up faster than expected—battery costs drop 40% in five years, solar installs surge—those utilities are stranded with expensive, aging fossil fuel plants that can't compete. They'll try to shift costs to ratepayers. Regulations will follow. The third risk is regional instability. A few utilities controlling generation, transmission, and data center contracts in Texas or the Carolinas creates chokepoint pricing. If NextEra or Duke Energy faces a cyberattack, a physical outage, or regulatory pressure, the whole region's data center economy goes dark. No redundancy. Centralizing power infrastructure to serve centralized AI compute is the opposite of resilient.

In one sentence

When power gets scarce in a nutshell

Data centers are eating the grid, and anyone who can't pay premium rates or build their own power plant is getting left behind.
