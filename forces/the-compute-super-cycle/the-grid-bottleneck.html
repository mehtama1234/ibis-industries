B · The power scramble

The grid bottleneck

Transmission and gas delivery, not generation, are the real constraint—and whoever controls the pipes to data centers wins.

The US power grid is hitting a hard wall. Data centers alone will consume 75.8 gigawatts by 2026, and renewables keep adding unpredictability on top of that. The problem is not that we can't generate electricity anymore—it's that we can't move it fast enough. Transmission got stuck in a permitting nightmare while everyone built generators.

What changed

The grid used to be a simple model: utilities built wires, people paid rates, the margin stayed steady at 7–8% forever. That's over. Data center demand outran transmission capacity in 2025, forcing utilities to book record profit margins—up to 15% of revenue in some regions like Florida Power & Light (27.4% in 2025-2026) and Mid-American Energy (27.6%)—double the historical norm. The real bottleneck is not generation or money; it's the interconnection queue. FERC's transmission system has become so toxic that tech giants and power companies stopped trusting it. Instead, hyperscalers announced 101 gigawatts of behind-the-meter natural gas generation to bypass the grid entirely—a structural revolt against waiting years for permitting. In PJM, the wholesale power market, capacity costs spiked 262% in 2025, and spot power prices jumped 49%, because data center demand ran headfirst into a transmission grid designed for slower growth. The math is brutal: data centers will gobble 90 gigawatts of peak load growth by 2026, but existing wires can't deliver it fast enough.\n\nNatural gas is now pinched from two sides. Industrial consumption hit a record 23.6 billion cubic feet per day in 2025, driven by both manufacturing and those same data centers running backup generators 24/7. Retail gas prices rose 60% in 2025 versus 2024, but regulated utilities can't pass that on fast enough—their margins got squeezed even as input costs spiked. Henry Hub, the benchmark, is forecast at $3.67 per million BTU for 2026, elevated and volatile. Meanwhile, LNG exports are booming: US capacity will grow from 11.4 Bcf/d in early 2024 to 28.7 Bcf/d by 2029. That export push locked in high prices and prioritized foreign buyers over domestic grid stability. Behind all this, pipeline operators added 12.8 Bcf/d of new gas transmission in 2024–2025 and have another 44.9 Bcf/d queued for 2026–2027, mostly from the Permian. The paradox: pipelines get built on cost-plus FERC regulation, so revenue is tied to what regulators allow, not price movements. Kinder Morgan, the largest, posted record 2025 earnings of $16.9 billion, up 12%, because the system rewards scale and throughput, not efficiency.\n\nTransmission and distribution, the actual wires that move power to your door, are now the commanding position. The market grew to $536.2 billion in 2025, up from $476.1 billion in 2022, and utilities are racing to raise capital. The grid investment plan is $1.4 trillion for 2025–2030, but permitting delays mean each year lost is money lost and data center projects get cancelled or shifted to willing jurisdictions. FERC Order No. 1920 in late 2025 tried to fix this with new transmission planning reforms, and a new ruling on large load interconnection is due April 30, 2026, but compliance will take years. The bigger shift: consolidation. NextEra Energy and Dominion Energy announced a $66.8 billion merger, the largest utility deal in 2025, to secure generation and transmission assets ahead of the grid crunch. Utilities now have pricing power they never had before. Margins climbed because demand is inelastic—data centers can't just move elsewhere, and people still need power—while supply is constrained. The transmission grid became the narrow gate everyone must pass through, and whoever owns it can charge what the market will bear.\n\nBattery storage, the wild card, exploded from 15 gigawatts added in 2025 to 24 gigawatts planned for 2026. This isn't solving the grid problem; it's reshaping how transmission operators manage it. Batteries let them absorb renewable intermittency without building new wires, but only if they have enough capacity nearby. This incentivizes data centers to co-locate batteries and gas generators with servers, further fragmenting the grid into hyperscaler-owned microgrids. The natural outcome: the utilities that survive are the ones that upgrade their transmission fast enough to handle local demand spikes, lock in long-term data center contracts, and control the gas pipelines that feed backup generators. Everyone else—mid-size distributors, independent power producers like Pine Gate Renewables (which filed bankruptcy in November 2025)—gets squeezed out of the value chain.

The evidence

  • Electric Power Transmission: $536.2 billion market (2025), up from $476.1 billion (2022), with 1.4 trillion dollars total transmission investment planned for 2025-2030.
  • Data Centers & PJM Wholesale Power: Capacity costs spiked 262% in 2025; spot power prices jumped 49%; data centers consuming 75.8 GW by 2026 forces 90 GW peak load growth.
  • Natural Gas Distribution: Market hit $222.5 billion (2025); industrial consumption record 23.6 Bcf/d; prices rose 60% in 2025 versus 2024, squeezing utility margins.
  • Gas Pipeline Transportation: $44.2 billion market (2026); US LNG capacity grows from 11.4 Bcf/d (early 2024) to 28.7 Bcf/d by 2029; new pipeline capacity added 12.8 Bcf/d in 2024–2025 with 44.9 Bcf/d more planned for 2026–2027.
  • Utility Consolidation: $66.8 billion NextEra-Dominion merger (2025) and Kinder Morgan record $16.9B earnings (up 12% in 2025) reflect scale-seeking as utilities secure generation and transmission assets ahead of grid constraints.

Who rides it

Transmission operators and vertically integrated utilities like NextEra, Dominion, and Kinder Morgan win big. They control the pipes everyone else depends on, and demand is inelastic—data centers have to accept whatever rates regulators allow. Profit margins doubled overnight because the constraint moved from generation to delivery, and whoever owns the wires to the data center owns the golden goose. Gas pipeline operators especially win: they move gas on cost-plus FERC regulation, so throughput growth (from LNG and data center generators) directly flows to revenue. Major utilities are consolidating to lock in scale and generation assets, and regulators, having seen permitting failures in the interconnection queue, are now approving transmission projects faster.

Who's squeezed

Mid-size utilities and independent power producers get squeezed. If you're a regional distributor without transmission assets or a long-term data center contract, you're selling electricity in a capped market while your input costs spike and you can't pass the full bill on fast enough. Pine Gate Renewables filed for bankruptcy in November 2025 because independent power producers can't compete with hyperscalers' 101 GW of behind-the-meter generation announcements and regulated utilities' price-setting power. Residential and commercial customers lose too—utilities are raising rates aggressively to cover transmission buildout, and those rate hikes flow through to everyone on the grid, not just data centers.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that the grid fractures. Hyperscalers building 101 GW of behind-the-meter generation to avoid interconnection queues means fewer customers for the grid and less revenue per wire. Utilities need that load to justify $1.4 trillion in transmission spending, but if data centers exit the public grid entirely, the utility business model breaks—regulated retail rates can't cover the sunk cost of wires nobody uses anymore. Meanwhile, FERC's April 30, 2026 deadline for large load interconnection rules might come too late: by then, every tech giant with a data center will have already built their own gas generator to avoid the wait. The grid becomes a managed decline for midsize cities and rural areas, while data centers cluster in hyperscaler-friendly zones with dedicated transmission and cheap gas. That's not a grid anymore; it's two parallel networks, one working and one left behind.

In one sentence

The grid bottleneck in a nutshell

Transmission and gas pipelines became the constraint, not generation—hyperscalers are bypassing the grid with 101 GW of behind-the-meter generators, utilities doubled margins, and whoever controls the wires to the data center wins the buildout.
