C · The physical trades of the buildout

Concrete, Steel & the Shell

AI data centers are eating a generation's worth of cement, steel, and construction labor in five years—whoever can pour concrete fast enough and source carbon steel at scale wins.

Data centers are not just consuming electricity. They are physical machines made of concrete, steel beams, rebar, and cooling systems—and the sheer velocity of build is making these materials suddenly scarce and expensive. The compute super-cycle is colliding head-on with a construction industry that was already limping: not enough workers, tariff-driven cost spikes, and financing rates that kill traditional projects. This is a story about who gets squeezed out and who gets rich from the buildout.

What changed

In 2024, data center construction starts were running at about $400 million per month. By December 2025, they hit $6.5 billion—a 190% surge year-over-year to $77.7 billion annually. The Stargate Project alone ($500 billion over four years, announced January 2025) locked in a decade-plus pipeline. Amazon's $23 billion Ohio expansion and Microsoft's Wisconsin mega-facility are both due live in 2026. This is not gradual. This is a compression of a decade's normal build into five years.

The construction industry's capacity did not double. The US cement industry operates at 102-105 million metric tons annually—that ceiling has been flat for five years. Consumption sits at 105-108 million tons. The gap is covered by 25-26 million tons of imports, roughly 21% of apparent consumption in 2025. When a 25% tariff on cement took effect in April 2025, Canadian and Mexican imports became uncompetitive overnight. Domestic production cannot expand—there are only five major firms (LafargeHolcim, Martin Marietta, CEMEX, HeidelbergCement, Buzzi Unicem), and new kiln capacity takes three to five years to build. The data center buildout is now competing for the same concrete tonnage that keeps roads, bridges, and warehouses going. The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA) deployed only $500 billion of its $1.2 trillion commitment by September 2025—but that created a multi-year pipeline of 207,000+ miles of roadway and 11,400+ bridge projects underway. Both demand cement. At the same time.

The labor shortage is worse than the material shortage. Commercial building construction employed 249,166 workers in 2022. It needs 277,367 by 2027. That's a 500,000-worker gap across the whole industry—the Bureau of Labor Statistics estimates it costs the sector $10.8 billion annually. Crane operators, concrete finishers, ironworkers, and heavy-equipment operators are not sitting idle. They are pricing their time aggressively. Wage inflation in construction outpaced general inflation by 3-4 percentage points in 2025. The firms that can move fastest—the mega-contractors consolidating through M&A (562 deals in 2025, up 18.2% from 2024)—are snapping up crews and entire firms. Mid-market contractors are losing bids because they cannot staff the crew. Data center builders have deep pockets and urgency. They win the bidding war. Office construction, retail renovation, and mid-market warehouse builds all get pushed down the queue or cancelled.

By 2026, the commercial building construction market will be split in two. Data centers are the only engine: the $77.7 billion in 2025 starts seeded by Stargate, Amazon, and Microsoft will sustain $315.5 billion in total commercial construction, but concentrated in power, cooling, and land prep. Traditional office construction is at a 10-year low—office demand fell 41% since 2019, new deliveries dropped 26% in 2025, and vacancies are concentrated in older Class-B and Class-C buildings. Retail and hospitality are soft but stable. The tariff on cement (now 25% for most sources, 46% for Vietnam) has shrunk import competition and raised prices. Portland-limestone cement (PLC, Type IL) dominates the market—all 50 states approved it by April 2024, and 97% of blended cement shipments are now PLC. This shift happened because PLC cuts CO2 by 10% and fits into federal infrastructure mandates, but it also requires capital investment and retooling. The firms investing now (like CRH, which acquired Eco Material Technologies in July 2025, and Martin Marietta, which swapped assets with QuikCrete in Q1 2026) are positioning for long-term margin. The firms that do not invest are losing share to tariff-trapped competitors.

The evidence

  • Commercial building construction in the US surged to $315.5 billion in 2026, but only 1.1% growth masks a brutal two-tier split: data center construction at $77.7 billion (190% growth in 2025 starts) versus office construction at a 10-year low, with hybrid work reducing office demand by 41% since 2019 (2026).
  • Cement manufacturing capacity is stuck at 102-105 million metric tons annually while consumption is 105-108 million tons; the 25% tariff on cement (April 2025) eliminated price-competitive imports from Canada and Mexico, which previously supplied 27% of the US market (2025).
  • Data center construction employment demands are pushing wage inflation 3-4 points above general inflation; Construction M&A deals surged 18.2% to 562 transactions in 2025 as large firms consolidated to access labor and absorb tariff-driven cost spikes (2025).
  • Portland-limestone cement (PLC) now dominates at 97% of blended cement shipments after universal state approval by April 2024; the shift to lower-carbon cement is reshaping production economics and favoring firms like CRH (which acquired Eco Material Technologies in July 2025) and Martin Marietta (Q1 2026 asset swap with QuikCrete) (2024-2026).
  • The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act deployed only $500 billion of $1.2 trillion by September 2025, but seeded a multi-year pipeline of 207,000+ miles of roadway and 11,400+ bridge projects competing for the same cement tonnage as the Stargate Project and mega-data-center expansions (2025).

Who rides it

Large contractors who consolidated through M&A ($562 deals in 2025, up 18.2%) now control crews and can bid on massive projects. Amazon, Microsoft, and the Stargate consortium have the capital to outbid traditional construction and lock crews for multi-year buildouts. Cement firms investing in lower-carbon capacity (CEMEX, Martin Marietta, CRH) are raising margins: CEMEX reported 19% EBITDA growth to $2.27 billion in Q3 2025, signaling demand recovery. Data center builders themselves are insulated by deep pockets and are passing costs onto cloud infrastructure customers. They get the labor, the materials, and the profit.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market contractors are getting squeezed out of bids. Small specialty trades (roofing, electrical, HVAC) cannot command the scale to win data center work and are competing for scraps in office renovation and light industrial. Office landlords are stuck with declining portfolios—office occupancy fell 41% since 2019, and new deliveries dropped 26% in 2025. Retail and hospitality developers are soft. Firms that did not invest in lower-carbon cement production are losing share to tariff-trapped competitors. Importers who relied on Canadian and Mexican cement are facing margin collapse as the 25% tariff makes their supply chains uneconomical.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The binding constraint is not materials or machines—it is sustained labor capacity. The construction industry needs 500,000 more workers by 2027 but is facing structural wage inflation and burnout. If crews cannot be hired fast enough, even the deepest-pocketed data center builders will face project delays. A single delayed mega-facility cascades: cloud providers miss AI training deadlines, chip makers backpedal, and the entire economic thesis of "compute is the new commodity" stalls. Additionally, tariff policy is fragile. If tariff rates shift or are reversed, the domestic cement industry's pricing power evaporates overnight, and low-cost imports (Turkey, Vietnam) flood back into the market, crushing the margins of firms that just invested billions in new capacity to compete on price during the tariff window.

In one sentence

Concrete, steel & the shell in a nutshell

The compute buildout is draining concrete and steel from everywhere else in construction and paying wages that small contractors cannot match—the industry is splitting between mega-data-center winners and everyone else.
