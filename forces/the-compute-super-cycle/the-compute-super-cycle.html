The Compute Super-Cycle

AI's hunger for power is reshaping the grid and everything plugged into it—whoever has electricity, land, and cooling near the data centers wins a once-in-a-generation buildout.

For 15 years, electricity was cheap and transmission was abundant. That era ended in 2024. AI data centers—training rigs burning 30–100+ kilowatts per rack, inference farms running 10–20 kW steady—have turned the US power grid from a taken-for-granted utility into a scarce, rationed resource. The entire economy is suddenly competing for electrons, and the winners are those who own generation, land, and cooling near demand.

What changed

Until 2020, colocation facilities competed on real estate—who had the most square footage. Today they compete on power. The US colocation market reached $72.37 billion in 2026 (up from $46.84 billion in 2025, a 54.6% jump in one year) and occupancy is at 1% vacancy, meaning there is nowhere left to build. The colocation industry's 11.5% CAGR through 2030 masks the real story: pricing has jumped 17–19% in just two years after 15 years of decline. Electricity access, not real estate, is now the moat. The grid cannot keep up. Interconnection queues exceed 1,400 gigawatts of stuck requests, with lead times stretching to four to five years in saturated markets like Northern Virginia, Dallas, and Phoenix. Federal regulators (FERC, December 2025) issued data-center-specific transmission tariffs for PJM, and states are rushing their own—Oregon signed the POWER Act in August 2025 mandating 10-year power-purchase agreements for any facility over 20 megawatts.

This squeeze on electricity has catalyzed what utilities are calling "The Great Re-Gassing." Natural gas generation remains at 40% of US electricity in 2026, but in March 2026, NextEra Energy announced a historic 10-gigawatt natural gas expansion in Texas and Pennsylvania, backed by the Trump administration, explicitly to meet AI data center demand. Duke Energy committed $103 billion in capex for 2026–2030, including 10 gigawatts of new gas procurement in the Carolinas. Southern Company is expanding similarly. The coal industry, which had been in steady decline, saw a temporary rebound to 17% generation share in 2025 (from under 15% previously) as Henry Hub natural gas prices surged 60% in 2025 and are forecast to rise another 30% by 2027. Coal retirements hit a 15-year low of 2.6 gigawatts in 2025, delayed by DOE emergency orders to keep plants online. None of this solves the underlying problem: utilities are racing to build generation and transmission infrastructure that will take years to permit and construct, while data centers are arriving now.

The transmission system is the true bottleneck. The electric power transmission market reached $536.2 billion in 2025 (up from $476.1 billion in 2022, a 12.6% increase in three years), but $1.4 trillion in transmission investment is planned for 2025–2030—and that may not be enough. Data centers will consume 75.8 gigawatts by 2026, accounting for nearly 90 gigawatts of total peak load growth. Wholesale power costs in PJM spiked 49% in 2025, with capacity costs up 262%, driven by data center demand outrunning supply. Profit margins at utilities have climbed to 15% of revenue in 2025, double the historical 7–8% norm. Companies like NextEra and Dominion merged in May–June 2026 (a $66.8 billion deal) to secure generation and transmission assets before constraints tighten further. Battery storage deployment is exploding—24 gigawatts planned for 2026 versus 15 gigawatts added in 2025—as operators scramble to buffer intermittent renewables and smooth demand peaks. Tech giants (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta) are responding by investing directly in generation: buying renewable energy, negotiating long-term PPAs, and signing deals with utilities for dedicated capacity.

This buildout is creating a secondary labor shortage. Data center construction spending hit $50.7 billion annualized in April 2026, up 28% year-over-year, and electricians are the constrained input. Journeymen electricians now earn $76,000–$110,000+, with specialists in solar, EV charging, and data center work commanding premium rates. However, tariffs have hit the industry hard: copper wiring prices jumped 18%, electrical panels 22%, squeezing smaller contractors' margins while consolidation accelerates. The electricians industry as a whole is $370.9 billion (2026) and has added businesses at a 3.7% CAGR, but residential construction has collapsed due to high interest rates, forcing the industry to depend on data center work as its growth engine. The structural mismatch is clear: the grid, generation capacity, transmission, and skilled labor are all becoming scarce at the same moment. Whoever owns renewable generation near cooling water, has transmission capacity, controls real estate, or can command electrician labor wins. Everyone else is stuck in a queue.

The evidence

  • Colocation facilities: Market cap reached $72.37 billion US in 2026 (up from $46.84 billion in 2025) with occupancy at 1% vacancy and pricing up 17–19% after 15 years of decline.
  • Electric power transmission: Market size $536.2 billion in 2025 (up from $476.1 billion in 2022); $1.4 trillion investment planned 2025–2030; utility profit margins doubled to 15% of revenue in 2025 from historical 7–8% norm.
  • Coal and natural gas power: Natural gas remains 40% of US generation (down from 42% in 2024); NextEra Energy announced historic 10 GW gas expansion in March 2026 for AI data centers; coal retirements hit 15-year low of 2.6 GW in 2025 due to emergency delays.
  • Electricians industry: Data center construction spending $50.7 billion annualized (April 2026), up 28% YoY; journeymen earn $76,000–$110,000+; copper wiring up 18%, electrical panels up 22% due to tariffs.
  • Data center interconnection: Queues exceed 1,400 GW with 4–5-year lead times; wholesale power costs in PJM up 49% in 2025 with capacity costs up 262%; NextEra–Dominion merger ($66.8 billion, June 2026) driven by need to secure generation and transmission assets.

Who rides it

Tech giants (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta) and utilities with existing generation assets or access to renewable energy sources win decisively. They can negotiate long-term PPAs, build their own solar and wind farms near data centers, and secure transmission capacity before constraints tighten. Equinix and Digital Realty, the two largest colocation operators (combined ~20% US share), win by raising prices and controlling capacity-constrained facilities. Renewable energy companies win in the near term by securing long-term power contracts. Utilities with coal or gas plants and transmission assets also win—profit margins have doubled—though the long-term bet is on gas expansion (not coal). Large, well-capitalized electrical contractors with data center specialization win; smaller, undercapitalized firms squeeze out.

Who's squeezed

Smaller colocation operators who lack power PPAs or renewable generation contracts lose. Residential construction and small electrical contractors lose because talent and capital flow to higher-margin data center work. Consumers face rising electricity rates as utilities pass through interconnection and capital costs. States without natural gas infrastructure or renewable capacity lose—California and the Mid-Atlantic remain capacity-constrained and cannot attract new major facilities. Coal plant workers lose as retirements resume once gas capacity comes online. Independent power producers without long-term contracts (like Pine Gate Renewables, which filed Chapter 11 in November 2025) lose to scale and consolidation.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is a misalignment between the speed at which data centers are being built and the speed at which the grid can be upgraded. Data center construction is running 28% year-over-year growth, but FERC interconnection rule changes won't take effect until 2026 at the earliest, and transmission buildout takes 5–7 years. If data centers are installed faster than generation and transmission can support them, the grid will experience rolling blackouts, forced demand destruction (shutdowns of data centers or other large loads), and a demand shock that crashes semiconductor and AI chip prices. Alternatively, utilities overbuild natural gas capacity to rush online, locking in carbon-intensive infrastructure for decades and wasting capital on stranded assets once renewables scale. A third risk: permitting bottlenecks remain unsolved. FERC rules help, but state and local permitting in data center hubs (Virginia, Texas, Arizona) is still a multi-year slog. The queue will get worse before it gets better.

In one sentence

The compute super-cycle in a nutshell

AI made electricity and land scarce again, and the power-and-buildout chain is the trade of the decade.
