A · The office breaks

Adaptive Reuse & Conversion

Dead offices get gutted and rebuilt—but the contractors doing it are starving on thin margins.

Hybrid work killed the demand for traditional office space. Rates stayed high. So millions of square feet of Class-B and Class-C office buildings now sit half-empty, especially in secondary markets. That creates a real opportunity: tear out the cubicles, rewire for apartments or mixed-use, and sell the landlord a comeback story. But the contractors doing that work are trapped between material inflation, tariff shock, and a grinding shortage of skilled workers—all crushing margins that were already razor-thin.

What changed

Hybrid work erased 41% of demand for office space since 2019. New office deliveries fell 26% in 2025 alone. National office vacancy sits at 17.6% (April 2026), but the real problem is concentrated: Miami's at 12.5%, but Seattle's bleeding at 25.2%. Class-A trophy towers still command premiums and find tenants; it's the older Class-B and Class-C buildings—the bread-and-butter stock in every American city—that are now dead weight. Those buildings can't compete on price because their rents don't cover debt service at 5.7–6.6% mortgage rates. The math just doesn't work. So owners are forced to either sell at a loss or convert.

That conversion is real work, and it's real opportunity. An office building can become apartments, hotels, co-working, light industrial, storage, mixed-use retail-and-housing. The Commercial Property Remodeling market is $39.8 billion (2026), up from $45.2 billion in 2022 in dollar terms—but that's nominal inflation, not growth. The CAGR from 2021 to 2026 is just 0.8%. In 2026 alone, the market grew only 1.5%. Meanwhile, material costs surged 40.3% from January 2021 to April 2026. Tariffs added another layer: 50% on steel and aluminum, 15% on industrial equipment, 10% on softwood lumber. Contractors are reporting total project escalation of 6–10% just from tariff stacks. That eats the margin. Contractors were already squeezing 3.6% margins (2022), and those are probably compressed further by 2026 due to tariffs and labor inflation.

Labor is the second guillotine. Commercial building construction has a 500,000-worker shortage that costs the industry $10.8 billion annually. The shortage is structural: retirements and chronic underinvestment in trades training mean there simply aren't enough HVAC techs, electricians, plumbers, and framers to go around. Wages are climbing faster than inflation itself—residential construction wages jumped 9.2% year-over-year in mid-2025. Contractors can't turn to offshore labor or automation for the craft work involved in adaptive reuse; every old building is different. They either hire at inflated wages or they turn away jobs. For mid-market remodeling, many are turning away jobs.

But there's an undertow of consolidation happening. Commercial HVAC and mechanical trades are being rolled up by private equity—Blackstone/Legence, Morgan Stanley/Sila, Leonard Green/Wrench—and subcontractor acquisition volume jumped 38.6% year-over-year to 366 deals in 2025. The theory is that larger platforms can absorb tariff inflation through volume, leverage supply chains better, and lock in labor by offering better benefits. Meanwhile, commercial chapter 11 filings hit a 10-year high in 2025 and surged another 76% year-over-year in January 2026 versus January 2025. With $875 billion in commercial mortgages maturing in 2026, refinancing is a bloodbath for anybody who overlevered into office.

The evidence

  • Commercial building construction: Data center construction starts reached $77.7 billion annually in December 2025, a 190% year-over-year surge, with monthly spending averaging $6.5 billion in December 2025 (2025)
  • Commercial property remodeling: Market size $39.8 billion in 2026, up nominally from $45.2 billion in 2022 but growing only 0.8% CAGR; margins at 3.6% in 2022 and compressed by 2026 (2026)
  • Construction labor: 500,000-worker shortage costs the industry $10.8 billion annually; residential construction wages up 9.2% year-over-year mid-2025 (2025)
  • Office market: Office vacancy improved 210 basis points year-over-year to 17.6% by April 2026 (first sustained positive absorption in 18 quarters), but regional spread is severe: Miami 12.5%, Seattle 25.2% (2026)
  • Tariff impact: Material costs surged 40.3% January 2021–April 2026; tariff stacks (50% steel/aluminum, 15% equipment, 10% lumber) pushing total project escalation 6–10% (2026)

Who rides it

PE firms and consolidators win. Blackstone/Legence, Morgan Stanley/Sila, and Leonard Green/Wrench are rolling up mechanical and HVAC trades to build platforms that can absorb tariff shocks and labor wage inflation through scale and better supply-chain leverage. Subcontractor M&A volume jumped 38.6% year-over-year to 366 deals in 2025. Larger contractors with balance sheets can also wait out the remodeling cycle, pick the best projects, and refuse margin-killing work. And the mega-firms doing data center construction (Amazon, Microsoft, Google) are the only segment genuinely thriving—$77.7 billion in starts in 2025 (up 190%) makes office look like a graveyard by comparison.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market and small remodeling contractors get squeezed into bankruptcy or consolidation. The industry has 29,469 highly fragmented establishments with no player over 5% share and 80,000–90,000 workers. Those contractors can't absorb tariff inflation or wage pressure without cutting margins below viability. They can't wait out the cycle; they need work now. They face a choice: merge up into a PE platform, specialize narrowly (only luxury conversions, only historic preservation), or exit. Office landlords holding Class-B and Class-C stock also suffer, especially those who financed at 3% and now can't refinance the maturity wall; $875 billion in commercial mortgages maturing in 2026 will force fire sales or extended holding patterns.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The conversion pipeline could stall entirely if labor wages and tariff costs don't stabilize. Adaptive reuse is inherently expensive and unpredictable—every old building is different. Contractors need predictable material costs and stable labor to bid those jobs. Right now, a 6–10% tariff-driven escalation can blow a project's underwriting and tank the deal. If tariffs stay at 50% on steel through 2026–2027, and construction wages keep climbing at 9%+ annually, the math breaks. Owners might simply hold empty buildings and wait for a recession or tariff relief rather than convert. That would freeze a sector that's supposed to absorb the 41% decline in office demand, leaving millions of square feet in limbo and small contractors permanently damaged.

In one sentence

Adaptive reuse & conversion in a nutshell

Hybrid work and higher rates created a $39.8 billion adaptive-reuse opportunity, but tariffs and labor shortages are crushing the margins of the fragmented contractors supposed to build it—pushing capital upmarket to PE rollups and away from the small firms that do most of the actual work.
