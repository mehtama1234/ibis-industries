A · The office breaks

The office crisis

Hybrid work gutted office values while refinancing walls and conversion pipelines reshape American cities.

The office sector is in structural crisis. National office vacancy peaked at 19.6% in Q1 2025 and only fell to 17.7% by mid-2026 — a level that economists call unrecoverable. Hybrid work broke the occupancy math. Higher interest rates broke the refinance math. Both forces hit at once, and the collision reshaped the entire commercial real estate sector.

What changed

Hybrid work gutted office occupancy. When employees stopped coming in five days a week, vacancy exploded across secondary and tertiary markets. San Francisco and Austin — both tech hubs — saw vacancy rates exceed 26% by 2025. Class B and C office buildings became economically obsolete overnight. The only offices that stayed strong were Class A trophy assets in central business districts, the kind that multinational firms anchor with prestigious leases. Every other building — the mid-market office, the suburban corporate park, the regional tower — faced a choice: find new tenants who no longer existed, or die.

The refinance crisis turned distress into catastrophe. In commercial real estate, assets mature on predictable cycles: a loan taken out in 2013 comes due in 2023 or 2024. In 2025 alone, $957 billion in CRE loans matured. Through 2028, another $2.8 trillion comes due. Lenders who refinanced at 3% rates can now only get 6% or 7%. The math breaks. If occupancy is down 20 percent and rents are flat, the owner cannot service new debt at double the cost. CMBS delinquencies reached 7.29% in 2025 — the highest distress signal in the market. Chapter 11 bankruptcies hit a 10-year high, with 694 corporate filings in 2025, the most in 14 years.

Conversion became the survival strategy. Instead of fighting for tenants, real estate owners pivoted to adaptive reuse: tearing out office cubicles and building apartments. Office-to-apartment conversions reached 90,300 units in 2026, a 28% jump year-over-year. Now 47 percent of the entire 193,900-unit adaptive reuse pipeline is former office space. This signals defeat. It means owners acknowledged that the office market will not recover at historical occupancy rates. It means they see more value in apartments — a bet on the housing shortage (3.4 million homes short nationally) and the 105 percent premium to buy versus rent — than in rehabilitating their office towers.

Architecture felt the shockwave first and hardest. When office construction cratered, billings collapsed. The Architecture Billings Index hit 47.6 in October 2025, a reading below 50 that signals declining demand. Thirty-eight percent of firms reported falling revenue. Small independent practices, already thin on margins, took the biggest hit: firms with under $250,000 in annual billings expected a 7 percent revenue drop. Seventy percent of declining firms cited fewer new projects ahead. AI software accelerated the squeeze, automating site planning, specification writing, code-violation detection, and BIM workflows. Larger, consolidated firms integrated these tools fast; smaller practices could not compete and faced pressure to merge or shut down. Meanwhile, consolidators like Stantec acquired UK firm Hydrock in May 2024, adding 950 professionals and signaling that private equity saw opportunity in rolled-up, consolidated practices. The sector is consolidating under pressure, not from growth, but from survival.

The evidence

  • Real Estate: $957 billion in CRE loans matured in 2025 alone, with another $2.8 trillion maturing through 2028 as refinance rates doubled.
  • Real Estate: Office vacancy peaked at 19.6% in Q1 2025 and only fell to 17.7% by mid-2026, forcing Class B and C buildings into obsolescence.
  • Real Estate: Office-to-apartment conversions reached 90,300 units in 2026 (up 28% YoY), now representing 47% of the entire adaptive reuse pipeline.
  • Architecture: The Architecture Billings Index hit 47.6 in October 2025, with 38% of firms reporting falling revenue and small practices facing 7% cuts.
  • Real Estate: Multifamily net absorption hit 124,600 units in Q2 2026 with investor transaction volume up 39.5% YoY, the strongest sector as capital flees offices.

Who rides it

Real estate investment trusts and large, consolidated architecture and engineering firms are riding the conversion wave. Capital rotates into multifamily assets (124,600 units absorbed in Q2 2026) powered by the housing shortage and 105% buy-versus-rent premium. Industrial real estate thrives: 940 million sq ft leased in 2025, the second-best year on record, as e-commerce tenants account for 56% of retail goods sales growth. Large consolidated practices — especially those backed by PE capital — integrate AI tools, absorb smaller competitors, and win the shrinking pool of projects. Only Class A trophy offices in CBDs retain premium occupancy; owners there hold pricing power.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market and Class B/C office owners face decade-long losses and forced debt restructuring. Small independent architecture practices are squeezed hardest: facing revenue declines of 7 percent, unable to afford AI tools, and losing deals to consolidated competitors. Junior architects and traditional drafting roles face automation. Real estate development firms focused on office are obsolete or in bankruptcy. Regional and suburban office markets see permanent demand destruction as hybrid work reshapes occupancy norms. The $63.5 billion architecture sector grows at 0.1% in 2026, a practical standstill, as commercial construction remains dormant.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is a cascading debt spiral. CMBS delinquencies are already at 7.29%, but the real wave hits 2027-2028 when the bulk of the $2.8 trillion refinance wall matures. Owners who cannot reposition (convert, or sell at a loss) will default, flooding the market with distressed assets and depressing pricing further. A debt standstill among major commercial landlords could choke both capital availability and development momentum for years. If conversion pipelines saturate and apartment supply finally catches up to demand, the single remaining exit for office owners closes, and a permanent loss spiral begins.
