Retail's Property Fallout

Store closures are flooding retail real estate onto the market as tariffs and e-commerce crush the department store model.

Department stores, the backbone of American shopping malls for decades, are imploding. Tariff rates jumped from 14.7% in January 2025 to 35.1% by December 2025, crushing margins. Saks Global—which had just merged Saks Fifth Avenue, Neiman Marcus, and Bergdorf Goodman—filed for bankruptcy in January 2026 carrying $3.4 billion in debt. Macy's is closing 150 stores by end-2026, dumping prime retail real estate onto the market faster than it can be repurposed.

What changed

For decades, a department store on Main Street or in a regional mall was money: stable anchor tenant, foot traffic, reliable credit quality. That math broke in 2025-2026. Tariffs on apparel imports exploded—14.7% in January 2025, climbing to 35.1% by December 2025—compressing margins industry-wide from 6.1% in 2024 to an estimated 3.6% if retailers absorbed the costs. Retailers had three bad choices: eat the tariff hit and wipe out profit, pass it to customers and watch traffic tank, or manufacture differently. None worked. Saks Global, carrying $3.4 billion in debt after its December 2024 merger of three iconic luxury banners, hit a wall in January 2026 when vendor payment delays cascaded and store productivity collapsed. That bankruptcy was the signal. It told landlords that even prestige retail was vulnerable.

Mainstream department stores followed. Macy's executed a controlled retreat, closing 50 stores by end-2024, then 66 in 2025, with 14 more planned for 2026—that's 150 doors gone, each one freeing a landlord to either hold vacant space, discount massively, or let the tenant walk. JCPenney's FY2024 revenue fell to $6.6 billion despite a 'Yes, JCPenney' comeback campaign that lifted May 2025 traffic by 3%. Nordstrom's family launched a $3.8 billion bid to take the company private in late 2025-2026, signaling they saw more value in the off-price Rack format than in traditional department stores. The shift was structural, not cyclical. Consumer spending bifurcated: affluent households kept spending; lower and middle-income households traded down to discount stores at five times faster rates in 2026, with inflation (PCE up 4.1% year-over-year as of May 2026) driving the wedge. Discount stores and off-price retailers (TJX, Ross) won that migration. Department stores were caught in the middle, losing traffic to cheaper channels above and below them.

The real estate consequence is cascading vacancy and repricing. Mall anchors are no longer anchors. Department store real estate—prime 50,000-100,000-square-foot boxes in regional malls and downtowns—is flooding the market with no clear next tenant. The 666,500 employees in US department stores are declining 2.87% annually through 2028; fewer jobs means weaker demand for nearby service space, parking, and logistics. Landlords face a choice: hold space at distressed rents, convert to fitness, dining, or entertainment, or accept lease terminations and redevelop. Each option destroys value vs. the pre-2020 model. Off-price and discount retail are expanding—Ross Stores reported comparable store sales up 17% in Q3 2025 and announced ~110 new store openings for 2026—but those boxes are smaller, lower-rent, and serve different locations. They won't fill the real estate vacuum left by department store collapse.

The evidence

  • Department stores market: $227.1B in 2026, declining at 0.4% in 2026 alone with 6.0% margins compressed by tariffs and discounting (IBISWorld 2026).
  • Apparel tariff shock: rates climbed from 14.7% (Jan 2025) to 35.1% (Dec 2025), cutting retail profit margins by estimated 2.5 percentage points if absorbed (US trade data, 2025-2026).
  • Saks Global bankruptcy: $3.4B debt January 2026 after luxury merge; Macy's 150-store closure by end-2026; Nordstrom family $3.8B take-private bid (2026 retail consolidation).
  • Consumer trade-down: lower/middle-income households shifting to discount stores at 5x faster rates in 2026 with 4.1% PCE inflation (May 2026 consumer behavior data).
  • Off-price winning: Ross Stores +17% comp sales Q3 2025 with ~110 new openings for 2026; TJX gaining share from collapsing mid-tier (Q3 2025 earnings).

Who rides it

Off-price and discount retailers are the clear winners. Ross Stores posted 17% comparable store sales growth in Q3 2025 and announced 110 new store openings for 2026. TJX and other value formats are capturing traffic from the collapsing mid-tier. The "treasure hunt" experience of curated, changing inventory remains defensible against e-commerce, so discount stores are outperforming traditional department stores across the board. Nordstrom's family, betting $3.8 billion on taking the company private, sees the future in Rack expansion, not in full-line department stores. Logistics and alternative uses for vacated real estate may also win—repurposed department store boxes can become storage, fitness, dining, or entertainment venues if landlords are creative.

Who's squeezed

Department store landlords and the vendors, workers, and service providers that depend on them are getting squeezed. Macy's is closing 150 stores by end-2026; JCPenney is fighting for survival despite a 3% traffic bump; even luxury collapsed (Saks Global bankruptcy). Department store employees are declining 2.87% annually through 2028. Mall owners are losing their anchor tenants. Landlords are either holding vacant space, offering fire-sale rents to attract new tenants (who may never come), or accepting lease terminations and redevelopment losses. Regional malls that built their entire model around department store anchors are facing existential questions about their future.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is the speed and cascade of store closures creating a real estate death spiral in regional malls and secondary downtowns. Macy's 150 stores, Saks Global bankruptcy, JCPenney's struggles, and smaller retailers' exits are all happening in 2025-2026 simultaneously. As landlords lose anchor tenants and traffic, they cut maintenance, raise rents on remaining tenants (squeezing smaller shops), and the mall spirals down. Secondary and tertiary locations are most vulnerable—a mall in a smaller metro loses its Macy's and has no clear alternative anchor. If landlords can't find new uses or tenants fast enough, you see vacancy cascades, property value crashes, and forced sales at steep discounts that ripple through REIT portfolios and commercial property financing.

In one sentence

Retail's property fallout in a nutshell

Tariffs and e-commerce broke department stores; now their real estate is flooding the market with no clear next tenant, forcing landlords to either discount massively, hold vacant space, or accept redevelopment losses.
