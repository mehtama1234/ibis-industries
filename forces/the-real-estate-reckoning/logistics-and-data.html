Logistics & Data Centers: Capital Finds Electricity

Warehouses and data centers pull capital from office towers because they move goods and compute, not people—and the race for power has reshaped what matters most.

Hybrid work killed the case for downtown office towers. But it also turned AI and e-commerce from trends into infrastructure problems. Developers and capital are no longer fighting over square footage. They're fighting over power, grid access, and the industrial real estate that can handle 30-100 kW per rack or move packages at scale. The U.S. colocation market has nearly doubled in five years—from $26 billion in 2021 to $72 billion in 2026—and that money is still moving.

What changed

For fifteen years, colocation facilities were cheap and plentiful. A data center operator could lease space, plug in servers, and compete on price. That game is over. AI training demands 30 to 100 kilowatts per single server rack. Inference at 10 to 20 kW per rack still looks simple until you multiply by thousands. The constraint flipped: electricity is now the scarcity, not space. North American colocation occupancy hit 1% vacancy in 2026. Operators are buying sites not for acreage but for power—proximity to hydroelectric dams, natural gas plants, or utilities willing to guarantee grid capacity. The Texas hyperscale campus that ECP and KKR announced in July 2025 (part of a $50 billion AI infrastructure partnership) exists because Texas utilities have capacity. The U.K. subsidiary acquisition by Digital Realty ($200 million, July 2024) happened because London has grid slots.

The electrical grid itself became the bottleneck. Grid interconnection requests now exceed 1,400 GW stuck in queue, with lead times of four to five years for high-capacity connections in saturated markets like Northern Virginia, Dallas, and Phoenix. In December 2025, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) broke the dam by issuing data-center-specific transmission tariffs for the PJM Interconnection—the largest regional grid operator. Starting January 2026, data centers had to follow new rules about how they buy power and pay for grid upgrades. Oregon's POWER Act (August 2025) flipped the model: facilities over 20 MW now must sign ten-year power-purchase agreements with renewable generators. California and Virginia followed with large-load tariffs starting at 25-40 MW thresholds. Power pricing jumped 17-19% in the last two years—the first sustained spike after fifteen years of decline.

Capital is flowing to the operators and utilities that control power, not space. Equinix and Digital Realty—the two largest U.S. colocation companies (10.28% and 9.98% market share in 2024)—are consolidating smaller operators and bidding aggressively on grid contracts. The market is growing at 11.5% compound annual growth in the U.S. through 2030, and globally at 14% CAGR, but that growth is narrowing to companies with utility partnerships, real estate in high-power zones, and access to capital to build custom cooling (liquid-cooled racks need specialized design).

Meanwhile, logistics real estate is less dramatic but just as locked in. Warehouses that move boxes—parcel hubs, last-mile centers, regional distribution nodes—have become essential to the e-commerce flywheel. That demand is steady and boring, which is exactly why capital moved there. Unlike offices (uncertain occupancy) or data centers (capital-intensive, technically risky), logistics is a proven yield play: ground leases at 6-8% on income-producing assets in tier-1 nodes (Atlanta, Dallas, Los Angeles, Chicago). The rents on well-located warehouses have held firm even as office space craters, because the goods still have to move.

The evidence

  • Colocation (Technology & Digital): U.S. market grew from $26 billion in 2021 to $72.37 billion in 2026, with 11.5% annual growth through 2030 driven by AI infrastructure.
  • Data centers: Occupancy at 1% vacancy in 2026; 1,400+ GW of grid interconnection requests stuck with 4-5 year lead times in Northern Virginia, Dallas, and Phoenix.
  • Power costs: Colocation pricing jumped 17-19% in two years (2024-2026) after 15 years of decline, and FERC mandated data-center-specific transmission tariffs (December 2025).
  • Logistics (implied from real estate capital flow): Warehouse rents in tier-1 nodes (Atlanta, Dallas, Los Angeles, Chicago) holding firm at 6-8% yields even as office vacancy spikes.
  • Regulatory shift: Oregon POWER Act (August 2025), California and Virginia large-load tariffs (2025-2026), and FY 2026 federal infrastructure authorization cliff (September 2026) reshaping regional competitiveness.

Who rides it

Operators with power contracts win—Equinix and Digital Realty (10.28% and 9.98% U.S. market share) consolidate smaller rivals and ink utility deals. Regions with stable renewable power or utility capacity (Texas, Oregon, parts of the Pacific Northwest) attract the bulk of new hyperscale investment. Logistics operators in tier-1 nodes see steady demand and pricing power because goods still need to move, and e-commerce growth is inelastic to recessions. States and utilities that act fast on power policy—Oregon's fast-track PPAs, FERC's new tariffs—become migration magnets for AI infrastructure capex that used to flow to office parks.

Who's squeezed

Developers who own empty office towers get squeezed as capital rotates to power-constrained data centers and logistics hubs. Mid-market colocation operators without utility partnerships face margin collapse as power costs rise 17-19% and occupancy is already maxed. Construction firms building data center campuses face labor shortages and tariffs on materials—heavy engineering construction margins are compressing from 7.5% to 5% even as labor wages jump 8-12% annually. States without power capacity or outdated grid infrastructure lose AI capex entirely; Northern Virginia is saturated, and grid queues now favor greenfield sites in deregulated regions.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The power grid itself is the single biggest fragility. Interconnection queues exceed 1,400 GW with four-to-five-year waits for new capacity in saturated markets. If AI demand keeps accelerating and utilities can't build capacity fast enough, data center operators will face forced load-shedding, outages, or have to build their own on-site generation (hugely expensive and regionally constrained by fuel supply and environmental permitting). A hard recession could stall the federal infrastructure funding that has propped up heavy construction, since authorization expires in September 2026 and state/local budgets are already tight. If grid tariffs or renewable PPAs become unaffordable, AI capex could freeze or migrate offshore to cheaper electricity zones, leaving U.S. colocation operators with expensive build-outs and no demand.

In one sentence

Logistics & data centers in a nutshell

Capital flowed from office real estate to logistics and data centers because they move goods and compute—but now the constraint is electricity, not space, and power access has become the only moat that matters.
