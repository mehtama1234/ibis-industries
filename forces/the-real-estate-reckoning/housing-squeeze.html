The Housing Squeeze

High rates freeze buying and pressure multifamily even as housing stays scarce.

The housing market faces a squeeze from two sides at once. Buyers can't afford to buy because rates stay near 6%, while landlords in rent-controlled cities can't raise rents fast enough to cover soaring insurance and labor costs. The result: a frozen market where builders cut prices and offer mortgage buydowns, apartment companies merge into mega-REITs to survive, and smaller operators face bankruptcy.

What changed

Higher interest rates broke the math on both sides of the housing market. On the homebuilding side, a buyer who could afford a $400,000 house with a 3% rate now can't afford that same house at 6%. Builders saw housing starts crash 15.4% from January to May 2026, hitting 1.177 million units—the lowest since May 2020. Rather than wait for demand to return, they've started cutting prices by an average of 5% and offering to pay down mortgage rates for buyers just to move inventory. At the same time, construction tariffs hit 25-30% in 2025, raising the cost of each home by about $10,900, and labor shortages cost the industry $10.8 billion annually. Builders are getting caught between higher input costs and lower prices—a squeeze that squeezes margins from 6.1% in 2022 down to 6-8% by 2026. In response, the industry is consolidating fast. Between August 2025 and June 2026, the share of builders reporting acquisition approaches doubled from 9% to 18%, and major deals like Taylor Morrison going to Berkshire Hathaway, Tri Pointe to Sumitomo Forestry, and United Homes Group to Stanley Martin show that only scale survives. The US home builders industry is now flat at $166.2 billion in 2026 revenue with near-zero growth, a far cry from the building boom of 2021–2023.

On the rental side, the squeeze takes a different form. After years of climbing rents—double-digit growth in 2021–2023—the apartment market hit a wall. Vacancy rates climbed from 6.3% in 2021 to 7.0% in Q2 2025, and luxury segments sit at 11.4% vacant. Developers chased the rent-growth tailwind and built aggressively in the Sun Belt and Mountain West, but when occupancy tightened, owners stopped raising rents and started offering concessions instead. The apartment rental market is still huge at $305.7 billion in 2026 revenue, but growth has slowed to 1.7% annually. Worse, rent-controlled markets like New York and California face a cruel squeeze. Tenants pay record-high percentages of their income for housing, yet landlords cannot raise rents fast enough—NYC caps increases at 2.75%–5.25% per year, California at 5% plus inflation capped at 10%—to cover surging insurance, labor, and maintenance costs. Multifamily loan delinquencies hit 1.37% in Q3 2025, a 3.4× increase over two years and the highest since 2008. Smaller leveraged owners and rent-stabilized properties face the worst of it: Pinnacle filed for bankruptcy in May 2025 with $500M in mortgage debt; 5,100 of its rent-stabilized NYC apartments went to auction. Sonder, the short-term rental company, collapsed entirely when Marriott terminated its licensing deal and it filed for Chapter 7 liquidation in 2025. Consolidation is the only exit. AvalonBay Communities and Equity Residential announced a $69 billion merger to create the nation's largest apartment REIT with 180,000+ units and $175M in cost cuts expected within 18 months—a vote of no confidence in independent operators' ability to survive.

What became scarce? Affordable housing, paradoxically, even though oversupply is pushing rents down in competitive markets. In rent-controlled cities, supply is locked in place by regulation, and demand for those cheap units far exceeds supply. What became cheap? Single-family homes, at least in hot markets like Florida and Texas where builders are cutting prices to clear inventory. What became risky? Leverage on apartment buildings, especially those with rent-stabilized portfolios that can't cover rising costs. What became newly valuable? Scale and capital strength. Big builders like D.R. Horton and Lennar can absorb tariff shocks and rate headwinds; small builders get acquired or disappear. Big REITs like the new AvalonBay-Equity Residential colossus can negotiate better insurance rates, automate labor, and hide losses in diversified portfolios; small mom-and-pop landlords go bankrupt.

The evidence

  • Homebuilding (Construction): US housing starts collapsed 15.4% from January to May 2026 to 1.177 million annualized units, lowest since May 2020, as tariffs at 25-30% effective rate added $10,900 per home and rates stayed near 6%.
  • Homebuilding (Construction): M&A activity among builders doubled between August 2025 and June 2026, with 18% reporting acquisition approaches; major deals include Taylor Morrison to Berkshire Hathaway and Tri Pointe to Sumitomo Forestry.
  • Apartment Rental (Real Estate): Apartment vacancy rates climbed to 7.0% in Q2 2025 from 6.3% in 2021, with luxury segments at 11.4% vacant, ending years of double-digit rent growth and forcing concessions.
  • Apartment Rental (Real Estate): Multifamily loan delinquencies hit 1.37% in Q3 2025, 3.4× higher over two years and highest since 2008, driven by rent-stabilized properties squeezed between frozen rents and soaring costs.
  • Apartment Rental (Real Estate): AvalonBay Communities and Equity Residential announced a $69 billion merger closing in 2026 to create 180,000+ unit mega-REIT with $175M in cost cuts, reflecting consolidation as smaller operators face bankruptcy.

Who rides it

Big builders and mega-REITs win. D.R. Horton, Lennar, and PulteGroup have the scale and cash to absorb tariff shocks, hold price through a down cycle, and buy distressed competitors. On the rental side, the AvalonBay-Equity Residential merger creates a giant that can negotiate insurance discounts, invest in automation to replace expensive labor, and diversify risk across 180,000 units. Capital flows to winners who can play a multi-year game.

Who's squeezed

Small builders and independent landlords get squeezed out. A builder with a handful of projects can't absorb a $10,900 per-unit tariff cost while cutting prices 5% and offering rate buydowns—margins don't pencil. A mom-and-pop landlord with a rent-stabilized building in New York faces a $5,000-per-unit per-year gap between frozen rents (capped at 2.75%–5.25%) and soaring insurance and labor costs. Bankruptcy becomes the only exit. Pinnacle, Sonder, and countless unnamed operators are liquidated to pay debt, and their portfolios get picked up by REITs at distressed prices.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The debt wall. Multifamily delinquencies at 1.37% in Q3 2025 are already at financial-crisis levels, and most distressed debt is still working through the courts or negotiating workouts—the real wave of defaults could take years to clear. If commercial real estate debt—maturities are heavy in 2026–2027 for office and weaker multifamily—has to refinance at higher rates, the pain spreads to the banks that hold the mortgages and the insurance companies that insure the loans. A cascading default scenario could freeze credit and tip the broader economy into recession.

In one sentence

The housing squeeze in a nutshell

Higher rates and tariffs trapped homebuilders in a margin crunch while rent controls left apartment owners unable to cover surging costs, forcing both sides to consolidate around winners with scale and capital strength.
