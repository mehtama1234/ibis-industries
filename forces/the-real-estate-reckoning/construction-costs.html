D · The tensions

Building Costs Bite

Tariffs, labor, and rates raise the cost to build anything new.

The construction industry is caught in a vise. Labor shortages, tariff shocks, and elevated borrowing costs have made new buildings more expensive to build and harder to finance. The split is brutal: data centers (the only growth story) are surging while traditional offices, retail, and housing are stalling.

What changed

The commercial construction market in the US is $315.5 billion in 2026, growing just 1.1%—a reflection of a deep, two-tier split. Data center construction is the only engine: starts reached $77.7 billion in 2025, a stunning 190% jump from 2024, as Amazon commits $23 billion to an Ohio facility and Microsoft deploys what it calls the world's most powerful data center in Wisconsin, both coming online in 2026. The Stargate Project, a $500 billion four-year AI infrastructure investment by OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle, and MGX announced in January 2025, anchors long-term pipelines. But office construction hit a wall. Hybrid work hollowed out office demand by 41% since 2019; new office deliveries dropped 26% in 2025, and vacancy concentrates in older Class-B and Class-C towers while Class-A trophy buildings stay full at premium rents. Retail, warehouse, and hospitality remain soft but stable—no recovery in sight.

The binding constraint is labor scarcity, which now costs the industry $10.8 billion annually. A 500,000-worker shortage has pushed wage inflation to 4% per year and forced mid-market contractors to turn away work. Material costs are surging from tariff shocks: effective tariff rates on construction goods hit 25–30% in 2025 (the highest in 40 years), raising the typical home cost by $10,900 per unit. December 2025 saw monthly spending on data center construction average $6.5 billion—up from just $500 million in mid-2021. Elevated mortgage rates at 5.7–6.6% in August 2026 have frozen financing for traditional commercial deals, squeezing both developers and lenders.

The home builders industry reflects this pain even more sharply. At $166.2 billion in 2026, the market is flat (0.0% growth in 2026, 1.1% CAGR from 2021–2026) and margins are compressed to 6–8% net, down from 6.1% in 2022. Housing starts crashed 15.4% month-over-month in May 2026 to 1.177 million (seasonally adjusted annual rate), the lowest since May 2020, as buyers confronted the combined shock of tariff-driven costs and persistent high rates near 6%. Builders have cut prices by an average of 5% and are offering mortgage rate buydowns to move inventory, hollowing out profits. Sixty percent of builders report tariff-driven cost increases; smaller builders are hit hardest because they cannot absorb the margin squeeze. M&A activity among home builders doubled between August 2025 and June 2026: 18% of builders report acquisition approaches (up from 9%), signaling that scale and capital strength now separate winners from sellers. Major deals include Taylor Morrison acquired by Berkshire Hathaway, Tri Pointe Homes acquired by Sumitomo Forestry, and United Homes Group and Holiday Builders acquired by Stanley Martin Homes. Construction M&A deals as a whole surged 18.2% to 562 transactions in 2025 as firms consolidated to offset labor shortages and rising input costs.

This is a structural reckoning, not a cycle. Tariff regimes are sticky, labor shortages reflect demographic collapse, and rates will not reset to 3% overnight. The data center boom is real and capital-intensive, but it does not employ construction workers at the same scale as mid-market office and retail projects. Smaller builders, independent contractors, and traditional commercial developers face a narrowing window: either consolidate, pivot to niche markets (or niche markets), or exit.

The evidence

  • Commercial Building Construction in the US: $315.5 billion market (2026) with 1.1% growth; data center starts surged 190% to $77.7 billion in 2025, but office deliveries fell 26% in 2025 as hybrid work reduced demand by 41% since 2019; labor shortage costs $10.8 billion annually.
  • Home Builders in the US: $166.2 billion market (2026) with flat 0.0% growth; housing starts crashed 15.4% month-over-month in May 2026 to 1.177 million annualized (lowest since May 2020); tariffs added $10,900 per unit cost; M&A activity doubled (18% of builders acquired or approached by June 2026 vs. 9% in August 2025).
  • Tariff Impact (2025-2026): Effective tariff rates on construction goods hit 25-30% in 2025 (40-year high); 60% of builders report tariff-driven cost increases; construction M&A surged 18.2% to 562 transactions in 2025 as firms consolidated.
  • Data Center Boom (2025-2026): Stargate Project announced ($500 billion over four years); Amazon committed $23 billion to Ohio expansion; Microsoft deployed mega-facility in Wisconsin; both due live in 2026; monthly data center spending reached $6.5 billion in December 2025.
  • Labor and Rates (2025-2026): 500,000-worker shortage drives 4% annual wage growth; mortgage rates 5.7-6.6% in August 2026; margin compression to 6-8% net for builders (down from 6.1% in 2022).

Who rides it

Large-cap builders with scale and capital (D.R. Horton, Lennar, PulteGroup, Berkshire Hathaway, Sumitomo Forestry) are consolidating smaller rivals and can absorb tariff shocks and margin compression. Data center developers and capital-intensive infrastructure firms tied to AI booms (Amazon, Microsoft, OpenAI, SoftBank) are winning massive project pipelines and long-term contracts. Established commercial real estate players with Class-A trophy assets in tight markets command premiums while older Class-B and Class-C buildings struggle.

Who's squeezed

Smaller home builders, independent contractors, and mid-market commercial developers are squeezed on both price and cost. Labor shortages force them to turn away jobs or pay premiums they cannot pass through. Tariffs bite hardest on smaller firms with thin margins and limited purchasing power. Retail and hospitality developers are stuck in soft markets with no end in sight. Regional construction firms without scale or diversification into data centers face consolidation pressure or exit.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The labor shortage is structural, not cyclical. Demographic decline, immigration politics, and decades of underinvestment in construction training mean the 500,000-worker gap will not close soon. Tariff regimes, whether 25-30% or higher, look sticky given political commitments and reshoring ideology. If mortgage rates stay above 5.5%, housing starts will remain depressed, which means fewer new units, which means rents stay high, which means first-time buyers stay locked out. The debt wall in commercial real estate (maturing office mortgages, refinancing risk) could trigger a cascade of distressed sales and further consolidation.

In one sentence

Building costs bite in a nutshell

Tariffs, labor shortages, and high rates have split construction into two economies: data centers booming at $77.7 billion starts in 2025 while homes, offices, and retail stall, squeezing smaller builders and forcing consolidation.
