B · Where capital rotates

Owning the Land

Land leasing and RV parks shift from growth to scarcity—a steady, consolidating landlord business serving renters priced out of home ownership.

Office real estate lost its tenant base to hybrid work and rising interest rates killed its financing math. But capital doesn't disappear—it rotates. Into land. Into RV parks. Into manufactured housing communities. These asset classes are small, sleepy-sounding, and boring by design. They're also consolidating fast, commanding near-total occupancy, and printing 47% margins while traditional property chokes.

What changed

Land leasing in the US is a $16.0 billion market (2026) moving at 0.6% CAGR since 2022, but growth is accelerating right now because housing is genuinely unaffordable. The industry serves two overlapping populations squeezed out of homeownership: price-conscious renters who can't afford stick-built homes, and retirees who are aging through their 60s and 70s and don't want property maintenance. RV adoption stayed high after the pandemic as remote work made mobility realistic. Occupancy rates sit near 95%—nearly full—and land leasing operators pushed through 7% rent growth in 2025 without backing down. That pricing power comes from scarcity. New community development is stuck. Zoning boards in most states treat manufactured housing as a nuisance, forcing developers to spend 24-48 months and $60,000 per site just to get a new campground or mobile home community open. Each year the supply stays constrained, existing operators become harder to dislodge.

Regulators are starting to notice the rental squeeze and pushing back: Washington state capped annual lot rent increases at 5% in May 2025, and Florida and Virginia are loosening zoning to allow more manufactured homes. But these policy moves won't matter if supply stays locked. In campgrounds and RV parks—a subset of land leasing that's $11.3 billion and flat to declining in volume—the structural play is even clearer. Total reservations fell 1.0% in 2025, the first annual decline in platform history, but revenue rose 5.2%. Operators raised prices instead of filling more sites. Average nightly rates at medium parks jumped 16.6% to $83. This is the opposite of growth-through-expansion; it's yield-through-power. Glamping—luxury camping with comfortable beds and hot showers—grew 43.6% while regular RV parks flatlined, revealing that younger, remote workers are willing to pay premium prices for a mobile lifestyle. Consolidation is accelerating. Sun Communities spent $457 million in October 2025 acquiring 14 manufactured housing and RV communities, then immediately divested its 129-property marina portfolio to exit a riskier business. KOA added new communities in North Carolina and Massachusetts in 2025, but these are rare; most capital is flowing into consolidation of existing sites, not greenfield development.

The economics are becoming clearer too. Cap rates for institutional investors sit at 8.0%, but the actual cost of borrowing runs 8.7-9.3% per year. That means day-one leverage is negative; returns depend on slowly paying down debt and repositioning the asset, not on cash-on-cash returns. This is not a get-rich-quick play. It's a bond-substitute play: you're taking operational leverage and accepting single-digit cash flow yields in exchange for inflation-protected rent growth and full occupancy. The 47.3% EBIT margins for land leasing (2026) are remarkably stable because the operator is just charging rent and maintaining infrastructure; there's no variable cost to scale. Labor is rising—parks struggle to hire groundskeeping staff—but that's a cost of maintaining the asset, not a margin killer. The 2025 Guadalupe River flood and Hurricane Helene exposed uninsured waterfront risk and prompted Texas to tighten safety codes, but this just favors larger REITs who can absorb insurance costs and regulatory adaptation. There are ~98,000 land leasing operators in the US (2026), ranging from one-person lot rentals to huge REITs like ELS (452 communities, 173,201 sites) and Sun Communities (5,000+ communities). Consolidation is accelerating because small operators can't navigate zoning friction, insurance risk, and regulatory patchwork anymore.

The evidence

  • Land leasing: US market at $16.0 billion (2026) with 47.3% EBIT margins, serving ~98,000 operators and 129,000 employees, anchored by REITs like ELS (452 communities, 173,201 sites) consolidating smaller landlords.
  • RV parks and campgrounds: $11.3 billion market (2026) with 15,000+ establishments, 88% independently owned; revenue rose 5.2% in 2025 while total reservations fell 1.0%—pricing power over volume growth.
  • Occupancy and pricing: Manufactured housing communities near 95% occupancy (2025-2026) with 7% YoY rent growth; campground nightly rates jumped 16.6% to $83 average (medium parks, 2025).
  • Premium shift: Glamping grew 43.6% in 2025 while conventional RV parks flatlined, attracting younger remote-work-enabled demographics willing to pay higher prices.
  • Supply barrier: New campground development requires 24-48 months permitting and costs $60,000 per site, creating durable scarcity that protects incumbents and drives consolidation—Sun Communities spent $457 million acquiring 14 communities (October 2025).

Who rides it

Real-estate investment trusts (REITs) like Sun Communities and ELS benefit from consolidation as regulatory complexity and insurance risk make small operators unviable. Large campground networks like KOA can adapt faster to premium demand (glamping growth +43.6%) and absorb financing costs. Operators of existing land leases and RV parks win through scarcity—95% occupancy and 7% rent growth with no volume pressure. Younger, remote-enabled travelers and retirees willing to accept mobile or manufactured housing get access to affordable shelter, though at rising costs.

Who's squeezed

Small, independent land-leasing operators (majority of the 98,000 total) are squeezed out by zoning friction, insurance risk, and consolidation pressure. Mid-market campground owners face negative day-one leverage (loan rates 8.7-9.3% exceed cap rates 8.0%) and rising labor costs, forcing them to sell to REITs or abandon expansion. Renters and retirees face 7% annual rent growth and shrinking affordable housing supply as policy pushback (Washington's 5% cap, zoning loosening) lags actual demand. Conventional homebuyers remain locked out as mortgage rates stay high and land-lease communities capture inventory.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is a recession that halts remote work and RV adoption, collapsing demand just as occupancy sits at 95% and operators have locked in high debt levels with negative day-one leverage. If a downturn hits, operators can't cut rates fast because they have fixed debt service; they'd face forced sales or defaults. Second, policy intervention accelerates: if Washington state's rent-cap model spreads to other states and federal regulators tighten land-lease oversight, margins compress and the investment case collapses. Third, climate risk is becoming real—Hurricane Helene and Guadalupe River flooding exposed uninsured waterfront campgrounds and forced safety code tightening in Texas, a leading RV park state; catastrophic loss could hit leverage and insurance costs across the sector.

In one sentence

Owning the land in a nutshell

As office towers empty and traditional housing becomes unaffordable, capital quietly rotates into land leasing, RV parks, and manufactured housing—where occupancy stays near 95% and pricing power grows because supply is stuck.
