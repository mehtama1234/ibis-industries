C · Hospitality & retail property

Hotels as Real Estate

The lodging market bifurcates into high-margin luxury and extended-stay while economy motels collapse as chains financialize the category as an asset class.

The US hotel industry is stuck. Market size inched from $258.1 billion in 2022 to $286.5 billion in 2025, but that 0.5% growth masks a story of radical bifurcation: luxury boutiques pulled 6% revenue gains while economy properties contracted, extended-stay hotels captured supply growth, and Sonder's November 2025 bankruptcy shocked the market. Higher rates broke the capital math for conversion plays and independent operators, and labor scarcity made skeleton crews the new normal.

What changed

Higher interest rates killed the conversion-play economics that had kept the hotel pipeline full. For years, investors could acquire distressed motels, pour capex into a refresh, and refinance at a spread over treasuries. By 2025, that math inverted: debt service on empty properties became toxic, maturity walls hit Q4 2025 through Q1 2026, and Revo Hospitality Group's 260 properties landed in Chapter 11. Sonder, the Marriott-backed boutique tech startup, collapsed in November 2025 after the licensing deal severed, stranded guests mid-stay, and left its asset base in limbo. The refinancing crisis was real: hotels that could not roll over loans were forced to sell, compress capex, or file for restructuring.

Simultaneously, the labor cost wall inverted the margin ladder. Wages for hotel staff rose 25.6% versus 2019, and labor cost per occupied room jumped 21% from Q4 2024 to Q4 2025, hitting $54.98 by year-end. Operating costs exploded too: insurance premiums up 100%, utilities up 30% from 2024 to 2025, while demand flatlined after the pandemic recovery bounce. Large chains could absorb these hits by running skeleton crews with 70-80% annual turnover and automating guest-facing functions. Independent operators and smaller chains could not. By 2026, boutique hotels with tighter unit economics faced compression of average margins from 18.4% in 2016 down to 15.1% in 2026, making refinancing harder and forcing consolidation.

What made lodging newly valuable was scale and distribution, not the property itself. Luxury boutique hotels achieved average daily rates of $452 in 2025 with RevPAR of $307, well above the industry-wide luxury average of $263, because they could command premium pricing from high-frequency travelers and corporate accounts. Extended-stay hotels grew supply by 4% in 2025, the largest increase in four years, occupying 12.4% above industry-average occupancy because they served project-based employment and hybrid work relocations, not one-off travelers. Casino hotels bifurcated by destination: premium Las Vegas resorts remained resilient with higher-value customers per room, while mid-tier regional casinos faced slower traffic and price competition, reflecting geographic pricing power. What made rooms cheap was economy lodging: traditional midscale and budget properties faced contraction because their occupancy could not sustain their debt loads at 2025 borrowing costs.

The real money rotated to asset financialization. IHG opened a record 443 hotels in Q1 2026, passing 7,000 operating hotels with 1.03 million rooms, and signed 694 additional properties, converting franchising into a real-estate brokerage. Hyatt acquired Playa Hotels & Resorts for $2.6 billion in 2025, and Blackstone purchased Hamilton Island for $1.2 billion, treating resort properties as collateral-backed income streams rather than hospitality operations. Fertitta Entertainment offered $7 billion for Caesars Entertainment (a $31.5 billion deal including debt) expected to close late 2026 or early 2027, consolidating casino properties into a mega-operator with 60 casinos. VICI Properties acquired Golden Portfolio properties (7 casinos) in April 2026 for $1.15 billion and Gamehost assets in Canada for $141 million in June 2026, positioning itself as a landlord to casino operators rather than an operator itself. Hilton launched its Outset Collection with 60+ properties in pipeline, targeting independent owners seeking global distribution without losing brand autonomy. In essence, the property became the collateral, the franchise became the moat, and the occupancy rate became the bond yield.

The evidence

  • Luxury boutique hotels: 53.5% of boutique market revenue in 2025 with 70-75% occupancy and RevPAR climbing 5.3%, while economy segments declined (2025)
  • Extended-stay segment: Grew 4% in supply during 2025, driven by project-based employment and hybrid work patterns, occupying 12.4% above industry average (2025)
  • Labor crisis: Wages rose 25.6% versus 2019, turnover 70-80% annually, with cost per occupied room up 21% from Q4 2024 to Q4 2025 (2025)
  • Casino hotels: Labor cost per occupied room jumped 12.8% in 2025, compressing margins from 12.2% baseline; premium Las Vegas resorts resilient, mid-tier regional casinos face price competition (2025-2026)
  • Consolidation wave: Hyatt acquired Playa Hotels & Resorts for $2.6B (2025); Fertitta offered $7B for Caesars Entertainment ($31.5B including debt, expected close late 2026); IHG opened record 443 hotels Q1 2026, signed 694 additional properties (2025-2026)

Who rides it

Large chains and asset financiers win. IHG, Hilton, and Marriott capture franchise fees and brand licensing without owning physical properties, turning landlords and independent operators into renters of their systems. Blackstone, VICI Properties, and other pension-backed real-estate players acquire distressed assets at discounts, hold them as collateral-backed income streams, and lease them to branded operators, earning spread and optionality. Luxury boutique properties and extended-stay conversions win because their occupancy and RevPAR justify the debt load: boutiques achieved $452 average daily rates in 2025, extended-stay captured 12.4% above industry occupancy, and their guests pay premium rates or sign longer leases. Digital distribution platforms win too: metasearch and Google Hotel Ads commanded $8.2 billion in annual spend in 2025, bypassing traditional OTA channels and giving chains direct customer data.

Who's squeezed

Independent hotel owners and economy-segment operators lose hard. Sonder's November 2025 collapse and Revo's January 2026 Chapter 11 bankruptcy forced asset sales into an unfriendly debt market, and survivors faced margin compression from 18.4% in 2016 to 15.1% in 2026. Economy and traditional midscale properties contracted throughout 2025 because their occupancy could not justify their debt service at higher rates and their labor costs could not be absorbed by lower RevPAR. Mid-tier regional casinos lost pricing power and foot traffic, forced to compete on discounts rather than experience. Guests in stranded Sonder locations lost access mid-stay. Workers in hospitality faced the highest wage gains (25.6% since 2019) but also the highest turnover (70-80% annually), suggesting jobs became temporary and unstable despite raises.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is a debt spiral among the remaining independent and mid-tier operators. Maturity walls peaked Q4 2025 through Q1 2026, meaning a wave of loans reached renewal exactly when refinancing rates remained high and occupancy had plateaued. Hotels that cannot roll over debt at acceptable rates will be forced into emergency sales, flooding the market with distressed inventory and crushing pricing for those still solvent. Regional casinos face regulatory fragmentation: Illinois, Indiana, Hawaii, Maine, and others are legalizing online casinos and micro-betting, which will siphon players from land-based resorts and require operators to choose between maintaining price and defending volume. If online expansion accelerates, mid-tier regional casinos could face a 10-15% revenue loss within three years, triggering another wave of bankruptcies and asset fire-sales.

In one sentence

Hotels as real estate in a nutshell

Higher rates and labor scarcity bifurcated the hotel market into high-margin luxury and extended-stay winners and contracting economy properties, while large chains and asset financiers consolidated control by franchising brands and acquiring distressed real estate as collateral.
