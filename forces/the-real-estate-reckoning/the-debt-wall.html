D · The tensions

The commercial-debt wall

When $2.8 trillion in maturing loans hit refinancing at higher rates, the math breaks.

The commercial real estate sector is running into a wall. Not the office crisis, though that's real and getting worse—the problem is simpler and more mechanical. Billions of dollars in debt is maturing, and the math that made sense at 4.3% does not work at 6.2%. Banks are getting squeezed, borrowers are getting squeezed, and the reckoning is happening right now.

What changed

The Federal Reserve cut rates sharply from September 2024 through the end of 2025—a total of 1.75 percentage points—to ease pressure on banks and borrowers after the 2023 banking crisis took down SVB, Signature, and First Republic. But that relief was temporary and one-directional. The real problem sits in the debt that was issued years earlier, when rates were lower and office buildings still looked like safe bets. Between 2015 and 2022, commercial real estate borrowers locked in loans at rates around 4.3%. Now those loans are maturing. In 2025 alone, $957 billion in CRE loans came due for refinancing. Through 2028, $2.8 trillion more will mature. Today's rates for new CRE financing sit at 6.2%. The spread—190 basis points—is not a rounding error. For a $100 million office building loan, that's $1.9 million more per year in interest expense. Scale that to a portfolio or a whole asset class, and the math breaks.

The refinancing wave exposes a deeper structural problem. Office properties refinancing at 6.2% often cannot generate enough rental income to service the debt at the new rate. Commercial Mortgage-Backed Securities (CMBS) delinquencies hit 7.29% in 2025—the highest distress signal in years. Chapter 11 bankruptcies in the real estate sector hit a 10-year high in 2025, and corporate bankruptcies broadly reached 694 cases, the highest count in 14 years. The market knew this was coming, but knowing and absorbing it are different things. Banks are holding $2.7 trillion in CRE exposure on their balance sheets, concentrated in regional and community banks that lack the diversification of the mega-banks. When office tenancy fails and cap rates reset, the collateral value drops. A building worth $60 million based on rents six years ago might be worth $35 million based on what rents and interest rates say today. That gap is where losses live.

The Fed's rate cuts from mid-2024 into 2025 actually compressed banks' net interest margins even as they recovered slightly to 3.39% by Q4 2025—the highest since 2019—because deposit costs were sticky and slow to fall. Meanwhile, competition for deposits intensified. Uninsured deposits surged $214.7 billion in Q4 2025 alone, a sign that corporate clients and sophisticated investors were chasing yield and moving money to banks offering better rates. Banks needed higher yields on their loan books to offset deposit-cost pressure and compensate for credit losses on troubled CRE. But borrowers already stressed by rate resets cannot bear higher yields. The two sides are being crushed in the same vice: refinancing rates that destroy deal economics, and deposit competition that erodes the interest-rate cushion banks have left.

The real twist is that industrial and multifamily—the strongest real estate segments—are actually absorbing losses from office. Industrial had a banner 2025 with 940 million square feet of leasing, the second-best year on record, fueled by e-commerce tenants accounting for 56% of retail sales growth and needing 50 to 70 million square feet of warehouse space for every 1% rise in online penetration. Multifamily absorbed 124,600 units in Q2 2026 alone and drove 39.5% year-over-year growth in investor transaction volume, powered by a 3.4-million-unit housing shortage and a 105% premium to buy rather than rent. But both segments are refinancing into the same higher-rate environment. The difference is they earn enough to service the debt. Offices do not. This is creating a bifurcated market where strong asset classes are paying more to fund weak ones, and the debt wall is forcing a capital reallocation that looks less like recovery and more like triage.

Who rides it

Industrial landlords and multifamily operators are riding this force because their tenants have pricing power and lease growth resilience. Industrial absorbed 940 million square feet in 2025, the second-best year on record, because e-commerce tenants need 50 to 70 million square feet of warehouse space for every 1% increase in online penetration—a structural requirement they will pay for. Multifamily absorbed 124,600 units in Q2 2026 and drove 39.5% year-over-year growth in investor transaction volume, lifted by a 3.4-million-unit housing shortage and a 105% premium to buy versus rent. Both asset classes can refinance at 6.2% and still maintain positive cash flow, which means they can absorb rate increases while office buildings cannot. Real estate investors with logistics and residential portfolios are consolidating and buying at distressed valuations from borrowers forced to sell. Banks lending to these strong segments are actually more profitable because yield is higher and credit losses are lower.

Who's squeezed

Office building owners, especially those holding Class B and C properties, are getting crushed. The sector sits in structural crisis: office vacancy peaked at 19.6% in Q1 2025 and has only begun to decline as 127 million square feet was absorbed, but the underlying demand is gone. Refinancing that building at 6.2% often destroys the debt service coverage ratio. Owners are forced to refinance into the same higher-rate environment, accept losses on resale, or default. Community and regional banks holding concentrated CRE portfolios face margin compression from deposit competition and credit losses from office defaults. The $2.7 trillion in CRE exposure on bank balance sheets is not diversified enough in smaller banks to absorb losses on the weak segment while rates remain higher. Developers and building managers face obsolescence: office-to-apartment conversions reached 90,300 units in 2026, a 28% year-over-year jump, signaling a wholesale rewrite of the use case for millions of square feet that will never be profitable offices again.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is a cascading default spiral in office and secondary properties. When one major owner defaults on a $500 million or $1 billion property, it forces a mark-to-market loss on comparable collateral across the banking system, which triggers more defaults and more mark-downs in a feedback loop. The $2.7 trillion in CRE exposure sits concentrated in smaller banks that do not have the capital buffers or diversification to absorb a 10% or 20% loss on their portfolio. A wave of bank failures in 2026 or 2027, timed to coincide with the peak refinancing wall, could trigger a credit crunch that starves even strong borrowers (industrial, multifamily) of capital to expand or refinance. The broader economy would suffer not because the real estate problem is new, but because the debt maturity cliff is concentrated in time and overlaps with rising labor costs and energy prices.
