The fields: Who grows and processes the food

Immigration enforcement is now the bottleneck limiting which crops get harvested at all.

American agriculture runs on immigrant labor—and border enforcement has become an existential input risk. Droughts hit crops, tariffs hit prices, but labor scarcity is what actually leaves fruit rotting in fields. Farms lose 10-20% of harvestable crops when workers don't show up; pollination fees double in a decade; and growers can't replace that loss with wages alone.

What changed

Immigration enforcement—explicit policy, daily deportations, visa delays, employer checks—has tightened labor supply in farm work fast enough to reshape which crops get grown and where. Between 2024 and 2025, fruit growers reported labor-scarcity losses jumped from 33% of operations down 10% of crop to 65% losing between 5-10% of harvestable crops. That's a doubling of scarcity impact in a single year. Specialty crops like berries and stone fruit now spend 30% of operating cost on hand-harvesting alone, and when workers aren't available, 21% of growers saw profitability cut directly by wage pressure to attract remaining labor. The farms that survive are consolidating around capital-intensive, low-labor crops (almonds, walnuts) and increasingly requiring visa labor—41% of growers now use H-2A visa workers as a fixed input cost rather than a variable wage.

Tariffs and drought are real, but they're not new to farming. What changed in 2025 is that labor became scarce enough to limit production directly. Almond pollination costs hit $209 per hive in 2025, up 15% from 2024, because a 62% honeybee loss required shippers to truck in 2 million hives from across the continent just to meet almond acreage—and beekeeper fees follow scarcity. Almond growers planted only 105,000 new non-bearing acres in 2025, well below historical norms, because expanding capacity requires confident labor supply. California almonds alone consume 3.2 million acre-feet of water annually—half of Los Angeles' household water—and the drought forced 80% of almond production to face restrictions in 2022-2025. But the real ceiling on expansion is labor, not water.

Agricultural employment across the entire US sector sits at 3.5 million workers. Labor-intensive crops—berries, stone fruit, lettuce, tomatoes, grapes—rely overwhelmingly on immigrant labor, documented and otherwise. When deportation risk rises or visa caps tighten, growers lose a percentage of their workforce immediately. They can't hire locally at any wage that keeps them profitable; rural American workers don't queue for seasonal harvest work at any price point that survives the commodity margin. So the sector has two choices: shift to crops that need fewer hands (like tree nuts, which mechanize better), or invest in harvest automation. A quarter of fruit growers are now banking on robotics—but those systems are still years from replacing hand-picking berries or cutting specialty crops.

The immediate result is a bifurcated agriculture market. The broad commodity crops (corn, soybeans, wheat) weather tariffs via scale and government support. The broad commodity animal products (beef especially) face structural headwinds—the US beef herd collapsed to 94.2 million head, lowest since the 1950s—but scarcity creates price support. It's the middle that squeezed: specialty fruits and vegetables that require hand labor, moderate capital, and thin margins. These crops get abandoned, consolidated into fewer hands, or offshored. Imports of berries, stone fruit, and tomatoes rise as domestic growers shrink.

The evidence

  • Fruit & Nut Farming: 65% of growers lost 5-10% of harvestable crops to labor scarcity in 2025, vs. 33% in 2024—a doubling in one year (2025)
  • Agriculture sector-wide: 96% of growers saw labor costs rise or remain flat in 2025; 41% faced significant increases; labor spending hit $53B (2025)
  • Almonds (California): Pollination fees reached $209/hive in 2025 (+15% YoY); 62% honeybee loss required trucking 2M hives from across the continent (2025)
  • Fruit & Nut Farming: 41% of growers adopted H-2A visa labor as fixed input; specialty crops now spend 30% of operating cost on hand-harvesting (2025)
  • US Agriculture: 3.5M employees; labor-intensive crops losing 15-20% domestic production capacity by mid-2026 without immigration relief; 105,000 new almond acres planted in 2025 vs. historical norms (2025-2026)

Who rides it

Consolidated commodity growers—large-scale almond, walnut, and tree-nut operations that mechanize well and can afford visa labor as a line item—lock in higher margins via scarcity. Beef producers benefit from structural herd collapse (94.2M head, the lowest since the 1950s) that tightens supply and supports prices. Food importers and offshore growers capture share of berries, stone fruit, and lettuce as domestic capacity shrinks. Equipment and automation vendors selling harvest robotics see growing investment from growers betting on machines to replace workers.

Who's squeezed

Hand-labor specialty crops (berries, stone fruit, tomatoes, peppers, lettuce) get abandoned or consolidated by larger operators who can absorb visa costs. Mid-sized farms with 100-500 acres of mixed fruit lose margins as labor costs rise and scarcity forces them to fallow land. Rural workers who might have done seasonal harvest work face permanent closure of that option—employers shift to visa labor and automation rather than compete for seasonal wages. Consumers in the US see higher prices on fresh fruit and vegetables as domestic production shrinks and imports rise.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Immigration enforcement is now a production bottleneck, not a wage negotiator. If deportations accelerate or H-2A visa allocations shrink further, 15-20% of domestic production capacity in labor-intensive crops vanishes by mid-2026—not because crops fail, but because there's no one to pick them. This creates both immediate food-supply risk (imports surge, prices spike) and permanent capacity loss (growers exit, land gets repurposed, replanting takes years). The system has no fast recovery path if policy changes rapidly.

In one sentence

The fields in a nutshell

Immigration enforcement has become the binding constraint on US farm labor, making border policy decisions directly control which crops get harvested and at what price.
