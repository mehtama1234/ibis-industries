A · Who grows and processes the food

The processing plants

Meat and dairy plants run on the same labor pool — immigration enforcement turned supply-chain fragility into survival math.

Chicken plants, beef plants, and dairy facilities don't usually compete for the same workers. But they hire from the same pool—immigrants, often undocumented, willing to do work Americans consistently reject. When immigration policy tightens, the bottleneck hits all three at once. Processors can raise wages (and they have, by 71% in 2024), but they can't force Americans to debone chickens or eviscerate cattle. So they're automating instead—and the plants that move fastest survive.

What changed

Meat processing in the US is a $387.7 billion industry (2026) with 539,000 workers spread across 6,587 plants. In normal times, it runs on thin margins (5.1% in 2026) and commodity pricing. But in 2025–2026, two separate shocks hit the labor supply at the same time. First, avian influenza devastated poultry. H5N1 swept through 868 commercial flocks across all 50 states, culling 169 million birds by May 2025 alone and costing $1.4 billion in losses. When bird populations collapsed, so did the work—fewer birds meant fewer jobs in deboning, packaging, and shipping. Normally this would relieve labor pressure. Instead, it reversed. As supply tightened, chicken prices spiked to $2.09 per pound in mid-2025 (a record), which meant processors needed to run harder, faster, with even fewer workers. Then immigration enforcement stepped in. The Trump administration's border actions made undocumented workers far riskier to hire. Plant managers faced a choice: automate or lose production capacity. They chose automation. Cobots (collaborative robots) now handle de-hoofing, evisceration, and deboning. AI vision systems inspect carcasses and trim defects. VR-operated stations let technicians work from remote locations. The global automation-robotics market for food processing is projected to hit $4.5 billion by 2033 (9.2% CAGR), and meat plants are driving that growth.

Beef specifically shows how tight the squeeze became. In 2026, beef cattle prices are forecast to average $240 per hundredweight, up 7% from 2025—driven by tight cattle supplies and strong demand. Yet processors can't hire workers fast enough to move that beef. The poultry sector tried to hold the line with wages. Pilgrim's Pride, Tyson, Perdue, and others agreed in late 2024 and early 2025 to pay out $250 million+ to resolve a wage-fixing conspiracy case, acknowledging they'd underpaid agriculture workers for years. Tyson increased pay by 11% in 2024; Pilgrim's Pride promised similar hikes. But wage gains alone didn't fill the jobs. Why? Because the work—fourteen-hour shifts in 40-degree coolers, trimming muscle off bone at 300 birds per hour, evisceration lines where a single cut can lacerate a hand—is something Americans, even poor Americans, turn down. Turnover in poultry processing hit 58% in 90 days in 2025, meaning plants lost more than half their workforce every quarter. That kind of turnover destroys productivity and quality. So processors invested heavily in cobots and AI. Tyson, JBS, and National Beef all announced major automation rollouts through 2025 and 2026. Cargill's turkey facility in Springdale, Arkansas shifted toward prepared foods (higher margin, lower labor intensity) rather than raw-bird processing. Pilgrim's Pride broke ground on a new prepared-foods facility in Georgia in fall 2025, due in 2027.

Dairy hit a different crisis. Milk prices collapsed—all-milk prices fell from a 2022 high to $21.05 per hundredweight in 2025, and are forecast at just $19.25 per cwt for 2026. This 46% deflation crushed producer margins. Dairy farm bankruptcies surged 46% year-over-year in 2025, with 315 Chapter 12 filings nationally and 185 in the top 24 dairy states alone. Wisconsin, the nation's number-two dairy state, has lost 76% of its herds over recent decades; the decline accelerated in 2025–2026. Yet even as farms shuttered, the remaining dairy plants still needed workers. Robotic milking systems (which do exist—they dominate in Denmark and the Netherlands, covering 20–25% of herds) cover only ~5% of US dairy farms. The gap meant manual labor remained essential. The US shed 155,000 agricultural workers between March and July 2025 alone, many of them in dairy-heavy regions. Meanwhile, tariff chaos erupted: in August 2026, the Trump administration imposed 50% duties on select Canadian dairy imports (whey, powders, casein), while exempting cheese and fluid milk. This fractured the market. Cheese demand stayed resilient (24.5% market share), but plant-based alternatives exploded: they grew 13.8% CAGR and captured 62.8% of the organic segment by 2026. The upshot was consolidation. Leprino Foods closed its old California facilities (Lemoore and Tracy) and opened a high-capacity plant in Lubbock, Texas—a move that consolidated production into fewer, larger plants with deeper capital for automation.

The evidence

  • Poultry: 868 commercial flocks infected with H5N1 across all 50 states (2024–2025); chicken prices spiked to $2.09/lb in mid-2025, then fell to 63 cents/lb by early 2026
  • Meat processing: $387.7B market (2026), 539,000 workers; beef cattle prices forecast $240/cwt in 2026 (up 7% from 2025)
  • Labor crisis: 58% poultry-processing worker turnover in 90 days (2025); processors raised pay 71% in 2024 but still cannot fill positions
  • Dairy: milk prices collapsed to $19.25/cwt forecast for 2026; farm bankruptcies surged 46% in 2025 (315 Chapter 12 filings nationally); robotic milking covers only ~5% of US dairy farms
  • Tariffs: August 2026 imposed 50% duties on Canadian dairy imports (whey, powders, casein); China locked out US beef in 2025 (96% decline to 556 mt in September 2025)

Who rides it

Large processors with capital for automation—Tyson, JBS, Cargill, National Beef—move faster than competitors. They invest in cobots, AI vision, and remote-operated stations. They also consolidate, buying up smaller competitors or reshaping operations toward higher-margin prepared foods. Leprino Foods built a high-capacity cheese plant in Texas. Tyson acquired Cargill's turkey facility. Pilgrim's Pride opened a new prepared-foods facility. These moves let them absorb higher input costs, shrink labor dependency, and survive tariff shocks. Workers who operate, maintain, and program automation equipment also win—they earn far more than line workers and are in shorter supply.

Who's squeezed

Small and mid-sized poultry farms, unable to absorb H5N1 losses or automation capital, lose contracts or go under. Small beef processors can't afford cobots or AI vision systems, so they either sell out to the Big Four or shut down. Dairy farms get squeezed hardest: 315 bankruptcies in 2025 alone. Manual line workers lose shifts, hours, and income as cobots roll in. Manual milkers and evisceration crews see their jobs shrink. Undocumented workers, who once had informal access to these jobs, now face far higher enforcement risk, locking them out of the work entirely. Consumers pay higher prices as tariffs arrive (50% on Canadian dairy powders) and export markets tighten (China's beef lockout through mid-2026).

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that automation speed varies by firm and region, creating a two-tier system. Large, well-capitalized processors automate away labor dependency fast. Smaller plants stay labor-dependent and vulnerable to enforcement raids, tariff shocks, and disease. This accelerates consolidation and reduces competition further, potentially handing the Big Four even more pricing power. Meanwhile, automation technology is still imperfect—AI vision systems sometimes miss defects; cobots jam or misalign. If automation fails to keep pace with labor scarcity, production will gap, prices will spike again (as happened mid-2025 with chicken), and consumers bear the cost. That's a fragile equilibrium: prices high enough to fund automation, but low enough to keep demand from cratering.

In one sentence

The processing plants in a nutshell

A $540 billion meat and dairy processing sector now races to automate away labor it can no longer fill, even as immigration enforcement locks down the supply.
