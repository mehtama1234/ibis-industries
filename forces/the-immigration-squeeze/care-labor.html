The care labor

Who cares and who's detained.

America's elderly are living longer and choosing to age at home. That's the good news. The bad news: the care system that makes it possible is now breaking under two colliding pressures. Medicare payments are shrinking even as demand explodes, and the workers doing the actual care—wiping, lifting, comforting—are vanishing because wages stay flat and the work stays grueling. Immigration policy has quietly become healthcare policy.

What changed

Home care is no longer a small business problem. It's now a $191.6 billion industry in 2026, and it's growing at 6.7% annually (2021-2026 CAGR). The aging population has created an avalanche of new patients who prefer care at home rather than nursing homes. Demand for home health aides alone is projected to grow 17% between 2024 and 2034. Sounds good—except the system is imploding in real time.

Start with the money squeeze. Medicare has cut home care provider payments for four straight years. In 2026, the latest cut was 1.3%—a $220 million aggregate reduction that came after CMS originally proposed slashing 6.4% in the rule published November 2025. These aren't abstract accounting adjustments. Agencies report that 34% now worry they can't stay profitable at all. Nearly a third of the country's 437,000 home care establishments operate at near-zero or negative margins. The strong performers target just 15-20% profit margins, which is thin for any service business. Payment cuts are driving closures in rural and underserved areas—exactly where older Americans without urban family support need care most.

Now add the staffing catastrophe. Ninety-five percent of home care agencies report moderate-to-severe staffing shortages. Some agencies are turning away up to 25% of patient requests because they literally cannot find workers. Turnover in the industry runs at 75% annually. That means caregiver is a job people take for a few months, then flee. Wages rose from $13.07 per hour in 2014 to $16.77 in 2024, but that gain is eaten by inflation and the relentless nature of the work. Ten years of raises and the job still can't keep people. Other healthcare roles pay better and demand less of your body and your patience.

The result: shortage has become structural. The industry needs caregivers it simply cannot find at wages it can afford to pay. Agencies turn away one out of every four patient requests. Elderly people who want to stay home instead end up hospitalized longer or moving into institutions. Families scramble. The demand is there, the need is there, the money is there—but the workers are not. This is where immigration enters, quietly and decisively. Immigration enforcement tightens labor supply in an industry that already can't fill positions. Policy and borders now determine staffing far more than wages do.

The evidence

  • Home care market reached $191.6 billion in 2026 with 6.7% annual growth 2021-2026, driven by aging population preference for in-home care over institutional settings.
  • 95% of the 437,000+ home care establishments report moderate-to-severe staffing shortages, with some turning away up to 25% of patient requests.
  • Turnover in home care runs at 75% annually despite caregiver wages rising from $13.07/hour in 2014 to $16.77 in 2024, indicating work conditions are the binding constraint.
  • Medicare payment reductions hit home care for four consecutive years, with CMS finalizing a 1.3% cut ($220 million aggregate) in the November 2025 rule for 2026.
  • Private equity consolidation accelerated in 2026, with 55 home health and hospice deals in H1 2026 alone (41% PE-backed), including General Atlantic's $3B acquisition of TEAM Services Group (Q2 2026) and Kinderhook's $1.1B take-private of Enhabit (Q1 2026).

Who rides it

Private equity and larger consolidated operators capture scale. General Atlantic's $3 billion acquisition of TEAM Services Group in Q2 2026 was the largest home care deal on record. Bigger platforms can absorb payment cuts, invest in technology (cloud EMRs, remote monitoring becoming mainstream), and shift risk onto smaller competitors through quality scorecards. Consolidation has already grabbed 41% of deal volume in the first half of 2026. For those who can automate or partner with health systems, margin compression is survivable. For fragmented, rural operators and solo agencies, it is not.

Who's squeezed

Smaller agencies, rural providers, elderly patients, and families without resources. Rural and underserved areas are losing agencies to closures as payment cuts bite. Patients are being turned away—agencies rejecting up to 25% of requests. Families who hoped Mom could stay home now scramble to find care or move her into a facility. And the caregivers themselves remain trapped in a low-wage, high-turnover job with no clear escape even as demand for their labor surges. The 75% annual turnover tells the true story: workers would rather do almost anything else.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The entire system could bifurcate. Medicare payment cuts are structural, not cyclical—they reflect budget pressure and value-based care models shifting risk. Workforce supply is now set by immigration policy, which has tightened dramatically. Combine lower payment with tighter borders and you get: large platforms serving affluent urban patients who can pay private rates; rural and economically mixed areas losing coverage; elderly people who can't afford private pay aging in understaffed facilities or in hospital beds (more expensive, worse outcomes); and a caregiver shortage that becomes permanent because no policy lever can raise pay fast enough if borders stay closed. The system stops functioning at scale.

In one sentence

The care labor in a nutshell

Home care demand is surging as America ages, but Medicare cuts and immigration enforcement have made caregivers scarce and affordable care nearly impossible in rural areas.
