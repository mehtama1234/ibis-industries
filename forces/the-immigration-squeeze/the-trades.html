The Building Trades

Construction trades run on immigrant labor—so when borders tighten, entire industries can't find workers no matter what they pay.

Masonry, roofing, and drywall work are the skeleton of every building in America. Yet three-fifths of those trades are staffed by immigrants or workers whose families came here one or two generations ago. When immigration policy shifts, it doesn't just move people—it makes whole industries, collectively worth over $200 billion a year, suddenly unable to find enough hands to do the work at all.

What changed

The building trades have always run lean on labor, relying on a steady stream of workers willing to do physically demanding, often seasonal work. In masonry alone, the industry needs roughly 20,700 replacement workers every year just to keep the current workforce stable as people retire and move on. But in 2025-2026, that dependable pipeline narrowed sharply. Drywall and insulation installers are 34% immigrant workers in the broader construction sector but exceed 60% in the drywall trades specifically. Roofing is similarly dependent. So when enforcement activity tightens—or when the rhetoric around immigration shifts and workers become uncertain about their legal footing—contractors face something wages alone cannot fix: a sudden shortfall in available labor.

The squeeze showed up first as a wage signal, then as real shortage. Labor costs jumped 10-30% for most roofing firms in 2025. Drywall installers now charge $1.50-$3.50 per square foot for labor, up sharply from years past. But wages only work if people are willing to take the job. By mid-2026, over 306,000 construction positions sat unfilled nationwide, and drywall and masonry roles were among the hardest to fill. The trades need roughly 499,000 new workers in drywall alone by 2026 just to meet current demand. Yet the apprenticeship pipeline is starved—training takes 5-7 years, and nearly 40% of skilled construction workers are already over 45. New US workers simply cannot be trained fast enough to replace outflow.

Tariffs compressed margins at exactly the moment labor got scarce, creating a double bind. In June 2025, US tariffs on steel and aluminum doubled from 25% to 50%, which hit metal roofing prices hardest—jumping roughly 60%—but rippled through the entire sector. General roofing material costs jumped 15-25% in 2026, pushing the national average roof replacement bill over $20,500. Masonry materials took hits too: Mexico-sourced ceramic masonry goods jumped from duty-free to 25% in 2025, while Italy-sourced structural masonry faced 10% Section 122 duties. Drywall contractors faced 25% tariffs on cabinets and vanities starting October 2025. Contractors already operating on razor-thin 5-7% profit margins could not pass all these costs forward—customers already struggling with mortgage rates at 6%+ and commercial vacancy at 20.5% had no more room in their budgets. Roofing firms filed for bankruptcy: Ready Roofing in June 2026, Ribbit Roofing in May 2026, Snohomish Roofing in March 2026, Certified Roofing Solutions in July 2026. The trades that had always solved labor constraints through the next wave of immigrants now faced a trifecta: borders tightening, material costs spiking, and demand actually softening from housing stalls.

In response, the industry accelerated consolidation and automation in parallel tracks. Subcontractor M&A activity in masonry jumped 38.6% year-over-year in mid-2025 (366 transactions), as larger firms acquired smaller shops specifically to quickly gain access to skilled crews. Larger roofing and drywall distributors consolidated aggressively: QXO acquired Beacon Roofing Supply for roughly $11 billion in May 2025; Home Depot had previously acquired SRS Distribution for $18.25 billion in June 2024. M&A in building products and construction surged to 852 closed deals in Q2 2026, up 22% from the prior year. Simultaneously, technology adoption accelerated. AI use among roofing contractors jumped from 29% to 40% year-over-year. Masonry began rolling out robotic bricklayers that can place hundreds of bricks per hour with precision, and contractors started assembling prefabricated masonry panels in factory settings to reduce on-site labor requirements. These moves were capital-intensive and still in early stages—but they represented an industry-wide shift toward automating tasks that could no longer reliably be staffed by human workers.

The evidence

  • Masonry: $40.0 billion market in 2026, down 1.6% in 2025 with 146,100 workers needing 20,700 annual replacements to stay level, yet tariffs on Mexico-sourced ceramic goods (25%) and Italy-sourced structural masonry (10%) compressed 5.1% margins further.
  • Roofing contractors: $92.5 billion market, 62% reporting difficulty filling skilled roles; labor costs jumped 10-30% in 2025 while material costs spiked 15-25% after June 2025 tariff doubling (steel/aluminum 25% to 50%), with four major bankruptcies filed in 2026 (Ready, Ribbit, Snohomish, Certified).
  • Drywall and insulation installers: $81.9 billion market, 404,000 workers, need 499,000 new by 2026; 60%+ of drywall trade is immigrant-staffed yet 306,000+ construction positions unfilled by mid-2026 cannot be filled domestically.
  • Consolidation surge: Masonry M&A up 38.6% YoY (366 deals), roofing distributor mega-deals (QXO+Beacon ~$11B, Home Depot+SRS $18.25B), building products M&A 852 deals in Q2 2026 (+22% YoY) as firms buy crews instead of recruiting.
  • Automation acceleration: Masonry robotic bricklayers placing hundreds per hour, prefabricated panels in factories; roofing AI adoption jumped 29% to 40% YoY; technology adoption driven by inability to staff at any wage level as immigration constraints bite.

Who rides it

Large, well-capitalized firms that can afford M&A to acquire smaller contractors' crews, distribute across geography to avoid local labor-policy impacts, and invest in automation and prefabrication. Distributors like QXO (post-Beacon acquisition) and Home Depot gain pricing power in a consolidating market. Manufacturers of robotic systems and prefab components benefit as contractors trade labor for capital. States with immigration-friendly policy or older housing stocks with high repair demand (extreme weather, aging roofs) see demand hold up; contractors in those regions can still price aggressively because material and labor are constrained nationwide.

Who's squeezed

Small and mid-sized contractors, especially those without national scale or cash for acquisition and automation capex, get squeezed out of the market. Homeowners and building owners face much higher renovation and maintenance bills as labor scarcity and tariffs push costs up 15-30% in roofing, 10%+ in masonry and drywall. Residential construction slows further as affordability worsens—already facing 6%+ mortgage rates, the need to hire costlier labor makes new projects even less pencil out. Commercial real estate developers face the same squeeze on retrofits and repairs amid an already-difficult leasing environment (20.5% office vacancy). Workers themselves—including immigrants and immigrant-descended workers—face wages that have risen nominally (10-30%) but are increasingly guarded behind immigration status uncertainty and rising living costs in tight housing markets.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that automation accelerates faster than anyone expects, permanently hollowing out the skill base for these trades. If robots and prefab panels become reliable and capital-efficient enough in the next 5-10 years, the industry won't rediscover a "normal" immigrant labor supply when policy inevitably loosens—the economic case for hiring humans will have evaporated. Simultaneously, if policy tightens faster than automation can ramp, there is a real risk of project delays, unfinished commercial and residential buildings, and cascading defaults in construction lending. The other silent risk is geographic arbitrage: high-regulation, high-wage states (California, New York) see contractors relocate to lower-cost, more immigration-friendly regions, leaving those states with aging infrastructure and no local capacity to maintain or upgrade it.

In one sentence

The building trades in a nutshell

The US construction trades, collectively worth over $200 billion annually and heavily reliant on immigrant workers, cannot fill 306,000+ open positions as immigration policy tightens the labor supply.
