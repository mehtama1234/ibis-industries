C · Who cares and who's detained

The enforcement complex

Detention and vetting are the other side of immigration policy—and they're reshaping entire business markets.

Immigration enforcement used to be small. Then detention exploded. The US now runs a $1 billion+ detention industry that's pulling in the two largest private prison operators and reshaping how background checks and compliance work. When the government decides to lock people up faster, the companies that manage them make money. When it decides to vet them faster, the companies that process paperwork do.

What changed

For decades, private prisons in the US were quietly dying. Incarceration had been falling 3.1% a year from 2020 to 2025—fewer prisoners meant fewer profits. Federal contracts dried up after Biden's 2021 executive order, and all the major Bureau of Prisons deals expired by 2023. That killed 17% of the industry's historical revenue. CoreCivic and The GEO Group, the two giants that dominate the market, faced a collapsing core business in a shrinking $5.9 billion sector that contracted at 3.2% a year from 2021 to 2026. Then Trump took office and reversed it. In January 2025, he rescinded Biden's ban on federal private prison contracts—not for regular prisoners, but for immigration detention. And the two companies pivoted hard. By Q2 2026, CoreCivic's total revenue had surged 27.3% year-over-year to $684.9 million, almost entirely from ICE detention. Their immigration detention revenue alone shot up 96% year-over-year to $261.3 million. The GEO Group reported record Q1 2026 earnings on ICE contract expansion, projecting $3.025 billion in 2026 revenue. In August 2026, CoreCivic won a $75 million annual contract for a 1,600-bed ICE detention facility in Minnesota alone. Immigration detention now represents roughly half of private prison revenue—a complete inversion of the business model in less than two years.

The real squeeze is that private detention facilities need staff, and that staff is expensive to hire and keep. Turnover in private prisons runs 52% a year—meaning half the workforce leaves every twelve months. That's five times higher than public prisons, which see 12-25% turnover. To fill those gaps at scale, contractors need steady hiring, which means they're always recruiting. But the labor shortage ripples everywhere: if you're posting jobs at ICE detention facilities in places like Minnesota, you're pulling from the same pool as hospitals, nursing homes, and manufacturing plants. This is where vetting comes in. The background check industry—$3.2 billion in 2026—has become a chokepoint. Every new hire needs screening. But the rules are fracturing. Ban-the-box laws now cover 40 states and 150+ cities; Texas joined in 2025, and Washington and Philadelphia tightened rules in 2026. Fair Chance to Compete for Jobs Act, cannabis screening bans, and a 30%+ spike in FCRA litigation through 2025 have forced screening firms to build compliance expertise just to operate. The industry grew only 1.0% in 2026 and actually contracted 0.1% annually from 2021-2026, trapped in a market where hiring cycles—not innovation—drive demand. Checkr and competitors now use AI to flag inconsistencies in real time, but the CFPB's questions in 2024 about whether AI adjudication itself violates fair credit rules created new legal risk. Consolidation accelerated: First Advantage acquired Sterling Check in October 2024 and expects $1.5-1.6 billion in 2025 revenue; HireRight and Bchex both bought ClearChecks in April 2025; Checkr acquired Truework in 2025. Vetting used to be a commodity. Now it's a regulatory gauntlet.

What got scarce, then, is the ability to move people through the system quickly—whether into the country or out of it. What got newly valuable is compliance expertise. Every company that detains people, screens workers, or verifies employment now faces overlapping federal rules, state bans, and litigation risk. The margin in detention stays tight at 9-10%, and the margin in background checks stayed flat at 5.4%, but the winners are firms that can navigate legal complexity while processing volume. CoreCivic and GEO can absorb regulatory cost because they operate at massive scale and have fixed contracts. A mid-market background check provider can't. That's why First Advantage, Checkr, and HireRight are all buying smaller competitors and consolidating—to bundle compliance, leverage AI, and split the legal bills. The enforcement complex doesn't just decide who gets detained. It decides who can be hired at all, and by whom.

The evidence

  • Private prisons: CoreCivic's ICE detention revenue jumped 96% YoY to $261.3M in Q2 2026; total revenue up 27.3% as immigration detention became roughly half the business.
  • Private prisons: GEO Group projects $3.025 billion in 2026 revenue on record Q1 2026 ICE contract expansion; the sector was contracting 3.2% annually before the pivot.
  • Private prisons: Staff turnover hits 52% annually in private facilities versus 12-25% in public prisons, driving chronic recruitment and training costs.
  • Background checks: US screening industry at $3.2 billion in 2026 grew only 1.0%, trapped by hiring-cycle volatility and regulatory compliance burden.
  • Background checks: Ban-the-box laws now cover 40 states and 150+ cities as of 2026; FCRA litigation spiked 30%+ through 2025, raising legal risk for AI-driven screening.

Who rides it

CoreCivic and The GEO Group turned a dying business model into a growth engine. Immigration detention contracts, once banned, now represent half their revenue. Compliance consultants and legal teams at screening firms also win—the regulatory maze is their specialty, and complexity is their moat. Tech companies selling AI-powered vetting tools, like Checkr, gain table-stakes advantage because speed and accuracy matter when the rules are constantly changing.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market background check providers and small-to-medium employers get squeezed. They can't afford compliance teams or AI systems. Ban-the-box rules and 40-state patchwork create constant headaches. Meanwhile, detention facility workers—guards, medical staff, intake processors—face 52% turnover because the work is hard, pay is modest, and margins are tight. The workers inside detention facilities, waiting for hearings or deportation, lose predictability. Faster detention processing means faster decisions, which can mean faster deportation. Healthcare providers and essential services also lose: when detention facilities hire aggressively to replace high turnover, they're fishing from the same labor pool as hospitals and care homes.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that detention and vetting become a self-reinforcing machine. As enforcement accelerates, more people flow through detention. Detention needs staff, so hiring ramps up. Hiring ramps up, so vetting demand increases. Vetting gets more complex because rules keep changing. Complex vetting slows hiring, which makes detention facilities more understaffed, which pushes for faster processing, which creates more enforcement pressure. The feedback loop tightens. And because detention is now half of CoreCivic and GEO's revenue, there's a financial incentive to keep detention volume high. The private prison industry lost its reason to exist a decade ago. Now it has a new one—enforcement—and that incentive is baked into quarterly earnings.

In one sentence

The enforcement complex in a nutshell

When detention exploded and compliance rules fractured, private prisons stopped dying and started thriving—but the real squeeze landed on workers trying to get hired and people waiting to be deported.
