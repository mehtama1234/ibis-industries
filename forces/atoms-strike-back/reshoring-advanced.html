Reshoring advanced manufacturing

Defense mandates and tariff uncertainty pull chip fabs, 3D printers, and electrical equipment back home—but at the cost of margin and speed.

Advanced manufacturing isn't coming home because it's profitable. It's coming home because tariffs, defense mandates, and supply-chain anxiety made offshoring too risky. Semiconductors, 3D printers, and electrical equipment—the infrastructure of an AI-soaked grid—are suddenly strategic. The companies that move fastest to build US capacity are locking in government contracts and customer loyalty. Everyone else is getting squeezed.

What changed

Semiconductors became a matter of national defense. In January 2026, the US and Taiwan signed a $250 billion direct-investment agreement to push Taiwanese chip makers into US fabs. Intel is now running 18A production at its Arizona Fab 52; TSMC and Samsung are building out US capacity on the back of $52.7 billion in CHIPS Act funding spread across 18 planned factories. This is not market economics—it's forced relocation with government backing. The payoff is that any company with a US fab now has guaranteed demand from defense contractors and data centers nervous about supply shocks. The cost is real: US semiconductor manufacturing is more expensive than Taiwan or South Korea, and margins are thinner.

Computer manufacturing illustrates the gap between what reshoring looks like in theory and practice. The overall market reached $22.3 billion in 2026, up 15.3% from 2025—but this masks a collapse in consumer PCs and a surge in AI servers. Consumer PC shipments dropped 7% in Q1 2026 to 15.8 million units as DRAM costs jumped 40–70% year-to-date, with another 60% increase forecast for Q1 2026. By contrast, Dell recorded $113.5 billion in revenue in FY2026 (ending January 30) with 40% growth in infrastructure solutions and a $43 billion backlog, all from AI servers. Chinese laptop exports to the US fell 47.6% year-over-year in April 2025 after tariff threats. The problem: building consumer PCs domestically is currently uneconomic. Building AI servers for hyperscalers is the only reshoring path that closes, because the margin is there and the government demand is certain.

Electrical equipment manufacturing—transformers, switchgear, motor controls, distribution systems—faces a different kind of pressure. The market hit $81.4 billion in 2026, up 7.8% from 2025, driven by grid modernization and data-center buildout. US utilities are spending $84.9 billion on transmission and distribution in 2025 alone and laying over 26,000 miles of new power lines to connect distant renewable farms to cities. Data centers are expected to add electricity demand above 2% annually through 2026—more than twice the historical average. But profitability is collapsing. Copper prices jumped 18% in 2025, steel conduit costs rose 14%, and tariffs can add another 25% on top. A 50% tariff on copper products took effect in August 2025. Equipment lead times have doubled to over 26 weeks. The industry operates on 5.5% margins (2022), so any input cost shock translates to losses. Eaton, Schneider Electric, Siemens, and ABB are all investing heavily in US capacity—Siemens opened a new Fort Worth plant in 2025 with 480 jobs hired immediately and a target of 800—but this is defensive positioning against tariffs, not profit maximization. They're betting that sustained grid and data-center demand will eventually make the investment pay out, but in the near term they're eating costs.

3D printer manufacturing is racing for a different finish line: the defense contract. The global market hit $30.5 billion in 2025 with 20.5% growth projected to 2026. The US had 182 manufacturers and 5,569 employees in 2025, up from a $5.181 billion baseline in 2021. Metal additive manufacturing—printing rocket nozzles, turbine blades, aircraft parts—is the fastest-growing segment, projected to reach $102 billion globally by 2035 at 24% CAGR. The FY2026 National Defense Authorization Act included restrictions on foreign 3D systems for Department of Defense work, and those orders are real. Stratasys merged with Desktop Metal in 2023 to integrate polymer and metal capabilities, invested in Tritone Technologies for metal and ceramic printing in 2025, and is hunting for every aerospace and defense contract available. Entry-level machines from Chinese makers (Bambu Lab, Creality) are underpricing commodity models, but the high-margin action is in aerospace aftermarket parts and military contracts. Companies that own domestic metal-printing capacity are locking in 10–15 year defense relationships. Companies that don't are watching their industrial printer sales collapse into a low-margin commodity market.

The evidence

  • Semiconductors: US and Taiwan signed $250B direct-investment agreement (January 2026) to push chip making into US fabs; Intel now runs 18A production at Arizona Fab 52; $52.7B in CHIPS Act funding backs 18 planned factories (2025-2026).
  • Computer manufacturing: Dell hit $113.5B revenue in FY2026 with 40% growth in AI infrastructure and $43B backlog; consumer PC shipments dropped 7% in Q1 2026 as DRAM costs surged 40–70% YoY; Chinese laptop exports fell 47.6% YoY in April 2025 (2025-2026).
  • Electrical equipment: Market reached $81.4B in 2026, up 7.8% YoY; copper prices jumped 18% in 2025 and tariffs add another 25% on top; switchgear lead times doubled to 26+ weeks; Siemens Fort Worth plant (opened 2025) hired 480 with target of 800 (2025-2026).
  • 3D printing: Global market hit $30.5B in 2025 with 20.5% growth; metal additive manufacturing to reach $102B by 2035 at 24% CAGR; FY2026 NDAA restricts foreign systems for DoD work; Stratasys-Desktop Metal merger (2023) integrating polymer and metal capabilities (2025-2026).
  • Grid modernization: US utilities spending $84.9B on transmission/distribution in 2025; laying 26,000+ miles of new power lines; data-center electricity demand expected above 2% annually through 2026, more than 2x historical average (2025-2026).

Who rides it

Companies with domestic manufacturing capacity—Stratasys in 3D printing, Intel and TSMC in chips, Eaton and Siemens in electrical gear—are locking in long-term defense contracts, government subsidies, and customer loyalty from risk-averse buyers. They're paying more to build here, but they're also getting certainty: guaranteed off-take agreements, tariff protection, and priority access to government infrastructure spending. For AI-server makers like Dell, reshoring is not a cost but an asset—the supply-chain security is worth a price premium to hyperscalers. For 3D printer makers, the defense mandate is a moat: Stratasys and Desktop Metal (now merged) own metal-printing IP that Chinese competitors can't easily replicate in the timeframe that DoD contracts require. First-mover advantage in domestic capacity is worth years of operating margin loss.

Who's squeezed

Low-cost contract manufacturers and commodity-hardware makers are getting crushed. Chinese 3D-printer makers (Bambu Lab, Creality) are undercutting industrial models in consumer and low-end commercial segments, but they're locked out of defense work. Chinese laptop makers lost 47.6% of US export volume in a single year as tariffs and supply-chain anxiety pushed customers toward domestic assembly. Entry-level computer manufacturers who don't have an AI-server story or government contract are watching margins compress: consumer PC margins were already thin, and now DRAM costs have spiked 40–70%, making a profit on a $500 laptop nearly impossible. Small electrical-equipment distributors are squeezed between tariffs pushing costs up 25–40% and lead times that have doubled to 26 weeks, destroying working capital and forcing inventory hoarding. Companies without US manufacturing have to choose between absorbing tariff costs or losing customers to Eaton and Siemens, who built domestic capacity and can pass costs along to government buyers.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that reshoring is only economically viable at the high end. AI servers and defense contracts have margins fat enough to absorb higher US manufacturing costs. Consumer PCs, entry-level 3D printers, and commodity electrical gear do not. If tariffs stay high and the government doesn't continue massive subsidies (like the CHIPS Act), reshoring will hollow out to just military and luxury goods, leaving the bulk of manufacturing offshore and the US supply chain more fragmented, not less. Companies betting on reshoring are also betting that government infrastructure spending (grid, data centers, defense) stays strong through 2028 and beyond. If demand softens—if recession hits, if AI buildout slows, if political will for defense spending wanes—the massive capacity investments made in 2025-2026 become stranded assets. Lead times are already at 26 weeks; if orders dry up, backlogs evaporate overnight and factories go dark.

In one sentence

Reshoring advanced manufacturing in a nutshell

Tariffs and supply-chain security rules are forcing US manufacturers of chips, 3D printers, and electrical gear to invest domestically—and fast—even though it's more expensive and less profitable.
