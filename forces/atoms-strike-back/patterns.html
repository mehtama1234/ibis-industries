The synthesis · what repeats across all 9

The patterns

Trade war and tariffs force reshoring, nearshoring, and inventory hoarding — physical supply chains get expensive and political, and whoever moves fastest to nearshore wins.

The 25% tariff on imported auto parts (effective May 2025) hit a sector already fragile from EV transition. Auto parts makers, facing both tariff walls and evaporating demand for exhaust systems and radiators as EVs take over, had only one choice: move production to Mexico. In the first nine months of 2025 alone, Mexican auto parts investment surged 200%—a reshoring in everything but name. US auto parts are bouncing (5.4% growth in 2026, market at $74.9 billion) but only because the larger customers can afford the tariff hit and force suppliers to absorb it. Smaller specialists are going bankrupt; employment fell despite stable production as factories got leaner. Bankruptcy filings among auto parts suppliers hit decade highs in 2025. This isn't a recovery; it's a consolidation.

The through-line

Tariffs forced reshoring and nearshoring, but only companies with capital and political access survived; thin-margin importers, small suppliers, and commodity-dependent industries are being consolidated or eliminated.

Who wins across the board

Nearshoring hubs win hardest—Mexico captured 200% of the auto parts investment wave, and adjacency to the US makes it the obvious valve for tariff-locked supply chains. Domestic makers with political access and capital scale (Cleveland-Cliffs, ADM, Stratasys, HP) absorb tariff shocks and consolidate competitors. Aerospace and defense contractors—and the industrial base they control—are the geopolitical winners; reshoring mandates turn 3D printing and advanced manufacturing into a national security play where price is irrelevant and volumes are guaranteed. Large, integrated players (Nippon Steel buying US Steel, Stratasys-Desktop Metal) lock in scale and reduce competition. Anyone making military or critical infrastructure components rides a tailwind of government contracts and reshoring mandates.

Who's squeezed across the board

Thin-margin importers and small auto parts suppliers are crushed. A 5% margin industry (auto parts) cannot absorb a 25% tariff hit, so they either move to Mexico or file for bankruptcy—both are consolidation. Small soybean processors cannot compete with ADM, AGP, and the mega-plants coming online; margins fell 40–50% and only the largest survive. Global commodity producers and export-dependent industries (Chinese steelmakers flooding the market, Asian auto parts makers) lose access to the US and face a tariff wall that makes local competitors cost-competitive again. Mid-tier manufacturers without capital for nearshoring or reshoring get caught between tariff costs and flat demand, unable to invest in EV transition or meet defense requirements—they disappear.

The biggest tension

The one to watch

The single biggest risk is that tariffs solved the import problem but unleashed commodity oversupply and margin compression that will not resolve for years. Chinese steel capacity alone (131M tonnes exports in 2025) is now fighting a 50% tariff wall, but the global excess capacity problem (640 to 745 million tonnes by 2028, per OECD) is structural—not a tariff can fix it. Soybean processing margins at $1.10–$1.50/bushel (August 2026) leave no room for error; a single crop failure, commodity price spike, or policy reversal vaporizes profit. The same is true for auto parts—tariffs bought time, but EV adoption is shrinking demand for traditional components (exhaust, radiators, engines), and bankruptcy is the exit for suppliers who cannot transition. Reshoring works when geopolitics and demand align (aerospace, defense, 3D printing), but when tariffs hit commodities and thin-margin industries, consolidation and hollowing-out are the results, not recovery.
