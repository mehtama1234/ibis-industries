Atoms Strike Back

Trade war tariffs make foreign supply chains expensive and risky; nearshoring wins, thin-margin importers lose.

Starting in 2025, US tariffs turned the global supply chain inside out. A 25% levy on auto parts, 50% on steel, and cascading restrictions on machinery transformed the math for manufacturers. The cost of importing is now so high that nearshoring to Mexico—or building at home—is faster and cheaper than shipping from Asia. The winners are companies with capital to move, domestic makers with pricing power, and nations next door. The losers are importers living on razor-thin margins and industries locked into oversupply.

What changed

The 25% tariff on imported auto parts (effective May 2025) hit a sector already fragile from EV transition. Auto parts makers, facing both tariff walls and evaporating demand for exhaust systems and radiators as EVs take over, had only one choice: move production to Mexico. In the first nine months of 2025 alone, Mexican auto parts investment surged 200%—a reshoring in everything but name. US auto parts are bouncing (5.4% growth in 2026, market at $74.9 billion) but only because the larger customers can afford the tariff hit and force suppliers to absorb it. Smaller specialists are going bankrupt; employment fell despite stable production as factories got leaner. Bankruptcy filings among auto parts suppliers hit decade highs in 2025. This isn't a recovery; it's a consolidation.

Steel faced a different shock but the same outcome: tariffs worked, then physics won. In February-June 2025, Trump imposed 25–50% tariffs, and steel prices spiked. The US became the third-largest steel producer globally in 2025—a genuine windfall. But the margin windfall is already eroding. Raw material costs rose sharply (iron ore +9%, coking coal +17% in 2025), and demand is flat. Construction spending grew 2.6% but volume didn't move. Automotive demand is steady at 10.2M units but is losing steel content as EV adoption hits 42% of output by 2031. Global oversupply is the immovable object: China exported a record 131 million tonnes in 2025 (up 153% since 2020), and OECD projects global capacity will grow from 640 to 745 million tonnes by 2028 while demand grows only 34 million. Tariffs won one round. Market physics is winning the next one. The industry is consolidating at speed—Cleveland-Cliffs acquired ArcelorMittal and Stelco, and Nippon Steel bought US Steel for $14.2 billion in June 2025, absorbing it under government "Golden Share" oversight. Domestic capacity is now political, not just economic.

Soybean processing tells the story of reshoring meeting market reality head-on. The EPA's renewable diesel mandates created nearly unlimited demand for soybean oil, triggering a 23% capacity expansion over three years and $6 billion in new crush facility investment. Crush volumes hit a record 2.54 billion bushels forecast for 2025–26. But crushing soybeans produces meal as a byproduct—tons of it. That meal flooded the market and collapsed margins from $2–$3 per bushel in 2023 to $1.10–$1.50 in 2025 as new plants came online in North Dakota, Kansas, and elsewhere. China's 13% tariff on US soybeans (in place since 2018, unchanged) reduced US exports to China by 33% versus 2024, forcing domestic processors to rely on crush rather than global markets. Soybean prices bounced around (peaked at $12.30 in early 2024, fell to $9.55 mid-August 2024, recovered to $10.30 end-2025, around $11.60 by August 2026) but the underlying math—record volumes, crushed margins—stayed grim. ADM announced in August 2026 that it was upgrading four crush plants to unlock 700,000 metric tons of additional annual capacity, even as margins stayed compressed. This is capacity built for politics, not profit. Only the largest processors survive; consolidation is the only answer.

3D printing is the one industry where reshoring is actually profitable—because it serves defense and aerospace, where price is secondary to security. The global 3D printer market is growing 20.5% year-over-year (2025–2026) and is projected to hit $102 billion by 2035 at 24% CAGR. Metal additive manufacturing is the fastest-growing segment, driven entirely by aerospace and defense demand plus post-FY2026 NDAA restrictions forbidding foreign 3D systems for DoD contracts. Reshoring mandates and supply chain redundancy are now strategic requirements, not cost negotiations. Stratasys merged with Desktop Metal in 2023 and is integrating capabilities; HP launched high-temperature FFF systems at Formnext 2025. Margins are thin (0.8–2.8%) and Chinese competitors (Bambu Lab, Creality) are undercutting on commodity machines, but aerospace aftermarket parts are profitable and growing. This industry is winning because tariffs aligned with geopolitical demand, not just punishing imports.

The evidence

  • Auto parts: 25% tariff on imports (May 2025) drove 200% surge in Mexican investment in first nine months of 2025; US market $74.9B (2026), 5.4% growth, but bankruptcy filings hit decade highs.
  • Steel: 50% tariff (Feb-June 2025) boosted US to third-largest producer globally in 2025, but global oversupply (China 131M tonnes exports in 2025, +153% since 2020) and material costs (iron ore +9%, coking coal +17% in 2025) eroding margins; Nippon Steel $14.2B US Steel buyout (June 2025) made domestic capacity political.
  • Soybean processing: Record 2.54 billion bushels crush forecast for 2025–26 (23% capacity expansion, $6B invested) collapsed margins from $2–$3/bushel (2023) to $1.10–$1.50 (2025); China's 13% tariff reduced US exports 33% versus 2024, forcing domestic consolidation.
  • 3D printing: 20.5% YoY growth (2025–2026), metal additive projected $102B by 2035 at 24% CAGR; aerospace/defense and NDAA reshoring mandates making margins viable (0.8–2.8%) despite Chinese commodity competition.
  • Across all sectors, tariffs succeeded in making imports expensive but unleashed market forces—oversupply, margin compression, consolidation—that reshape who survives; nearshoring and military-grade reshoring win; thin-margin importers and global commodity players lose.

Who rides it

Nearshoring hubs win hardest—Mexico captured 200% of the auto parts investment wave, and adjacency to the US makes it the obvious valve for tariff-locked supply chains. Domestic makers with political access and capital scale (Cleveland-Cliffs, ADM, Stratasys, HP) absorb tariff shocks and consolidate competitors. Aerospace and defense contractors—and the industrial base they control—are the geopolitical winners; reshoring mandates turn 3D printing and advanced manufacturing into a national security play where price is irrelevant and volumes are guaranteed. Large, integrated players (Nippon Steel buying US Steel, Stratasys-Desktop Metal) lock in scale and reduce competition. Anyone making military or critical infrastructure components rides a tailwind of government contracts and reshoring mandates.

Who's squeezed

Thin-margin importers and small auto parts suppliers are crushed. A 5% margin industry (auto parts) cannot absorb a 25% tariff hit, so they either move to Mexico or file for bankruptcy—both are consolidation. Small soybean processors cannot compete with ADM, AGP, and the mega-plants coming online; margins fell 40–50% and only the largest survive. Global commodity producers and export-dependent industries (Chinese steelmakers flooding the market, Asian auto parts makers) lose access to the US and face a tariff wall that makes local competitors cost-competitive again. Mid-tier manufacturers without capital for nearshoring or reshoring get caught between tariff costs and flat demand, unable to invest in EV transition or meet defense requirements—they disappear.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that tariffs solved the import problem but unleashed commodity oversupply and margin compression that will not resolve for years. Chinese steel capacity alone (131M tonnes exports in 2025) is now fighting a 50% tariff wall, but the global excess capacity problem (640 to 745 million tonnes by 2028, per OECD) is structural—not a tariff can fix it. Soybean processing margins at $1.10–$1.50/bushel (August 2026) leave no room for error; a single crop failure, commodity price spike, or policy reversal vaporizes profit. The same is true for auto parts—tariffs bought time, but EV adoption is shrinking demand for traditional components (exhaust, radiators, engines), and bankruptcy is the exit for suppliers who cannot transition. Reshoring works when geopolitics and demand align (aerospace, defense, 3D printing), but when tariffs hit commodities and thin-margin industries, consolidation and hollowing-out are the results, not recovery.

In one sentence

Atoms strike back in a nutshell

Tariffs forced reshoring and nearshoring, but only companies with capital and political access survived; thin-margin importers, small suppliers, and commodity-dependent industries are being consolidated or eliminated.
