A · The auto reordering

The auto tariff shock

25% tariffs on cars and parts in 2025 forced the auto supply chain to abandon the US and pivot toward Mexico, killing a century of manufacturing geography overnight.

In April 2025, the Trump administration slapped a 25% tariff on all imported vehicles and auto parts. It looked like one policy. What it actually did was rewrite the supply chain for an entire industry. Within months, US automakers and parts suppliers faced a choice: absorb massive costs, pass them to customers, or move everything to Mexico. Most of them chose Mexico.

What changed

The tariff hit on May 3, 2025, and it was not abstract. A 25% import duty on vehicles immediately raised the price of a car by $1,600 to $8,900 depending on where it was made. Steel and aluminum costs for domestic car makers jumped too. Ford, GM, and Stellantis absorbed $41.7 billion in collective tariff costs in the first half of 2025 alone. Stellantis reported a €2.3 billion ($2.7 billion) net loss partly because of the early tariff shock. BMW, Mercedes, and Volkswagen combined for $6 billion in losses. Customers simply didn't show up for the higher prices. EV sales, which were supposed to be the future, actually fell 4% in 2025 compared to 2024. Tesla's market share dropped from 49% in 2024 to 45% by 2026, even as the number of battery plants exploded—70 new facilities opened in 2025 alone, bringing US cell capacity to 20 GWh per year and threatening oversupply. What made all of this worse was that it happened right as the entire supply chain was supposed to transition away from gasoline engines toward electric. That transition requires retooling, new components (battery thermal management, lightweight materials, power electronics), and billions in R&D. Small parts suppliers didn't have the money to both absorb tariff losses and invest in EV tech. Some of them simply died. Auto parts supplier bankruptcies hit decade highs in 2025. First Brands, which owned Raybestos, Autolite, and FRAM, filed Chapter 11 in September 2025. Tayco and Accuride followed. About 29,000 parts manufacturing jobs disappeared in 2025 despite the fact that actual production volume stayed flat. The jobs vanished because suppliers automated harder and moved faster than ever before to Mexico.\n\nNearshoring to Mexico became the only rational move. In the first nine months of 2025 alone, new Mexican auto parts investment surged 200%. Mexico already sits inside USMCA, the trade deal that was supposed to keep manufacturing in North America. But the tariff regime created a new incentive structure: tariffs on non-FTA parts meant that Mexican-made components qualified for preferential duty treatment, while US-made parts faced retaliatory tariffs from China (10–15%) and compliance headaches with new Mexican tariffs (25–50% on non-FTA parts starting January 2026). For a supplier in Ohio or Michigan, the math became brutal. Move to Mexico, stay competitive, or go bankrupt. Stellantis announced a $13 billion US investment plan in October 2025, but the money was going into selective modernization—like a new four-cylinder engine plant in Kokomo, Indiana that will produce the GMET4 EVO starting 2026. This was not a reshoring surge. This was triage. The company was keeping just enough US capacity to say it was investing while pivoting the rest of the supply chain to Mexico.\n\nMeanwhile, the engine business was not dying—it was mutating. CAFE standards were reset in June 2025 to exclude EV credits from compliance calculations, which meant OEMs could sell more gasoline cars without penalty. But nobody wanted pure gasoline anymore. The market demanded hybrids, which jumped 10% year-on-year. So engine makers stopped making engines. They started making powertrains: gasoline blocks for legacy sedans, hybrid modules for new platforms, 48-volt mild-hybrid systems for trucks, and control electronics for everything. Engine and parts manufacturing in 2026 was a $43.8 billion market growing at 2.5% per year, which sounds stable until you realize it was half the complexity it used to be. The number of car platforms was shrinking. The number of suppliers was shrinking (from consolidation). The percentage of supply coming from Mexico was rising. By mid-2026, auto parts manufacturing in the US was a $74.9 billion market growing at 5.4% per year, but employment hadn't recovered from the 2025 collapse, and the margin profile had flipped: larger suppliers with R&D muscle were consolidating smaller players, and whoever wasn't big enough to invest in EV tech or agile enough to nearshore was getting acquired or liquidated.

The evidence

  • Auto parts manufacturing: US market size $74.9 billion in 2026, growing 5.4% annually, but employment collapsed after 2025 nearshoring wave.
  • Vehicle manufacturing: US auto industry lost $393.3 billion in 2026 (−1.0% growth), with Ford, GM, and Stellantis absorbing $41.7 billion in tariff costs in first half of 2025 alone.
  • Engine and parts manufacturing: $43.8 billion market in 2026 (2.5% growth), with 29,000 jobs lost in 2025 despite stable production, signaling rapid automation and nearshoring.
  • EV transition stalled: EV sales declined 4% in 2025 versus 2024; only 20% of new US sales were EVs or hybrids; Tesla share dropped to 45% by 2026.
  • Supply chain reordering: Mexican auto parts investment surged 200% in first nine months of 2025; Stellantis committed $13 billion to US modernization in October 2025 but shifted majority production toward Mexico under USMCA.

Who rides it

Mexico became the only rational location for North American auto supply. Any large supplier who could move manufacturing there did—whether to avoid tariffs, access USMCA duty treatment, or sidestep the Mexican retaliatory tariff regime by producing locally. Mexican facilities suddenly had cost advantage, political cover, and preferential access to the US market. Hybrids and battery thermal systems won too. Demand for hybrids jumped 10% year-on-year through 2025–2026, while pure gasoline demand stayed flat. That meant suppliers who could pivot to hybrid components and battery cooling systems (Denso, Bosch, Continental) picked up market share from legacy engine makers. Larger parts suppliers with R&D muscle and cash reserves won through consolidation. They bought up bankrupt competitors (First Brands, Tayco, Accuride) on the cheap and consolidated supply bases, which reduced their per-unit costs and made them more attractive to OEMs looking for financial stability.

Who's squeezed

Small and mid-sized US auto parts suppliers got squeezed the hardest. They couldn't absorb the tariff costs, couldn't afford EV retooling investments, and couldn't move to Mexico as easily as the big players. Decade-high bankruptcies in 2025 (First Brands, Tayco, Accuride) were the result. Traditional gasoline engine makers lost demand to hybrids and faced pressure from both tariffs and the shift toward electrification. About 29,000 parts manufacturing jobs disappeared in 2025 even though production stayed flat—automation and nearshoring killed them. Regions like the Rust Belt saw job losses concentrated in first-tier suppliers (the small shops that feed the big Tier 1 suppliers). EV startups and pure-electric platforms lost momentum too. EV sales actually fell 4% in 2025, and the industry was left with 70 new battery plants and capacity approaching oversupply by mid-2026—a classic boom-then-bust cycle where overinvestment preceded actual demand.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that the supply chain gets so concentrated and so dependent on Mexico that any disruption—a pandemic, a political crisis south of the border, a labor shortage—cascades through the entire North American auto industry. Stellantis, Ford, and GM have already committed to massive Mexican investments. If Mexico's investment environment shifts, if labor costs rise, or if political instability slows shipping, the US auto industry has no fallback. The US never actually reshored. It just offshored one state to the west. That gamble works fine until it doesn't.

In one sentence

The auto tariff shock in a nutshell

Tariffs killed reshoring dreams and forced a 200% nearshoring surge to Mexico, collapsing small suppliers and consolidating the industry in nine months.
