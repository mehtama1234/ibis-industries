D · Food & the retail edge

Food's tariff exposure

Tariffs and commodity shocks are reshaping meat, coffee, and soy processing—winners are those fast enough to nearshore or consolidate.

Food processing sits at the collision point of three forces that don't normally hit at once: tariffs, commodity volatility, and labor scarcity. Meat plants can't find workers to fill cutting roles even at rising wages. Coffee roasters face a 60% year-over-year spike in bean prices and tariffs that choke imports. Soybean crush margins are collapsing even as volumes hit record highs. The industry is reshaping fast—and winners are the ones moving fastest to consolidate, automate, and lock in supply.

What changed

The meat processing industry—a mature $387.7 billion (2026) sector that has barely grown in 50 years—is now squeezed from both sides at once. Beef cattle prices are up 7% to an average of $240 per cwt in 2026 as supplies tighten. But labor is the real shock: the Big Four processors (JBS, Tyson, Cargill, National Beef) can't fill meat-cutting and evisceration roles at any wage. The work is brutally physical, and immigration enforcement under Trump has tightened the labor pipeline sharply. The result is a surge in automation investment—meat processors are pouring money into cobots and AI vision systems to replace skilled butchers. Simultaneously, export markets have collapsed and recovered unpredictably. China locked out US beef in 2025, wiping out 96% of sales and leaving September 2025 exports at just 556 metric tons. A May 2026 Trump-Xi trade deal restored partial access, but US plants still struggle to meet the new quota requirements. Plant-based meat, which was the boogeyman in 2023–2024, has effectively vanished—down to 2–3% retail share as consumers rejected the taste and "lack of naturalness." What's actually reshaping the industry is consolidation and antitrust pressure: the DOJ opened investigations into the Big Four in 2025–2026, and price-fixing settlements have cost them hundreds of millions.

Coffee roasting—a $23.76–$29.03 billion (2025) industry—is caught between premiumization and commodity chaos. Consumers aged 18-40 are driving specialty demand; 66% of American adults drink coffee daily as of 2025, and ready-to-drink cold brew is growing at 7.5% annually through 2034. But input costs have turned brutal. Arabica prices hit record highs of $4.30 per pound by December 2024, up 60% year-over-year, as droughts ravaged Brazil and Vietnam—which together supply over 50% of global coffee. Brazil's 2025-26 production fell 4.4%, and Vietnam faces a 10-20% harvest decline. Then the US slapped a 50% tariff on Brazilian coffee imports in mid-2025, cutting August imports 75% versus 2024. Roasters scrambled to Colombian and other origins, but that creates supply-chain fragility and quality variation. Margins are compressed—retailers take 11–19 months to pass cost increases to consumers, leaving roasters absorbing losses in 2025-26. Consolidation is the response: Royal Cup acquired Farmer Bros in March 2026 to create scale, and Nestlé and Starbucks launched a cold-coffee concentrate line together, betting that RTD and functional (mushroom, vitamin, adaptogen-infused) formats can escape commodity volatility.

Soybean processing is flooded—but not for the reason anyone expected. The EPA's renewable-diesel mandates under the RVO (Renewable Volume Obligation) have created nearly unlimited demand for soybean oil as a feedstock. This triggered a 23% capacity expansion over three years, with over $6 billion invested in new crush facilities. Crush volumes hit a record 2.54 billion bushels forecast for 2025–26. But here's the trap: crushing soybeans for oil leaves meal—a massive byproduct. The flood of cheap meal has collapsed crush margins from $2–$3 per bushel in 2023 to just $1.10–$1.50 per bushel in 2025 as four new major plants came online in North Dakota, Kansas, and elsewhere. China's 13% tariff on US soybeans (imposed 2018, still standing in 2025) has reduced US soybean exports to China by 33% versus 2024, forcing processors to rely on domestic crush instead of global markets. Soybean prices themselves swing wildly—they peaked at $12.30 per bushel in early 2024, fell to $9.55 mid-August 2024, recovered to $10.30 by end-of-2025, and sat around $11.60 by August 2026. The response is aggressive expansion: Louis Dreyfus, North Dakota Soybean Processors, and Incobrasa all broke ground on new plants in 2024. By 2025–2026, ADM, AGP, and SD Soybean Processors brought facilities online. In August 2026, ADM alone announced upgrades unlocking ~700,000 metric tons of additional annual capacity. But margins stay compressed because supply is massive and there's nowhere to sell it except into renewable diesel—which is mandated, not profitable.

The evidence

  • Meat processing: Beef cattle prices forecast to average $240 per cwt in 2026, up 7% from 2025, while the Big Four (JBS, Tyson, Cargill, National Beef) face DOJ antitrust investigations and price-fixing settlements totaling hundreds of millions in 2025–2026.
  • Coffee roasting: Arabica coffee hit $4.30/lb by December 2024 (up 60% YoY), and the US 50% tariff on Brazilian imports in August 2025 cut imports 75% YoY, forcing roasters to shift to Colombian origins and compress margins for 11–19 months until retail prices catch up.
  • Soybean processing: Crush margins fell from $2–$3/bushel in 2023 to $1.10–$1.50/bushel in 2025 as new facilities added 75 million bushels of annual capacity, yet China's 13% tariff (2018–2025) reduced US soybean exports to China by 33% versus 2024.
  • Meat automation: Global market for automation robots projected to reach $4.5B by 2033 (9.2% CAGR) as meat processors race to replace labor in de-hoofing, evisceration, and packaging roles.
  • Coffee consolidation: Royal Cup acquired Farmer Bros in March 2026 to create scaled integrated beverage platform, while Nestlé and Starbucks launched cold-coffee concentrate line to capture fast-growing RTD and functional coffee segments (7.5% CAGR through 2034).

Who rides it

Consolidators win. Royal Cup, Nestlé, and Starbucks move fast to lock in scale and differentiate into cold brew and functional formats. Big meat processors with capital for automation—even facing antitrust heat—can offset labor scarcity by replacing butchers with cobots. Soybean crushers with integrated renewable-diesel operations (ADM, AGP) can absorb margin compression because they're making fuel, not competing on meal alone. The shared pattern: whoever captures nearshore supply first, invests in automation or product innovation, and has enough scale to weather tariff swings wins. Smaller, single-format players—regional coffee roasters, independent crush mills, local meat packers—get squeezed.

Who's squeezed

Regional and independent players get hammered. Small coffee roasters can't absorb the 60% commodity spike or negotiate tariff pass-through like Nestlé can. Independent soybean crushers can't invest in the capacity expansions that ADM and Louis Dreyfus are pouring $6 billion into—they get margin-crushed as meal prices collapse. Small and mid-sized meat packers can't afford the automation surge or the compliance burden of DOJ antitrust investigations. Labor-dependent processors without capital for automation lose workers to other sectors. And retailers initially absorb losses—the 11–19 month lag before coffee price increases hit shelves means smaller roasters face 12+ months of margin compression with no relief. Import-dependent roasters betting solely on Brazilian coffee get hit hardest by tariffs.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is tariff-on-tariff cascading and supply-chain bifurcation. If the US slaps reciprocal tariffs on another major food origin—say Colombia on coffee, or Canada on grains—the industry fractures into separate supply networks (US-nearshore vs. Asia vs. EU), forcing processors to duplicate infrastructure or pick sides. China's behavior is also unpredictable: the May 2026 beef deal restored partial access, but quota enforcement is unclear, and China could lock out US beef again over geopolitical friction. The labor crunch in meat processing is real and accelerating—automation helps, but it's capital-intensive and takes 2–3 years to deploy. If immigration enforcement stays tight and wages don't rise fast enough, some plants could go idle or relocate to Mexico. Finally, commodity volatility looks structural (climate, mandates, tariffs) rather than cyclical. Coffee droughts, soybean oversupply, and renewable-diesel mandates aren't going away. Processors with thin margins (1–5% in meat and soy, 10% in coffee but compressed) can't survive repeated $0.50–$1 per unit swings.

In one sentence

Food's tariff exposure in a nutshell

Tariffs and commodity shocks are forcing meat, coffee, and soy processors to consolidate, automate, and nearshore—but independent and regional players lack the capital and scale to survive the reshuffle.
