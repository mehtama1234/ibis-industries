C · Reshoring the factory

Appliances & durable goods

A 50% tariff on imported appliances in mid-2025 crushed margins and forced US makers to nearshore fast—or absorb billions in costs.

In June 2025, the US government slapped a 50% tariff on the steel-equivalent content in imported refrigerators, washers, dryers, dishwashers, and ovens. Within months, every major appliance maker in America was bleeding. Whirlpool absorbed approximately 300 million dollars in new tariff costs. Electrolux went negative on operating margins. LG and Samsung posted losses. And the market was too weak—too promotional, too flat—to pass those costs to consumers. The appliance business, which had hummed along at 2.8% operating margins when times were good, hit rock bottom. So they pivoted: nearshoring US plants, modernizing old factories, and betting on smart appliances instead of competing on price.

What changed

Tariffs on imported appliances rewrote the economics overnight. Before June 2025, US appliance makers had outsourced heavily: 55% of domestic demand was still met by imports despite decades of trade protection. Whirlpool, Electrolux, LG, and Samsung had spread manufacturing across Mexico, China, and Southeast Asia to keep costs down. The tariff was meant to protect US makers, but the timing was brutal. The housing market was already mixed—single-family starts fell 6.9% in 2025 to 943,000 units, while multifamily jumped 17.4%—and the retail market for appliances was essentially flat, growing just 0.3% to 0.6% in 2026. When you can't raise prices and your input costs jump 50%, you absorb the hit. Whirlpool's Q4 2025 margins collapsed to 2.8%. Electrolux turned negative at -2.9%. The market was so competitive and promotional that no one could pass through the tariff cost.

Tariffs made nearshoring newly valuable—and urgent. The moment those tariff bills landed in Q4 2025, every major appliance maker announced big US investment plans. GE Appliances (the Haier subsidiary that makes everything from refrigerators to ranges) committed 3 billion dollars over five years in August 2025, including modernization of 11 US plants and expansion in Kentucky, Alabama, Georgia, Tennessee, and South Carolina. Whirlpool followed with 300 million dollars for laundry manufacturing at its Clyde and Marion plants in Ohio, expecting to add 400 to 600 jobs by end-2026 and early 2027. These weren't small tweaks. They were bets that even though tariff costs were punishing in the near term, building in the US—or nearshoring to Mexico—was cheaper and faster than importing from Asia and eating the 50% hit.

The tariff also made smart appliances—AI-powered, connected to home ecosystems—newly valuable. When you're competing on price and your margins are destroyed, you pivot to higher-margin segments. Whirlpool, LG, and Samsung all shifted strategy toward connected appliances where consumers will pay premiums for convenience, energy efficiency, and smart features. This was the escape route from pure commoditization. But it also meant smaller, local manufacturers without the R&D budget to compete in smart appliances were locked out. The five major players—Haier/GE Appliances, Whirlpool, Electrolux, LG, and Samsung—controlled about 60% of the market going into 2026, and the tariff and margin crisis only concentrated power further.

By 2026, the appliance industry faced a painful choice: nearshore fast to Mexico or Southeast Asia with fresh investment, or stay in Asia and absorb tariff costs in perpetuity. For major players, nearshoring became the only path to margin recovery. For smaller manufacturers without capital for factories or supply chains, it became a squeeze play. The broad retail appliance market was 144.83 billion dollars in 2026—huge—but growth was essentially flat at 0.3% to 0.6%, leaving zero room for error. Tariffs made scale, speed, and capital decisive.

The evidence

  • Appliance Manufacturing: Whirlpool absorbed 300 million dollars in tariff costs from the June 2025 50% tariff on steel-equivalent imports, with Q4 2025 margins collapsing to 2.8%.
  • Appliance Manufacturing: Electrolux, LG, and Samsung all posted negative or razor-thin margins in Q4 2025, unable to pass tariff costs to consumers in a flat, promotional market.
  • Appliance Manufacturing: GE Appliances committed 3 billion dollars (August 2025) to modernize 11 US plants and expand in Kentucky, Alabama, Georgia, Tennessee, and South Carolina.
  • Appliance Manufacturing: Whirlpool announced 300 million dollars for US laundry manufacturing (October 2025), expecting 400-600 jobs by end-2026; industry market was 144.83 billion dollars in 2026 with flat 0.3-0.6% growth.
  • Housing: Single-family starts fell 6.9% in 2025 to 943,000 units, but multifamily surged 17.4%, creating demand for compact and stackable appliance configurations.

Who rides it

Whirlpool, Electrolux, GE Appliances (Haier), and other major manufacturers with capital to invest in US plants win in the long run. The nearshoring strategy—modernizing existing US factories and building new capacity in Mexico—puts them ahead of imports. They also win by moving into smart appliances, where margins are better and tariffs matter less because consumers pay for convenience and data, not just steel. Regional winners in the US South (Kentucky, Alabama, Georgia, Tennessee, South Carolina) gain jobs and factory investment. Suppliers of components for smart home systems also win.

Who's squeezed

Smaller appliance manufacturers without capital to nearshore lose access to low-cost Asian supply chains and can't compete on price anymore. The tariff and margin collapse squeeze out anyone who didn't have scale or R&D budget to move upmarket into smart appliances. Importers and distributors who counted on cheap Asian appliances to drive volume also get hurt. Consumers in the near term lose too—appliances got more expensive through the promotional squeeze (retailers absorbed costs), and the flat market meant less choice and innovation. Housing was weak in 2025 (single-family starts down 6.9%), which dampened appliance sales overall.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that nearshoring to Mexico or Southeast Asia doesn't solve the tariff problem fast enough, and US appliance makers stay trapped in margin collapse for years. The retail market grew just 0.3% to 0.6% in 2026—essentially flat. If housing demand doesn't recover (single-family starts are already down), and the market stays promotional and competitive, then even with lower costs, appliance makers won't have room to recover margins. The other risk: if tariffs on Mexico or other nearshore destinations rise later (as trade policy can do), then all the investment in new US plants and supply chains becomes stranded.
