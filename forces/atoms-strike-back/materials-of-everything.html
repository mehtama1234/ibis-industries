The materials of everything

Tariffs and energy costs repriced basic materials—cement, chemicals, glass, rubber—making nearshore sourcing the only math that works.

When tariffs hit in early 2025, the price of everything made from basic materials spiked overnight. Chemical feedstock costs jumped 8–15%, cement got slapped with a 25% tariff in April, and synthetic rubber hit $2,387 per metric ton by Q1 2026. Manufacturers couldn't just raise prices—their customers were already reeling. So margins collapsed, consolidation accelerated, and whoever could move supply chains closer to home suddenly had an advantage worth billions.

What changed

Tariffs made feedstock cheap in the past look like a fantasy. US chemical product manufacturing generates $53.1 billion (2026), but that's growing at just 1.9% annually—a sector flatlining under pressure. When the US imposed its 10% universal tariff in early 2025, plus 20–50% targeted levies on selected countries, chemical makers faced immediate feedstock costs that rose 8–15%. Crude oil swung from $64 per barrel (2022 baseline) to $84.81 in June 2026, adding another layer of volatility that makes planning impossible. Downstream demand—automotive and construction—had already collapsed during 2024 under prior price spikes, and tariff uncertainty kept those customers afraid to commit to projects. Firms absorbed losses instead of passing them downstream because they had no choice. The response: automation and consolidation at a scale not seen in years. Global chemical M&A surged 18% in 2025. Borouge grabbed NOVA for $13.4 billion, ADNOC picked up Covestro for $14.7 billion, and Carlyle took a $7.7 billion stake in BASF's coatings business. These were not growth acquisitions—they were survival plays. Larger players retreated from commodities into specialty and high-margin segments. Smaller players got squeezed out.\n\nCement tells the same story in sharper relief because the math is concrete (literally). The US cement market is $16.94 billion (2025), but domestic production capacity is frozen at 102–105 million metric tons annually while consumption sits 105–108 million tons. That gap used to be filled by imports—25–26 million tons, nearly 21% of supply. Then the 25% cement tariff took effect in April 2025. Canadian and Mexican imports, which once accounted for 27% of the market, became price-uncompetitive overnight. Turkish and Vietnamese suppliers faced even steeper punitive duties. Import volumes fell 8% year-on-year in 2024 and stayed depressed through 2025. The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act created durable demand—207,000+ miles of roadway and 11,400+ bridge projects underway—but it deployed only $500 billion of $1.2 trillion by September 2025. This floors demand but doesn't rescue margins: the industry runs a 4–5% EBIT margin, and tariffs ate straight through it. One bright spot emerged: Portland-limestone cement (Type IL) captured 97% of blended cement shipments by 2026 because it has 10% lower CO2 and all 50 states approved it by April 2024. Suddenly manufacturers had leverage on sustainability to justify higher prices, and consolidation quickened. CEMEX reported 19% EBITDA growth to $2.27 billion in Q3 2025—the first consolidated net sales increase since Q1 2024.\n\nGlass product manufacturing faced a different kind of disruption: tariffs upended financing while demand shifted. The market is $33.3 billion (2026) with 4,012 establishments, but the shocks in 2025–2026 are reshaping who survives. Tariffs on steel and aluminum hit 50% in June 2025, forcing fabricators to either pass costs downstream or absorb losses. Import restrictions on Chinese and Malaysian float glass tightened supply, but domestic producers haven't built new capacity yet because capital spending is still frozen by uncertainty. What changed is demand architecture: 89% of premium brands plan to switch from plastic to glass by end-2026. That shift is real because recycled PET now costs 20% more than virgin plastic, making glass cost-competitive for the first time in decades. This is creating new growth—container glass could grow 45% by 2035—in a historically stagnant industry. Regulatory pressure on emissions, heavy metals, and VOCs is also mounting, so smaller firms either invest in cleaner furnaces or exit. That's why NSG Group went private via Apollo restructuring in April 2026, why Apogee Enterprises acquired Kalwall for ~$115 million in July 2026, and why consolidation continues: the tariff-driven supply tightness meets the plastic-to-glass switch at exactly the moment firms need capital to upgrade emissions controls.\n\nRubber product manufacturing is the smallest market here—$23.7 billion (2026)—but it shows the clearest pain. The sector contracted 1.1% in 2026; employment declined; wages grew 4.4% in 2025 even as total sector size shrunk, meaning wage growth couldn't offset job losses. US manufacturers face 12% tariffs on rubber and plastics products (2025), and Japanese imports got hit retroactive to August 2025 at 15% baseline. Synthetic rubber prices stayed elevated at $2,387 per metric ton in Q1 2026, squeezing profit margins on commodity goods. Nearly 20% of domestic demand is now met by imports, up from historical levels. Automotive—the largest end market—weakened under consumer caution, though EV production creates specialized demand for new tire compounds and sealing systems that command premium margins. The only bright spot is that Mexico, 84% USMCA-compliant and tariff-advantaged, captured nearshore rubber production: imports from Mexico stayed competitive where Asian and Japanese suppliers got priced out. Import penetration rose overall, but geography mattered. Manufacturers report difficulty filling skilled roles; wages spiked but talent stayed scarce.

The evidence

  • Chemical manufacturing: Tariffs raised feedstock costs 8–15% in early 2025; global M&A surged 18% YoY with deals like Borouge/NOVA ($13.4B) and ADNOC/Covestro ($14.7B) consolidating into specialty segments (2025)
  • Cement: 25% tariff in April 2025 shrunk Canadian/Mexican imports from 27% to non-competitive; import volumes fell 8% in 2024, stayed depressed through 2025; CEMEX reported 19% EBITDA growth to $2.27B in Q3 2025, first sales increase since Q1 2024 (2025–2026)
  • Glass: 89% of premium brands planning plastic-to-glass switch by end-2026; recycled PET up 20% above virgin plastic, making glass cost-competitive; NSG Group went private (April 2026), Apogee acquired Kalwall for ~$115M (July 2026) amid tariff-driven consolidation (2025–2026)
  • Rubber: Synthetic rubber at $2,387/MT in Q1 2026; sector contracted 1.1% in 2026; import penetration at 20% of demand (up from baseline); US faces 12% baseline tariffs, Japanese imports 15% retroactive August 2025 (2025–2026)
  • Energy/crude volatility: Crude oil swung from $64/bbl (2022) to $84.81/bbl (June 2026), forcing chemical feedstock planners into impossible forecasts; manufacturers investing in automation and predictive maintenance to cut maintenance costs 25% and preserve margins (2022–2026)

Who rides it

Large materials producers with enough scale to consolidate and access to capital win. Borouge, ADNOC, Carlyle, CRH, and CEMEX all made major moves in 2025–2026 when smaller competitors couldn't afford to upgrade for emissions compliance or absorb tariff costs. Geographic arbitrage winners include Mexican rubber producers (84% USMCA-compliant, tariff-advantaged) and Turkish/Vietnamese cement suppliers facing lower duty rates than China and Southeast Asia. The plastic-to-glass shift rewarded glass makers who could finance new capacity and fund regulatory compliance upgrades at the exact moment tariffs made capital expensive. Companies that automated early—investing in digital twins and predictive maintenance that cut maintenance costs 25%—preserved enough margin to outbid rivals for acquisitions and talent.

Who's squeezed

Small and mid-sized chemical, cement, glass, and rubber manufacturers with low margins and no pricing power got squeezed out. Cement makers couldn't raise prices fast enough to offset 25% tariff costs and still keep construction customers. Rubber makers saw import penetration rise as tariffs pushed domestic prices above Mexican and USMCA-compliant alternatives. Glass fabricators absorbed margin cuts rather than lose customers to price-sensitive competitors. Chemical makers—especially those dependent on volatile crude oil feedstock with no specialty differentiation—either consolidated or shrank. Workers in rubber manufacturing saw wages spike 4.4% in 2025 even as total sector employment declined, meaning aggregate paychecks fell. Regions dependent on these manufacturing jobs faced continued attrition.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The largest risk is a liquidity crunch for mid-market suppliers when tariffs persist long enough to exhaust working capital. Chemical and cement makers operate on 4–7% margins; if tariff pass-through stays incomplete for more than 18–24 months, many will run out of cash before consolidation or price increases rescue them. A second risk: nearshoring capacity doesn't build fast enough to absorb tariff-driven reshoring demand. The US cement industry is already running at capacity; cement can't move easily across borders; if reshoring accelerates faster than domestic capacity grows, prices will remain pinched and project financing will seize up. A third risk: energy costs don't stabilize. Crude oil volatility makes feedstock forecasting impossible for chemical makers, and furnace energy is non-negotiable for cement, glass, and rubber production. If geopolitics drive another energy spike, these industries—which already operate at thin margins and have invested heavily in automation to preserve profitability—could face a cascade of bankruptcies among firms that bet wrong on energy futures.

In one sentence

The materials of everything in a nutshell

Tariffs repriced basic materials overnight—chemical feedstock jumped 8–15%, cement got 25% duties, synthetic rubber hit $2,387/MT—forcing consolidation at scale and shifting power to large players with capital to upgrade and nearshore faster than competitors.
