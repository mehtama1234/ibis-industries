Metals Behind the Tariff Wall

Steel and aluminum tariffs ripple into every downstream builder and maker.

When Trump imposed 25% to 50% steel tariffs in early 2025 and doubled them by June, the US steel industry got a short jolt of demand and pricing power. Same story with aluminum: tariffs climbed from 25% to 50%, and prices spiked 25% year-over-year to $3,273 per ton by August 2026. But the bounce is already fading. Global oversupply is relentless, and the cost of the metals themselves—raw materials for steel up 9% to 17%, electricity for smelting now at $115+/MWh—is eating into those gains.

What changed

Steel bounced hard, then reality hit. The US steel industry hit $112.9 billion in market size in 2026, a 3.3% jump from 2025, after tariffs triggered a price spike and domestic production surge. The US became the third-largest steel producer globally in 2025. But the gains are narrowing fast. Raw material costs climbed: iron ore up 9%, coking coal up 17%, and scrap up 2% for 2025. Margin pressure is real—the gap between input costs and selling prices has tightened since spring 2025. Demand is not coming to the rescue. Construction spending grew 2.6%, but construction volume is flat. Automotive demand stays steady at 10.2M units, but steel content per vehicle keeps shrinking as EVs (now 42% of forecast output by 2031) are lighter than gas cars. Meanwhile, the world is drowning in steel. China exported a record 131 million tonnes in 2025, up 153% since 2020. The OECD warns that global excess capacity will grow from 640 to 745 million tonnes by 2028—new capacity of 139 million tonnes will arrive while demand grows only 34 million tonnes. The industry is consolidating fast because survival is hard. Cleveland-Cliffs swallowed ArcelorMittal and Stelco. Nippon Steel completed a $14.2 billion buyout of US Steel in June 2025 under government oversight. Headcount and mill count keep dropping: 196 steel establishments in 2026, down from 332 in 2022. Employees fell to 72,600 in 2026, declining 2.3% annually since 2021.\n\nAluminum tells a different, supply-constrained story. Prices surged 25% year-over-year by August 2026 to $3,273 per ton, driven by tariff protection, China's production ceiling at 45.5M tons, Russia's export collapse, and soaring EV demand for battery enclosures and body panels. The market is booming: $71.8 billion projected for 2026, up from $46.5 billion in 2022, with 6.09% CAGR projected through 2034. But growth is crippled by an electricity crisis. Primary smelting requires vast, stable power. Four US smelters produce only 683,500 tonnes per year, and just two run at full capacity, because electricity now costs $115+/MWh. Tech companies outbidding aluminum makers for renewable power has left the smelting sector half-idled. The answer is secondary (recycled) aluminum: it's energy-efficient and now dominates the sector. Eighty-five percent of US-made aluminum is recycled, up from 20-30% in the 1980s. Secondary jobs exploded—recycling employment up 20%+ since 2024. Novelis, the largest rolled-products maker, got hit hard by fires at its Oswego, NY facility from September to November 2025, disrupting shipments 5% in fiscal 2026. But the company is building back: Bay Minette, Alabama (600 kt capacity) opens in the second half of 2026. Century Aluminum restarted Mt. Holly capacity in June 2026 after a $50 million investment. The tariff wall is working for aluminum makers—prices are up, demand is up—but they are bottlenecked by power, not tariffs. The reshoring and nearshoring play here is not new mills but new recycling and downstream finishing capacity, because primary smelting will stay capped by electricity cost until power prices fall or AI competition for power eases.

The evidence

  • Iron & Steel: US steel became third-largest producer globally in 2025 after tariffs, but faces 131M tonnes of Chinese exports (up 153% since 2020) and OECD forecast of 745M tonnes global excess capacity by 2028
  • Iron & Steel: Employment down to 72,600 in 2026 (2.3% annual decline since 2021), mills consolidated from 332 in 2022 to 196 in 2026, despite 25-50% tariffs and $112.9B market size
  • Aluminum: Prices hit $3,273/ton by August 2026 (up 25% YoY) on tariff protection and EV demand, but primary smelting capped at 683,500 tonnes/year as electricity costs $115+/MWh—tech outbidding metalmakers for power
  • Aluminum: Secondary recycling now 85% of US production (up from 20-30% in 1980s), with recycling jobs up 20%+ since 2024; Novelis fires (Sept–Nov 2025) cut rolled-product shipments 5% in fiscal 2026
  • Aluminum: Market size projected $71.8B in 2026 (up from $46.5B in 2022), with 6.09% CAGR through 2034, as EV demand for battery enclosures and body panels accelerates despite global oversupply in steel

Who rides it

Recyclers and secondary aluminum processors win. Energy-efficient recycling dominates and jobs are up 20%+ since 2024. EV makers win on aluminum because tariff protection and EV demand (battery enclosures, body panels for lighter cars) send prices high but also guarantee supply—at least as long as power stays available. Integrated mills that can source scrap and run rolling/finishing lines (like Novelis, despite its fire setback) win over pure primary smelters. Foreign acquirers win: Nippon Steel bought US Steel for $14.2 billion in June 2025, and the company kept operating because the US saw no downside to Japan owning the brand, only upside in tariff rents.

Who's squeezed

Primary steel mills get squeezed. Global oversupply is immovable: 640 million tonnes of excess capacity growing to 745 million by 2028. China's 131 million tonnes of exports in 2025 (up 153% since 2020) blow through any tariff wall. US steelmakers lose headcount (down 2.3% annually since 2021) and mills (332 down to 196 in six years). Tariff-driven price gains vanish once raw materials (iron ore +9%, coking coal +17%) catch up. Downstream builders—auto, construction, appliance manufacturers—lose because they pay the tariff tax upfront and never see margins recover if they raise their selling prices. Primary aluminum smelters lose to electricity prices ($115+/MWh). Four US smelters make only 683,500 tonnes per year with two running below capacity. AI data centers outbid them for renewable power, and coal power is fading. The tariff wall protects the price, but power scarcity protects the shortage.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Global oversupply in steel is irreversible by tariff alone. The OECD warns 139 million tonnes of new capacity arrives by 2028 while global demand grows only 34 million tonnes. China's 131 million tonnes of exports in 2025 (up 153% since 2020) and China's ceiling of 45.5M tonnes of aluminum prove that no US tariff wall seals completely. The real risk is that tariffs exhaust political capital without solving the structural problem—demand isn't growing, vehicle steel content keeps dropping as EVs scale, and the world has too many steelmakers. In aluminum, the electricity crisis could persist. If power stays above $115/MWh and AI keeps outbidding metalmakers, the US never repairs primary smelting capacity. Tariffs protect price and recycled supply, but they leave primary mills and smelters crippled.

In one sentence

Metals behind the tariff wall in a nutshell

Tariffs lifted steel and aluminum prices in 2025-2026, but global oversupply in steel and an electricity crisis in aluminum smelting mean the gains are borrowed—reshoring winners are recyclers and downstream finishers, not primary producers.
