A · The auto reordering

The EV transition & legacy parts

Electrification voids some parts and creates others; suppliers must pick a side — and tariffs force the choice fast.

The auto industry is splitting. EVs don't need transmissions. Battery costs fell 8% in 2025, but federal tax credits disappeared September 30, 2025, crushing demand. Meanwhile, tariffs jumped from 2.2% to 17.0% by April 2025, and the Big Three took $65 billion in writedowns. Legacy parts suppliers are caught: nearshore now or get steamrolled.

What changed

The EV boom was never real — it was a subsidy mirage. Hybrid electric vehicle manufacturing hit $108.2 billion in 2026, up 15.7% since 2021, but that growth stopped when Congress ended federal consumer tax credits on September 30, 2025. BEV market share dropped 1.9 percentage points in the following months, falling to 5.9% by July 2026. Sales fell 4% nationally — the first annual decline after a decade of growth. The Big Three automakers (GM, Ford, Toyota) collectively took $65 billion in EV-related writedowns through early 2026. GM alone took a $6 billion charge; Ford took $19.5 billion. Both delayed EV production and pivoted aggressively to hybrids. Toyota cut 2026 BEV production by one-third. This forced the real reckoning: electrification eliminates entire categories of legacy parts.

A conventional transmission disappears in every EV. Automobile transmission manufacturing was already shrinking — the market fell from $54.7 billion in 2022 to $44.6 billion in 2026, a 2.9% decline in 2026 alone. Employment in transmission manufacturing dropped 68,000 workers during 2025. But hybrids offer a temporary reprieve: hybrid sales surged 20.5% through mid-2026, and hybrids need efficient multi-speed transmissions. This created a two-tier industry: transmission makers with hybrid expertise survive; those betting only on EVs face obsolescence. Allison Transmission, recognizing the shift, acquired Dana's off-highway business for $2.7 billion in early 2026, capturing ~$120 million in annual synergies. Smaller players didn't make it. TransAxle, a New Jersey-based drivetrain remanufacturer, shut down all 13 locations in 2025, laying off 209 workers — no bankruptcy, just a permanent exit because there was no business left to restructure.

Then tariffs became the bigger killer than electrification. The average effective US tariff rate spiked from 2.2% at end-2024 to 17.0% by April 2025, with retaliatory duties specifically targeting US steering racks and control arms from China. Automobile steering and suspension manufacturing dropped to $17.7 billion in 2025, down 3.8% that year, from a $14.1 billion baseline in 2022. The industry has 250 US companies, down from more, and margins sit at 2.6% — thin enough that a 15-point tariff increase wipes out all profit. Suppliers faced an ugly choice: absorb the cost and destroy margins, or nearshore to Mexico and Europe to avoid duties and collapse domestic volume. USMCA labor-cost compliance surged from 44% in 2024 to 67% in 2025, forcing $16/hour minimum wages in Mexican plants. By early 2026, compliance exceeded 80%, which paradoxically made reshoring of some transmission component production to the US suddenly economic compared to China tariffs — but only for firms large enough to absorb the reshoring capex.

The existential threat for suppliers is steer-by-wire. Electric power steering became standard across most compact and mid-size vehicles by 2025, replacing hydraulic systems entirely. But that's just the transition. Steer-by-wire — fully electronic steering with zero mechanical backup — reached production milestones in 2026. Mercedes-Benz deployed ZF's Steer-by-Wire system beginning 2026. Nexteer launched Motion-by-Wire innovations in September 2025, including series-production Steer-by-Wire systems with ASIL D certification on Chinese new-energy vehicles. Nexteer posted $4.6 billion in 2025 revenue and is capturing the market. ZF Friedrichshafen posted €38.8 billion in 2025 sales with a 4.5% adjusted EBIT margin. Only firms with deep pockets and global reach can afford the software, sensors, and electronics R&D to stay in the game. Tenneco recognized this and sold its Ohlins Racing suspension brand to Brembo for $405 million in January 2025 to refocus on electric steering. Smaller suppliers can't. This is not disruption — it's erasure.

The evidence

  • Hybrid electric vehicle manufacturing: $108.2 billion in 2026, up 15.7% CAGR 2021-2026, but tariffs and tax credit expiration (Sept 30, 2025) triggered first sales decline in a decade
  • Transmission manufacturing: $44.6 billion in 2026, down 2.9% year-over-year, with 68,000 jobs lost in 2025 as EVs eliminate transmission demand
  • Tariffs: average effective US tariff rate spiked from 2.2% (end-2024) to 17.0% (April 2025), forcing nearshoring decisions for steering and suspension suppliers
  • Steering and suspension manufacturing: $17.7 billion in 2025, down 3.8% that year, with 250 US companies facing 2.6% margins crushed by tariffs
  • Steer-by-wire: Mercedes-Benz deployed ZF systems beginning 2026; Nexteer launched series-production systems in September 2025, signaling end of mechanical steering columns

Who rides it

Global tier-one suppliers with scale win. Nexteer, Allison Transmission, and ZF are consolidating the market. Nexteer posted $4.6 billion in 2025 revenue capturing steer-by-wire and motion-by-wire production. Allison acquired Dana's off-highway business for $2.7 billion in early 2026 and captured ~$120 million in annual synergies. ZF secured the Mercedes Steer-by-Wire contract for 2026 onward. Suppliers with scale can nearshore to Mexico under USMCA to avoid US tariffs (USMCA compliance jumped to 80% by early 2026), invest in electric steering R&D, and bid for the next-generation steer-by-wire contracts. The hybrid boom also buys them time: hybrid sales surged 20.5% through mid-2026, and hybrids need efficient multi-speed transmissions that only established firms can mass-produce.

Who's squeezed

Smaller regional suppliers and transmission-only specialists are crushed. TransAxle, a New Jersey-based drivetrain remanufacturer, shut down all 13 locations in 2025 and laid off 209 workers. Transmission manufacturing employment fell 68,000 in 2025 alone. Suppliers betting on EVs-only strategy face obsolescence; those without hybrid product lines lost volume as BEV sales collapsed 4% nationally after tax credits ended September 30, 2025. Domestic US steering and suspension makers lose to tariffs: a 17.0% tariff rate by April 2025 forces choices between absorbing losses (impossible at 2.6% margins) or nearshoring to Mexico (which collapses domestic volume). Mid-market firms like Tenneco had to sell off entire business units (Ohlins Racing to Brembo for $405 million in January 2025) just to stay solvent and refocus on surviving capabilities.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that electrification and tariffs are moving faster than supplier adaptation. Steer-by-wire production is ramping at Mercedes (ZF), ZF-China deals, and Nexteer contracts right now in 2026. Only firms that have already invested billions in electronic steering and sensor software since 2023-2024 can bid. But the tariff regime is shifting again: if tariffs spike further or retaliation escalates, even nearshored suppliers face supply-chain whiplash. USMCA compliance costs ($16/hour minimum wages in Mexico by 2025, 80%+ compliance by early 2026) are rising. And battery costs, which fell 8% in 2025 to $108/kWh, are volatile: tariffs on lithium-ion cells are up 25%, and China still dominates 80%+ of global battery production. A supplier that invests $500M in a Mexico steering plant, bets the farm on the hybrid cycle lasting through 2028, and then sees steer-by-wire adoption accelerate, EV sales rebound on cheaper batteries, or tariffs change again — that supplier goes under. Speed matters more than strategy now.
