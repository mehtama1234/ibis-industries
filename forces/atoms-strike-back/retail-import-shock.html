The retail import shock

Tariffs on clothing and furniture hit thin margins hard, collapsing brick-and-mortar chains while fast movers pivot to online and nearshore supply.

For decades, US retail rode on a simple machine: buy cheap from overseas, mark up thin. Tariffs—especially the August 2025 end of the de-minimis exemption that had let goods under $800 slip through duty-free—turned that machine off overnight. Clothing importers and furniture stores that made 2–5% net margins suddenly faced $136 extra costs per family just in tariff passes. The result wasn't subtle: a wave of bankruptcies, store closures, and a sharp split between retailers nimble enough to go online and those stuck in physical showrooms.

What changed

Tariffs made cheap imports suddenly expensive, and cheap imports were the entire business model. Women's clothing retail had been shrinking for years—down 0.3% annually even before tariffs hit—because fast-fashion platforms like Shein and Temu were already eating the mass-market's lunch with $3 t-shirts. Tariff costs just finished the job. The 25% duty on upholstered furniture, cabinets, and vanities (imposed October 2024, held at current rate through late 2026) forced choices: absorb the cost and watch your 3% margin evaporate, or raise prices and watch customers walk. Many chose neither and just shut down. Forever 21 filed Chapter 11 in March 2025 and liquidated all 354 stores. Liberated Brands (Volcom, Billabong, Quiksilver, Roxy, RVCA, Spyder) liquidated 122 stores. American Signature filed Chapter 11 in November 2025, closing Value City Furniture. Walker Edison Furniture filed in August 2025 and sold for a fire-sale $20 million.

The tariff wave also exposed that retail's moat was geography and shelf space, not supply chain resilience. Once shelf space became a liability—because you had to stock inventory when tariffs were in flux and customs delays made restocking risky—traditional retailers lost their edge. E-commerce platforms didn't have that problem. Wayfair captured 14% of the 2025 US furniture market; online furniture channels jumped to 28% of total market share by 2025, growing 5–10% annually. DTC brands are projected to hit $51 billion in US revenue by 2026. Women's clothing online sales hit $61.8 billion in 2025, nearly matched brick-and-mortar's $66.4 billion. Employment in online women's clothing grew 5.5% annually from 2019 to 2024, while brick-and-mortar employment kept shrinking.

The winners were retailers who could move fast: premium and specialty brands that hadn't built their entire model around volume and thin margins, and platforms that had already solved the last-mile logistics puzzle. Luxury and specialty women's retailers focused on affluent customers or niche segments (plus-size, athletic, sustainable) kept growing. They could absorb or pass tariff costs because their margins weren't 2–5%; they could also afford the geographic diversification that tariffs made necessary. Wayfair, DTC furniture makers, and Shein/Temu all had already rebuilt their supply chains around speed and scale outside traditional retail footprint, so tariffs just confirmed what they already knew: the future was logistics and omnichannel, not showrooms.

The losers were mass-market chains—independent retailers, mid-market players, and anyone who'd leaned on volume-at-low-margin as their entire strategy. Store closures hit 7,323 in 2024 and roughly 6,000 in the first half of 2025 alone. Macy's announced 150 stores over three years (66 announced January 2025). Housing-market weakness made it worse: new home sales dropped 17.6% month-over-month in January 2026 and 11.3% year-on-year, draining furniture demand just when retailers needed it most. Consumers, hit by tariff costs and inflation, traded down to cheaper goods, which meant Shein's $3 shirt beat everyone's $8 shirt. The bifurcation was brutal and fast. Tariffs didn't create the problem—e-commerce and ultra-low-cost platforms did—but they accelerated it beyond any retailer's ability to adapt except the ones who'd already adapted.

The evidence

  • Women's clothing retail in the US: $66.4B market (2026) declining at -0.3% annually with 2.3-5% net margins, hit by August 2025 tariff exemption end forcing $136 per-family cost pass
  • Furniture stores squeezed by 25% tariffs on upholstered goods (Oct 2024, held through late 2026) compressing 3% margins, while Wayfair captures 14% market share and online channels reach 28% of sales
  • Forever 21 bankruptcy liquidation (March 2025, all 354 stores), Liberated Brands liquidation (Feb 2025, 122 stores), American Signature Chapter 11 (Nov 2025) show mass-market retail collapse under tariff+e-commerce pressure
  • E-commerce women's clothing reached $61.8B (2025) nearly matching brick-and-mortar's $66.4B (2026), with online employment growing 5.5% annually while physical retail employment declined
  • Furniture market bifurcation: DTC brands projected $51B US revenue by 2026, online channels growing 5-10% annually, while new home sales dropped 17.6% month-over-month (Jan 2026) crushing traditional retailers

Who rides it

Online platforms and DTC brands won by having already rebuilt supply chains for speed and omnichannel logistics. Wayfair, Shein, Temu, and premium specialty retailers could either absorb tariff costs (if margins were fat enough) or pass them along (if their customers cared more about the product than the price). E-commerce furniture is projected to hit $51 billion by 2026; online women's clothing already crossed $61.8 billion in 2025. The winners weren't faster negotiators or smarter importers—they were the ones who'd already moved their entire operation away from traditional retail footprint and low-margin volume plays.

Who's squeezed

Mass-market brick-and-mortar chains and independent retailers got crushed. Thin margins—2-5% for women's clothing, 3% for furniture—left no room for tariff costs. Retailers couldn't absorb them, consumers wouldn't tolerate higher prices, and store closures accelerated: 7,323 in 2024, another 6,000 in the first half of 2025. Forever 21, Liberated Brands, American Signature, and Macy's (announcing 150 closures) all failed to adapt fast enough. Housing weakness made it worse for furniture: new home sales dropped 17.6% month-over-month in January 2026, draining the core demand engine.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that tariffs don't come down, and supply chains don't re-diversify—they just stay stuck in transition. That leaves US retailers in limbo: too expensive to import from Asia, too slow to build out nearshore capacity in Mexico or Southeast Asia, too dependent on physical showroom real estate in a shrinking market. If tariffs stay high and housing stays weak, furniture could look like women's clothing: a hollowed-out sector where only niche premium brands and ultra-low-cost e-commerce platforms survive, and the middle class buys nothing in between.

In one sentence

The retail import shock in a nutshell

When tariffs landed in 2025–2026, US apparel and furniture retailers discovered their supply chains were made of paper and their margins were thinner than they thought.
