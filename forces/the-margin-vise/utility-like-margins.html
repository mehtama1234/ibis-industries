C · Where the lock-in defends the margin

Utility-like resilience

Regulated or contracted essential services hold margins when competitors get squeezed.

When labor and materials and energy get expensive fast, and you can't just raise your price without asking permission, most businesses get crushed. But some industries have built walls. They operate under contracts, regulatory approval, or essential-service status that let them pass costs to customers or lock in margins for years. That structural protection is becoming worth more than operational efficiency.

What changed

The US waste-to-energy sector shows how this protection works in practice. The market is $1.4 billion in 2025, running 64 plants across 21 states, operated by just 9 companies with 1,870 workers. Margins sit at 18-25% EBITDA in 2025—almost double the baseline of 8.7% net profit in 2021. The reason: data centers are signing long-term renewable baseload power contracts. AI-focused data centers tripled power consumption in 2025, and tech companies signed 40% of all new corporate renewable energy PPAs. Those contracts lock in prices for waste-to-energy operators years in advance. Yes, landfill gas capture is eating into the sector—598 systems now produce 64% of US renewable gas, and an EPA methane rule in 2027 will push 705 more landfills toward gas extraction. But waste-to-energy margins stay fat because the contracts are there. Competition from cheaper alternatives (landfill gas runs 25-30% EBITDA margins) actually makes the locked-in contracts more valuable, not less. Reworld, the rebranded Covanta under EQT Infrastructure, processes 20.8 million tons yearly and is consolidating the industry, signaling that only operators big enough to hold long-term customer relationships survive.

Natural gas distribution shows the flip side. The US gas distribution market is $222.5 billion in 2025. It's fragmented—435 to 450 companies, ~99,000 workers—and operated by regulated utilities. Their margins should be stable. They're not. Natural gas prices jumped 60% from 2024 to 2025. Wholesale Henry Hub prices are forecast at $3.67/MMBtu for 2026 and $3.49 for 2027. The problem is structural: retail rates are regulated and move slowly through public utility commissions, but input costs spike overnight. In 2025, residential and commercial demand surged 7% to 22.5 Bcf/d due to weather and economic recovery. Industrial demand hit a record 23.6 Bcf/d, driven by manufacturing and the data center boom. Behind-the-meter natural gas generation—101 gigawatts of it—has been announced by hyperscalers bypassing the grid entirely. That demand surge should be good news for distributors. Instead, it's pressure. Regulators are adding fiber optic monitoring and methane controls to new pipe, and state climate targets (California wants 100% clean energy by 2045) directly contradict the utility business model that earns returns by building more gas infrastructure. The "utility-like" label protects distributors from price wars, but it also locks them into a rate-base model that's under siege from policy.

What's scarce now is optionality. Waste-to-energy margins stayed wide because they locked in demand early through contracts; gas distribution margins compress despite the "regulated utility" label because regulation is asymmetric—prices go up for inputs, down for outputs, and policy is turning against the business. Energy transition risk is real. Updated methane rules (2027), LNG export surges anchoring high prices abroad, and data center electricity demand all shift the ground. But the companies that win are those that can lock in long-term customer commitments at predictable prices. Reworld signals this by consolidating regionally and shifting from incineration to materials recovery and alternative fuels—moving toward customers with longer-term needs. Gas distributors are trapped: essential service status means you can't walk away, but it also means you can't raise prices fast enough to match costs. April 2025 saw Chesapeake Utilities merge its three Maryland gas distribution companies into one unified operator; consolidation is the only lever left when regulation breaks.

The evidence

  • Waste-to-energy operators hold 18-25% EBITDA margins in 2025 because data centers signed 40% of new corporate renewable energy PPAs, locking in long-term baseload contracts.
  • Natural gas distributors (US market $222.5B, 2025) see margins compressed despite regulated status because input costs (Henry Hub $3.67/MMBtu forecast 2026) spike faster than retail rate adjustments.
  • Data center electricity demand surged 17% in 2025, with AI facilities tripling power use, creating new demand for contracted baseload capacity that protects waste-to-energy margins but leaves flexible gas distributors exposed.
  • Behind-the-meter natural gas generation (101 GW announced) bypasses traditional distribution utilities, fragmenting the once-stable rate-base model and forcing consolidation (Chesapeake Utilities, April 2025).
  • Updated EPA methane rule (August 2025) pushes 705 additional landfills toward gas extraction, intensifying competition for waste-to-energy but reinforcing that only consolidated, contract-focused operators survive.

Who rides it

Waste-to-energy operators and regional gas consolidators that lock in long-term contracts and can pass through costs under regulation or PPAs. Reworld, the consolidated waste operator processing 20.8 million tons annually, wins by shifting toward materials recovery and alternative fuels—moving away from pure incineration toward customers with longer-term, stickier demand. Hyperscalers win by announcing 101 GW of behind-the-meter generation, circumventing grid constraints and forcing utilities to negotiate. Consolidated regional distributors (like Chesapeake after its 2025 merger) win by reducing operational complexity and bargaining power with regulators.

Who's squeezed

Fragmented, small-footprint waste operators that lack contracts or scale to negotiate with data center buyers. Traditional natural gas distributors caught between regulated retail rates and unregulated input costs—they can't raise prices fast enough to match the 60% surge in wholesale costs from 2024 to 2025. Independent landfill operators face margin pressure as 598 landfill gas systems now produce 64% of renewable natural gas, with 20 new projects adding 39.9 Bcf capacity in 2025 alone. Utilities serving areas with state climate targets conflicting with gas infrastructure earn returns on assets that policy is actively attempting to strand.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The regulatory protection that makes utilities "resilient" is eroding faster than expected. Data center demand (101 GW behind-the-meter) bypasses utilities entirely, fragmenting the rate base they depend on. State climate policy (California 100% clean energy by 2045) directly contradicts the gas utility business model. Updated EPA methane rules (2027) force capital spending on monitoring without guaranteed rate recovery. If regulators can't or won't raise retail rates in line with wholesale costs, the utility-like businesses become margin vises themselves—essential but unprofitable.

In one sentence

Utility-like resilience in a nutshell

Businesses that lock in long-term contracts or operate under regulatory price protection survive margin compression that destroys flexible competitors.
