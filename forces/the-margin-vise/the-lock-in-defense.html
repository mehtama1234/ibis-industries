The lock-in defense

Where captive locations and bundles defend margin when switching is hard.

When competitors circle and costs rise, businesses with customers who can't easily leave have a huge advantage. Cable broadband isn't thriving—it's under siege by fiber and 5G home internet, yet cable providers still defend pricing and margin because most households have no real alternative. Vending machine operators in captive locations (offices, transit hubs) do the same: they charge differently where exits are expensive.

What changed

The margin vise squeezes hardest when pricing power has already eroded elsewhere. Cable providers face a brutal two-front collapse: cable TV subscriptions dropped -2.6% annually through 2025-2026, accelerated by price-sensitive households (only 16% of those under 50 still subscribe, versus 64% of over-65 demographics). That was supposed to be fixed by broadband growth—except broadband turned negative in 2025 for the first time in 30 years. Fiber now captures 27% of the broadband market, while T-Mobile's fixed wireless access alone added 1.8M customers in 2025 and hit 8M+ by late 2025; AT&T added 900K FWA customers. Yet cable margins stayed thin at 8.6–9.8%, and instead of competing on price or service, the industry consolidated: Charter and Comcast approved a $34.5B merger in March 2026 (expected to close summer 2026) that creates a 38M+ customer bundle across 41 states. The bundle is the lock-in—you don't buy broadband alone; you're offered bundled video, phone, and internet, making switching costs psychologically and administratively higher. Meanwhile, vending operators face direct headwinds: the $7.9B market shrank -1.6% CAGR through 2026, soda consumption per capita fell, and work-from-home never reversed (staying at 44% hybrid in 2025-2026, not pre-pandemic). The industry's response wasn't to compete on price; it was to weaponize location. Cashless machines now command 58% market share (2025), allowing dynamic pricing: average transaction value rose from $1.78 to $2.24, a 26% lift. Micromarkets—unattended self-checkout kiosks in high-value spots—generate $20/month per customer versus $7 at traditional vending, but they only work in captive locations (offices, transit hubs) where exits are real-time-cost-prohibitive. Top 20 vending operators consolidated 40+ regional players in 2023-2025, cementing control of the best locations. The lock-in, again, is location: if you're hungry or thirsty at the right machine, you pay what the operator charges.

In both industries, lock-in emerges because head-to-head price competition would destroy margin entirely. Cable broadband is technically obsolete (fiber is faster, 5G home internet is cheaper), but bundling with video and phone—services many households still want—forces customers to pay the bundle or defect entirely, and defecting means switching your phone number and TV provider too. The risk of losing everything motivates acceptance of price increases on the broadband line. Vending follows the same logic: a machine in a transit hub or corporate cafeteria can raise prices 26% when cashless payment removes friction and the customer can't easily exit to a store—the cost of leaving the workplace, missing transit time, or hiking to a competing location is higher than paying the 50-cent markup. This is not brand loyalty or product quality. This is geography and architecture made expensive.

What the margin vise made scarce is *undefended shelf space*. When costs rise faster than revenue, only locations where competitors can't easily build defensible positions stay profitable. Cable defended itself through consolidation, bundling, and network duopolies (the last-mile fiber infrastructure remains hard to replicate). Vending defended itself through operator consolidation (top 20 now control the best captive locations) and the shift to cashless, dynamic pricing (which amplifies power in high-exit-cost locations). Neither industry made the customer better off. Cable subscribers in bundled markets now pay more for declining video quality and service while the company underfunds network maintenance to manage margin. Vending customers in transit hubs pay 26% more than pre-cashless averages for the same bag of chips, with no option to walk out.

The evidence

  • Cable providers (Media & Entertainment): Cable broadband subscriber losses in 2025 for the first time in 30 years, yet margins defended at 8.6–9.8% through bundling strategy; Charter-Cox $34.5B merger (March 2026) consolidates 38M+ customers across 41 states.
  • Fixed wireless access (Telecommunications): T-Mobile added 1.8M FWA customers in 2025; AT&T added 900K; FWA now serves 7–8% of US broadband market, yet cable still holds pricing power through bundle lock-in.
  • Vending machine operators (Retail): Cashless payment rose to 58% market share (2025), enabling 26% transaction value lift ($1.78→$2.24); micromarkets in captive locations earn $20/month per customer versus $7 at traditional vending.
  • Vending consolidation (Retail): Top 20 global operators consolidated 40+ regional acquisitions (2023-2025), controlling high-value captive locations (offices, transit hubs) where exit costs are high.
  • Work-from-home stasis (Workplace): Hybrid work remained at 44% (2025-2026), never reverting to pre-pandemic norms, yet vending in captive locations (offices, transit) maintained pricing power through location lock-in despite declining foot traffic.

Who rides it

Cable providers and regional broadband incumbents win by bundling declining services (video) with essential ones (broadband and phone). Switching costs—changing phone numbers, TV provider, and internet in one move—outweigh the price premium customers now pay. Vending operators win by controlling captive locations and consolidating the best spots; top 20 operators absorbed 40+ regional players to cement their hold. Both make money not by improving the product but by making the exit door more expensive than staying. Charter's $34.5B merger works because 38M+ bundled customers across 41 states can't easily switch without disrupting their entire household. A micromarket in a corporate cafeteria works because you're hungry *right now* and walking three blocks to a grocery store costs more time than paying the 50-cent markup.

Who's squeezed

Customers in bundled cable markets and captive vending locations lose. Cable subscribers pay higher rates for declining video quality and service (the price increases fund margin defense, not investment). Younger households especially lose—only 16% of under-50 cable TV subscribers remain, yet they're forced into bundled internet packages where they subsidize video losses with broadband overpayment. Vending customers lose to dynamic pricing: cashless payment enabled a 26% rise in transaction value, benefiting operators but shrinking customer surplus. Smaller regional cable providers and independent vending operators lose through consolidation: Charter-Cox creates a 38M+ duopoly that can raise rates with minimal competition, and top 20 vending operators now control the micromarket upside that smaller players can't access. The retail landscape becomes less competitive, not more efficient.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that lock-in margin defense is inherently temporary. Cable broadband lost customers to fiber and 5G for the first time in 2025 precisely because the bundle no longer justifies the price—video is dying, phone service is commodity, and pure internet-only entrants (fiber providers, 5G home internet) now offer the core service without the markup. As fiber deployment accelerates and FWA coverage expands, even bundled customers will defect. Vending micromarkets face a similar cliff: if workplace return accelerates or if mobile payment and delivery apps (DoorDash, Uber Eats) in office buildings compete for the same captive customer, the $20/month premium evaporates instantly. The lock-in works only as long as the exit actually costs more than the stay—the moment it doesn't, margin collapses.

In one sentence

The lock-in defense in a nutshell

Lock-in defends margin by making the exit door more expensive than the stay—bundle services, consolidate captive locations, and control geography—but it buys time, not permanence, as newer competitors enter the same space.
