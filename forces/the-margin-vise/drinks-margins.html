The drinks squeeze

Tariffs, aluminum, labor, and collapsing demand make beverages impossible to price at the old margins.

A beer costs what it cost three years ago, but the aluminum can is 15 percent more expensive. Workers at breweries and distributors demand $28 to $30 an hour. Consumers drink 20 percent less juice than they did five years ago and are switching to hard seltzers and spirits RTDs instead. The middle cannot raise prices fast enough.

What changed

The drinks industry is experiencing a three-front squeeze that has no easy escape. First, tariffs arrived fast and hit hardest on packaging. In April 2025, a 25 percent tariff on aluminum cans went into effect. Aluminum itself was already up 15 percent year-over-year as of early 2025, so the combined hit pushed packaging costs up double digits overnight. A craft brewery making 20,000 barrels a year cannot absorb a 20 percent jump in can costs and stay profitable at a $6 or $7 retail price. The large breweries—Molson Coors, Anheuser-Busch—have pricing power and scale to negotiate tariff costs or shift to bottled beer (which faces lower tariffs). The 481 craft brewery closures in 2025 and the bankruptcy of names like Duck-Rabbit and Alamo Beer happened because margins went negative, not because their beer was bad.

Second, labor costs are climbing in a shrinking industry. Beer wholesalers saw wage pressure spike 6.4 percent to $28.89 per hour in March 2026, even as total employment ticked down 0.6 percent and volumes fell 5.7 percent in 2025. That is the worst dynamic: fewer workers demanded, but they cost more. Brewery jobs were down 3 percent year-over-year as of 2025, with 191,000 workers left in the craft segment, but wages still climbed because the skilled roles—head brewer, packaging line operator, QA—could not be filled. A 500-barrel-per-month brewery cannot cut a full brewer's shift and still make good beer. It pays the wage or closes.

Third, category collapse is cannibalizing beer's share. Spirits-based RTD cocktails surged 16.4 percent to $3.8 billion in 2025, and they completely bypass traditional beer wholesalers—they go through wine and spirits channels instead. That shift means beer volumes fall at the wholesaler level even if production is stable, so fixed costs (dispatch, warehouse, cold-chain) get spread over fewer cases per route. The wholesaler's margin, already thin at 4.8 to 5.0 percent of revenue in 2026, gets thinner still. Juice faces a different kind of demand cliff. Americans cut juice consumption by 20 percent from 2019 to 2024, viewing it as just another sugary drink despite being 100 percent fruit. The FDA's August 2025 proposal to lower the orange juice sugar standard from 10.5 percent to 10 percent Brix—the first standard cut since 1963—signaled regulatory surrender on traditional juice. Orange juice fell from 67 percent of juice beverage share in 2022 to 62 percent in 2024. The only juice segment growing is cold-pressed and premium, expanding at 8 percent annually as health-conscious consumers pay more for minimal processing. The mass market juice maker watching their 11 percent EBIT margin compress cannot compete there.

The evidence

  • Breweries: 481 closures in 2025; Duck-Rabbit Craft Brewery liquidated (Chapter 7) and Alamo Beer Company filed Chapter 11 (February–April 2025) as aluminum tariffs and margin compression accelerated.
  • Beer wholesaling: Wage pressure spiked 6.4% to $28.89/hour in March 2026 while beer volumes fell 5.7% in 2025 and RTDs surged 16.4% to $3.8 billion, fragmenting the three-tier distribution model.
  • Juice production: Americans cut juice consumption 20% from 2019 to 2024, and FDA proposed lowering orange juice sugar standard from 10.5% to 10% Brix in August 2025—first cut since 1963.
  • Aluminum: Up 15% year-over-year with an additional 25% tariff on cans (April 2025) and 25% on aluminum imports, pushing packaging costs up double digits for mid-sized makers.
  • Consolidation: 46 M&A deals across beverage alcohol in 2026 (YTD) vs. 26 in 2025; large players like Molson Coors gain premium segment share while mid-sized independents close or pivot to premium/experiential models.

Who rides it

Large breweries with scale and automation—Molson Coors, Anheuser-Busch—can negotiate tariff costs, shift to bottled imports (which face lower tariffs), and raise prices in the premium segment (Above Premium now 28 percent of Molson Coors net sales, up from 23 percent three years prior). Consolidators and private equity buying distressed regional breweries also win because they can integrate packaging, cut labor overhead, and repricing the brands. Coffee and premium beverage makers win by pivoting away from traditional juice and RTDs—Keurig Dr Pepper's $4 billion acquisition of JDE Peet's (closing expected Q2 2026) signals the exit from margin-squeezed juice into higher-margin coffee and specialty beverages.

Who's squeezed

Mid-sized regional breweries, craft independents, and traditional juice makers get squeezed from both ends. Craft breweries fell 4 percent in production in H1 2026, with 9,344 operating breweries down 1.8 percent year-over-year. The 481 closures in 2025 are happening at this tier because they lack scale to absorb tariffs, cannot negotiate on cans or aluminum, and face an increasingly automated supply chain they cannot match. Juice makers face structural demand collapse as consumers view juice as unhealthy (20 percent consumption drop since 2019) and regulatory pressure (FDA sugar standards) makes the mass-market business unprofitable. Beer wholesalers are trapped: volumes shrink, labor costs climb, and RTD growth bypasses them entirely.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that tariff policies become permanent before the supply chain diversifies. Mexican beer and juice are highly tariffed, but 61.5 percent of beer trade is bottled or draft, which faces lower tariffs—so large players will shift format and leave canned craft beer to absorb the 25 percent cost alone. If tariffs stay and aluminum stays high, the US craft beer industry could shrink from 9,344 to under 8,000 breweries within two years, consolidating production into 15 to 20 national brands. Smaller makers cannot compete at any scale, and the experiential model (taproom-first, food programs) only works in high-rent urban and resort markets, not in regional towns where 70 percent of small breweries operate.

In one sentence

The drinks squeeze in a nutshell

In 2025-2026, input costs on labor and tariffed packaging rose too fast for beer, juice, and soda makers to raise prices, so margins vanished and hundreds of mid-sized players closed.
