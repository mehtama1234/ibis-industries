The Sweet-Goods Squeeze

Cocoa, sugar, and dairy tariffs crush indulgent-category margins, leaving only premium brands and automation escapes.

The candy, ice cream, and chocolate industries are not dying—they're restructuring around a brutal math. Tariffs jumped costs 30% since late 2024, but consumer willingness to pay rose only 10-20%, compressing margins that were already thin. The industry is splitting into winners (premium, automation, locked-in scale) and losers (middle-market brands with no pricing power).

What changed

Tariffs transformed sweet-goods economics overnight. In April 2025, the U.S. imposed 10% baseline tariffs on all imports, 20% on EU goods, 31% on Swiss chocolate, and 145% on bulk shipments from China. For chocolate makers, cocoa tariffs hit 21% on Ivory Coast imports and 10% on Ghana—the world's top two suppliers. Overnight, a chocolate producer's input costs jumped 30% between December 2024 and September 2025. Consumers did notice and resent it—86% of candy buyers now report seeing price hikes they notice and dislike. But they didn't double their spending; they simply paid 10-20% more for chocolate in 2025 versus 2024, with another 14% year-over-year increase in early 2026. That gap—30% cost inflation against 14-20% price pass-through—is the vise closing on the middle.

The squeeze is most visible in candy and ice cream. The U.S. candy market is $55 billion (2025), projected to grow just 1.4% annually through 2030. Margins in candy production, which sat at 4.5-4.7% in 2022, are now under structural pressure with no recovery in sight. The ice cream sector, worth $20 billion (2026), is growing only 2.66% annually. Both categories saw their tariff bills explode while consumer demand stayed flat. Chocolate fared slightly better—still a $28.9 billion market growing 4.58% through 2031—but only because chocolate is consolidating upmarket. Hershey, the dominant mass-market player with 33.5% share ($11.7 billion revenue 2025), is locked into retail channels where it has no pricing power against Walmart and Amazon. In contrast, single-origin and craft chocolate are accelerating at 10.15% and growing substantially via direct-to-consumer e-commerce (7.12% CAGR). The premium escape hatch is real: craft chocolate earned $842 million by 2032 and captures 40% higher purchase intent from younger consumers who will pay for story and quality.

What did this force make scarce and valuable? Premium positioning and automation. Unilever spun off its ice cream division as The Magnum Ice Cream Company (valued $9.1 billion, traded December 2025) precisely because ice cream is no longer a growth engine for conglomerates—but premium frozen desserts are growing. Plant-based ice cream jumped from $4.25 billion (2025) to $5.15 billion (2026), a 21% CAGR, as consumers trade regular bulk ice cream for higher-margin novelties. Single-serve impulse products now represent 42% of the ice cream market and are thriving. Meanwhile, technology adoption—3D printing, AI-driven coating sensors, IoT real-time monitoring—is accelerating in chocolate to reduce labor and yield variance, but adoption is uneven and expensive. Scale became newly valuable too: Mars spent $35.9 billion acquiring Kellanov (December 2025) to absorb Pringles, Pop-Tarts, and Cheez-It into its portfolio, a consolidation move driven by fear that only the very largest players can weather tariff shocks. Smaller players—like Kate Weiser Chocolate, a 12-year-old Dallas artisan brand—simply closed in April 2025, citing "the economy." What is now scarce: mid-market independence, low-tech profitability, and bulk commodity margins.

What did this force make cheap? Labor in manual confectionery and the ability to import finished goods at will. Tariffs inverted the economics of foreign manufacturing. When 31% tariffs on Swiss chocolate and 145% on Chinese bulk shipments kicked in, it became uneconomical for smaller U.S. brands to import finished products or semi-finished fills. Some producers shifted to lower-cost domestic sourcing or in-house automation to replace hand-packing. But labor itself (wages, benefits, turnover) did not cheapen—only the value of manual work diminished relative to automated alternatives. The industry employed roughly 24,500 in candy, 23,200 in ice cream, and 50,000 in chocolate (2022 baseline), with only 1% annual growth projected. Automation became the cheaper lever, not headcount cuts.

The evidence

  • Candy (2025): U.S. candy market at $55 billion growing only 1.4% annualized through 2030; tariffs and input-cost inflation compressing 4.5-4.7% baseline margins.
  • Ice cream (2026): $20 billion market growing 2.66% annually; plant-based segment exploding at 21% CAGR, while bulk ice cream declines; Americans' ice cream spending surged 26.5% month-over-month in May 2025 (inflation pass-through).
  • Chocolate (2026): $28.9 billion market growing 4.58% through 2031, but bifurcated—mass retail (Hershey 33.5% share) compressed, craft chocolate and single-origin accelerating at 10.15% CAGR, e-commerce at 7.12%.
  • Tariffs (2025-2026): 10% baseline, 20% EU, 31% Switzerland, 145% China; cocoa tariffs 21% Ivory Coast, 10% Ghana; chocolate input costs +30% Dec 2024–Sept 2025; early 2026 pricing +14% YoY against 30% cost inflation.
  • M&A (2025): Mars closed $35.9B Kellanov acquisition (December 2025); Unilever spun off $9.1B Magnum ice cream (December 2025); consolidation driven by tariff shock resilience and scale economics.

Who rides it

Premium, automation, and locked-in scale are escaping the squeeze. Unilever's spinoff of The Magnum Company signals that CPG giants can still profit in ice cream if they own the premium end; plant-based ice cream is the growth lever (21% CAGR), and single-serve impulse products (42% of market) thrive. Craft chocolate at $842 million (2032) captures 40% higher consumer intent and bypasses retail middlemen via direct-to-consumer e-commerce. Mars's $35.9 billion acquisition of Kellanov signals that only the very largest can absorb tariff shocks and restructure; smaller players who invest in automation (3D printing, AI coating sensors, IoT monitoring) are squeezing margin back via yield and labor reduction. Ferrara's launch of freeze-dried SweeTARTS and Lemonheads (August 2025) and M&Ms' POP'd Caramel (early 2026) both exploit a real trend: freeze-dried candy jumped from niche to $1.31 billion globally (2025) and is forecast to grow 12% annually through 2033. Unilever's $1.2 billion acquisition of Grüns functional gummy brand (April 2026) buys positioning in the 47% of regular candy buyers who now seek healthier alternatives—a escape from commodity pricing.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market, import-dependent, and labor-heavy producers are squeezed between tariff costs and consumer price resistance. A brand with no direct-to-consumer presence and retail-only distribution (like mid-size regional candy makers or smaller ice cream producers) faces a brutal choice: absorb the 30% cost shock and collapse margin, or raise prices 14-20% and lose volume as consumers trade down or switch brands. The 760 ice cream establishments in 2022 are consolidating via M&A; smaller players lack the capital to invest in automation or build premium positioning. Kate Weiser Chocolate, a 12-year-old Dallas artisan, simply closed in April 2025, citing "the economy"—but the real story is that without scale or direct-to-consumer reach, tariff shocks are unrecoverable. Hershey, despite its 33.5% market share, is locked into mass retail (Walmart, Target, grocery chains) where Walmart and Amazon dictate margins. Import-dependent producers who relied on finished goods or semi-finished fills from Switzerland (31% tariff) or China (145% tariff) face immediate uneconomical unit economics.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The structural bifurcation of the sweet-goods market into a premium top tier and a low-margin commodity bottom tier could tip into permanent capacity contraction and job losses. If tariffs persist through 2027-2028 and middle-market brands continue to fail, consolidation into 3-5 mega-players (Mars, Mondelēz, Hershey, Nestlé, Ferrero) will accelerate. The U.S. confectionery and ice cream industries employ roughly 100,000 workers combined (candy ~24,500, ice cream ~23,200, chocolate ~50,000 as of 2022), and most of those jobs are in manufacturing and packing. If automation becomes the only lever for profitability and tariffs force further margin compression, the incentive to automate aggressively will outpace new hiring. Second, the rise of health-conscious alternatives (47% of candy buyers now seek healthier options) and plant-based ice cream (21% CAGR) may be cannibalizing the traditional indulgent categories faster than margin compression alone explains. If consumption volume declines on top of margin pressure, even the big players will be restructuring for a permanently smaller market. Third, retaliatory tariffs on U.S. ice cream exports to China (125% tariff in 2026) are already capping export upside and stranding capacity.

In one sentence

The sweet-goods squeeze in a nutshell

Tariffs forced a 30% cost spike in sweet goods that consumers won't fully absorb, so only premium brands, automation, and mega-scale players escape the margin vise—mid-market producers are exiting or consolidating.
