C · Who's holding on

Omnichannel or die

Big-box retailers survive the channel shift only by fusing online and store — ship-from-store, buy-online-pickup-in-store — or they fold.

Physical retail isn't disappearing. But the store's job has changed. It's no longer just a place to buy. It's a warehouse, a fulfillment hub, a place to see and touch before you commit. Retailers who treat their stores as part of a seamless online-offline system are holding margins and traffic. Those who don't are closing stores and losing ground to Amazon, marketplaces, and DTC brands.

What changed

The channel shift made scarcity out of *velocity*. A product sitting on a shelf in a store that only sells in-person is now a missed transaction — someone bought it online instead, or used it for research and purchased elsewhere. Omnichannel became a survival mechanism, not a luxury. Home Depot laid off 800 corporate staff and closed distribution facilities in Mexico, Missouri, and Tennessee in 2025-2026 to rebuild supply chains around ship-from-store logistics. Lowe's saw this coming and doubled down: professional services revenue surged 15% in 2024, a segment that depends on stores as hubs for consultation, staging, and same-day delivery. This wasn't about growing bigger — it was about making stores *do more*.

The cost structure shifted too. E-commerce didn't just take sales; it raised the floor on what customers expect. Amazon claimed 12% of online DIY sales ($6.1 billion in 2024, up 25% year-over-year), which forced Home Depot and Lowe's to match both price and speed. Smaller chains couldn't absorb this. Wren Kitchens, a $1+ billion kitchen specialty retailer, filed for bankruptcy liquidation and shuttered all 15 US locations in 2025. The home improvement store industry is flat — just 2.3% growth in 2025, barely 2% expected for 2026 — which means every competitor is fighting for the same dollar with higher friction costs. Meanwhile, sporting goods retail faced the same squeeze: Dick's Sporting Goods bought Foot Locker for $2.4 billion (announced May 2025, closed September 2025), merging 3,200+ stores globally to consolidate back-office, close redundant locations, and build a unified omnichannel engine. Online sporting goods sales reached $39.2 billion in 2025, but brick-and-mortar still accounts for the majority — which made consolidation a rational move to share fulfillment and service costs across more locations.

Auto parts retail shows the darkest version of this. The $77.1 billion market (2026) grew only 1.6% last year, and tariff volatility (25% on Chinese parts from April 2025, then a 90-day truce down to 30% in May, then ongoing uncertainty) made planning impossible. O'Reilly Automotive, the market leader, posted an 8.1% comparable-store-sales increase in Q1 2026 and diluted earnings per share up 16% year-over-year — but that required *scale*, tight inventory discipline, and integrated online fulfillment. Smaller competitors didn't have it. First Brands, a major supplier, filed Chapter 11 in 2025; Marelli (a Japanese parts group) also filed. Online auto parts sales (excluding marketplaces) reached $23 billion in 2025 and are forecast to grow 4.6% annually, while Amazon and eBay/Walmart push that to 6.7% when included. The store became valuable only if it could fulfill fast and keep costs low — which meant omnichannel or liquidation.

Tariffs and labor costs amplified the pain. Kitchen cabinet tariffs hit 15-20% in home improvement; material costs overall rose 4-6%, labor costs 6-8% annually. This margin compression made scale essential — and made omnichannel the only viable way to push higher-margin services (like Lowe's professional segment) while absorbing commodity-price and wage inflation in stores. Retailers who couldn't build unified demand signals, inventory, and fulfillment across channels became cost-uncompetitive.

The evidence

  • Home improvement retail: Amazon reached $6.1 billion in sales in 2024, up 25% year-over-year, while Lowe's professional services revenue jumped 15% in 2024 only by building omnichannel service delivery
  • Sporting goods retail: Dick's acquired Foot Locker for $2.4 billion (2025) to consolidate 3,200+ stores into one omnichannel system, as online sales hit $39.2 billion but brick-and-mortar remained critical
  • Auto parts retail: O'Reilly Automotive's comparable-store sales rose 8.1% in Q1 2026 through integrated fulfillment, while smaller competitors (First Brands, Marelli) filed for bankruptcy as tariffs and e-commerce squeezed margins
  • Home improvement stores: Market growth was flat at 2.3% in 2025; Home Depot laid off 800 staff and closed distribution facilities in 2025-2026, restructuring for omnichannel logistics
  • Sporting goods retail: Transactions declined 9% in Q4 2025-Q1 2026 due to tariff pass-through, forcing consolidation and profitability-through-scale strategies among survivors

Who rides it

Dick's Sporting Goods, O'Reilly Automotive, and Lowe's are the survivors. They had scale, technology spend, and the balance sheet to absorb tariff costs, build unified omnichannel fulfillment, and close redundant stores without destroying value. Dick's integration of Foot Locker, though costly, gave it a bigger store network to spread fulfillment and professional-service costs; O'Reilly's 8.1% comp growth in Q1 2026 shows that omnichannel done well can still squeeze margin expansion even in a flat-growth market. Lowe's professional services surge is proof that stores become profit centers when they're hubs for higher-margin services tied to online visibility and quick fulfillment. Amazon and online-pure-play brands also win — they never had the store-cargo problem and are systematizing the data advantage (buy online, ship from warehouse, show customer reviews). But the real winner is the integrated operator with 500+ locations, because they can amortize omnichannel infrastructure and absorb one-time restructuring costs.

Who's squeezed

Smaller chains and single-format operators get crushed. Wren Kitchens closed all 15 US locations in 2025. At Home, Miller's Hardware, and countless independent auto parts shops either liquidated or sold to consolidators. Foot Locker was the classic case: strong brand, but 4,000+ US locations optimized for mall traffic and wholesale-driven apparel, not omnichannel. It became acquisition prey for Dick's, which needed the real estate but had to write off redundancy and integration costs. Middle-income consumers also lose — tariffs on kitchen cabinets (15-20%), athletic apparel (10%+), and auto parts (fluctuating 25%-30%) get passed through, and the store's inability to compete on price or speed means less shopping overall. Workers in small retail shops face job cuts as stores close and consolidation automates back-office.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that omnichannel only delays the inevitable for legacy retailers. Tariffs, labor costs, and consumer retrenchment are structural headwinds. Home Depot foot traffic was flat to negative (−0.08% in Q1 2026) even as it invested in omnichannel. Sporting goods transactions fell 9% in early 2026. If consumer discretionary spending doesn't recover and tariffs stay elevated or get worse, even omnichannel scale may not be enough to support current store counts. The auto parts industry is the warning signal: electric vehicles structurally shrink the repair basket per vehicle (no oil changes, simpler drivetrains, regenerative braking), so adding scale and omnichannel logistics just slows the decline, it doesn't reverse it. A $77.1 billion market shrinking at 1.6% growth is still shrinking.

In one sentence

Omnichannel or die in a nutshell

Omnichannel is not a growth strategy — it is the price of survival, and only retailers with scale and margin discipline can afford to pay it.
