C · Who's holding on

The experiential survivor

Expertise, immediacy, and in-person experience are the moats physical retail has left.

Physical stores are losing the commodity battle. You can get the thing cheaper online. But you can't get expertise, try-on, customization, or instant gratification through a screen. The stores that survive are the ones doing something e-commerce can't—and charging for it.

What changed

The channel shift has created a hard fork in retail. Brick-and-mortar jewelry stores contracted 12.1% in 2025 and 2.2% in 2026, with 167 US jewelry stores closing in just Q1 2025—a 21% increase from Q1 2024. The culprits are structural: gold prices surged 60% annually in 2025, peaking near $4,400 per ounce in October, pushing entry-level jewelry from $99 to $200-$500 and pricing out the mass market entirely. Tariffs on Chinese jewelry imports hit 145% by late 2025, forcing wholesale costs up 18-22% for retailers. But the real story is that online jewelry channels now capture an estimated 25% of global purchases, with mobile commerce accounting for over 60% of those transactions. Lab-grown diamonds have become the default—capturing 56.8% of the US engagement ring market as of late 2025—which means the floor for store visits isn't authenticity anymore, it's customization and styling. Stores like Claire's and Lugano Diamonds have filed for bankruptcy (Claire's in August 2025, its second bankruptcy in seven years) because they tried to compete on price in a channel shift world. The survivors aren't fighting that war.

Health stores face an identical squeeze. The brick-and-mortar health store sector grew only 3.8% year-over-year in 2025, while online supplements grew 11.4% annually. The Vitamin Shoppe and GNC—the two largest regional chains—both went through bankruptcy proceedings, with Kingswood Capital Management acquiring The Vitamin Shoppe in April 2025 for $193.5 million after Franchise Group's collapse. Tariffs on imported ingredients hit 10-34% depending on origin (China at 34%, Taiwan 32%), affecting 75-80% of imported raw materials used in vitamin and supplement manufacturing. Consumers buy vitamins from Amazon now, often not differentiating between generic and specialty brands. But personalized nutrition—subscription boxes, custom gummies, targeted formulations—is growing at 15% annually and specialty retailers are the only format equipped to stock and consult on it. The market is bifurcating: commodity supplements are being obliterated by Amazon and Target's private-label lines, but curated, personalized, and advice-driven health retail survives because the value isn't the product—it's the recommendation.

Convenience stores are the sharpest case study in what keeps stores alive. The US convenience store sector is massive ($43.7 billion in 2026) but nearly flat—just 0.2% CAGR from 2021-2026—because fuel margins are collapsing and organized retail crime is eating away at profit. Fuel sales fell to $476.3 billion in 2025 from $501.9 billion in 2024 as gas prices fell 5.9%. But prepared foodservice is now 39% of in-store profit and remains the only high-margin growth lever. 80% of operators expect foodservice to improve through 2026, betting on differentiation—think of this as betting on experience, convenience, and customization (hot sandwiches made to order, local pastries, regional specialties) rather than shelf inventory. The successful c-store chains are those adding experiential layers: Pilot Flying J is expanding EV charging to 50 new sites in H1 2026, turning the fuel stop into a destination with a 20-30 minute dwell time. Operators using AI-driven fuel pricing report 3.4% profit gains, which means the margin game itself is becoming an experience edge (knowing you're getting the deal). The failed mega-consolidation—Couche-Tard's $47.2 billion bid for 7-Eleven withdrew in June 2025—signals that scale doesn't solve the channel shift. Smaller, locally-optimized operators with strong foodservice and community presence are outperforming.

What the channel shift has made scarce is the commodity store itself. What it's made valuable is expertise, curation, and immediacy—especially for products where you want to try, touch, or talk to someone. Jewelry stores that survive now position themselves as design studios and custom workshops, not inventory warehouses. Health stores that survive are consultancies bundled with products. Convenience stores that survive are food destinations with fuel attached. The experiential moat is real, but it only works if you stop fighting e-commerce on its terms and start competing on presence, knowledge, and the thing you get right now instead of in two days.

The evidence

  • Jewelry: US market contracted 12.1% in 2025 and 2.2% in 2026; gold prices surged 60% annually in 2025 to $4,400/oz; 145% tariffs on Chinese imports; Claire's filed bankruptcy August 2025 (second in seven years)
  • Health Stores: Market grew only 3.8% in 2025 vs. 11.4% online; The Vitamin Shoppe acquired by Kingswood for $193.5M in April 2025 after Franchise Group bankruptcy; tariffs hit 10-34% on imported supplements (China 34%)
  • Convenience Stores: $43.7B market in 2026, flat growth (0.2% CAGR); fuel sales fell from $501.9B (2024) to $476.3B (2025); foodservice now 39% of in-store profit; organized retail crime cost $47.8B in 2025
  • Online Jewelry: Now captures estimated 25% of global jewelry purchases with mobile commerce at 60%+ of online transactions; fundamentally reshaping physical store value proposition
  • Personalized Nutrition: Growing 15% annually; subscription and custom formats thriving while commodity vitamin retail collapses on Amazon and Target private label pressure

Who rides it

Jewelry stores positioning as design studios and custom workshops (lab-grown diamonds, bespoke settings, styling consultation) survive the tariff and gold-price storm because they sell expertise and try-on, not commodity rings. Health retailers offering personalized nutrition consultation, subscription boxes, and curated recommendations (the 15% growth segment) win against Amazon. Convenience store chains betting on prepared foodservice, local differentiation, and experiential add-ons (EV charging that creates dwell time, regional specialties) win when fuel margins compress. They're all playing the same game: you can't click on it online, so come here for the experience, the advice, or the immediacy.

Who's squeezed

Mass-market jewelry retailers (Claire's, Lugano Diamonds) that tried to compete on inventory and price; specialty health chains that stocked commodity vitamins (The Vitamin Shoppe, GNC pre-bankruptcy restructuring) are getting hollowed out by Amazon and private-label alternatives. Convenience stores treating fuel as the profit center and foodservice as an afterthought; operators without local scale or brand identity. Tariff and margin pressure are accelerating the contraction—the 2025-2026 closures are the channel shift finally crashing the holdouts.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The experiential moat looks strong until inflation or recession dries up the customer base. Jewelry stores are already seeing a bifurcated market: affluent households remain confident spenders, but lower and middle-income households are squeezed by job market weakness and inflation, cutting off the middle of the market entirely. Convenience stores are being ravaged by organized retail crime ($47.8 billion in 2025, expected to hit $55 billion by 2028), which erodes the very margin gains that make experiential add-ons profitable. Health stores betting on premium personalized nutrition will win only if consumers have money to spend. The experiential survivor model requires a healthy middle class willing to pay for service and curation—if that customer disappears, the moat collapses.

In one sentence

The experiential survivor in a nutshell

Physical retail survives only when it does something e-commerce cannot—expertise, customization, immediacy, or presence—and every store that doesn't offer one of these is being methodically hollowed out by tariffs, online channels, and the channel shift itself.
