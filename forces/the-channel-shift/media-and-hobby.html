A · The categories that went online first

Media & Hobby Retail

The store stopped being where you buy — Amazon and marketplaces hollowed out these categories, and only omnichannel giants and experential stores survive.

The channel shift hits different in media and hobby retail. Three categories, three endings: bookstores staged a print-fueled comeback, art supply stores collapsed under omnichannel pressure and tariffs, and hardware stores clung to survival as a DIY-driven niche. Each tells a story about what made physical retail matter again—and what doesn't.

What changed

Bookstores discovered that the store itself became the product. For fifteen years, print books lost ground to e-readers and online ordering. Then something flipped. Print sales dipped only 0.9% in the first nine months of 2025, a reversal from the 10% cliff of 2020. Gen Z and millennial readers rejected digital convenience for the tactile, discovery-oriented experience of physical bookstores. BookTok—a TikTok community centered on book recommendations—drove approximately 59 million print book purchases in 2024 and contributed 800 million euros in sales across Europe in 2025. Barnes and Noble, under CEO James Daunt, proved omnichannel worked by giving local store managers autonomy over inventory and merchandising, mimicking indie bookstore curation. The result: 67 new Barnes and Noble locations opened in 2025 (the highest single year in 15 years), and 422 independent bookstores opened that same year, up 31% from 2024. Since 2021, the indie bookstore count rose 70%. This category made scarcity valuable—the rare, curated, in-person discovery experience—and made abundance (digital convenience) cheap and unwanted.

Art supply and craft retail experienced the opposite compression. The market sat at $1.2 billion in 2025-2026, down from $819 million in 2022, but that number masks a brutal winnowing. Joann Fabrics filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy in January 2025 and announced closure of all ~815 stores, marking the collapse of another chain competitor. Tariffs hit hard: raw materials (Chinese pigments, European linen, Canadian timber) saw costs jump 22% in 2025, squeezing margins industry-wide. Yet demand for creative supplies was real—Michaels reported 40% earnings growth in Q3 2025 and 12% comparable store sales gains by building an omnichannel fortress (online plus physical). Online retail now accounts for 56.8% of art supplies market share as of 2026, with e-commerce growing at 7.4% annually while physical stores shrink. The category made omnichannel scale valuable and made specialty retail scarcity unsustainable. Small independent art supply stores survived only if they built community and expertise that Amazon could not replicate.

Hardware stores persisted as a grudging niche. The market was $42.8 billion in 2026, growing at 1.3% that year, but the structure was broken: big-box chains controlled 56% while small independents closed at record rates. The industry was anchored in a sustained DIY movement—74% of homeowners do maintenance projects, and 24% either do work themselves or hire labor while buying materials directly. But tariffs on imports ran 10-12.5% as of mid-2026, pushing supply costs up 15-30% for multi-site operations and stretching lead times from 6-8 weeks to 16-24 weeks. True Value Company filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on October 14, 2024. Harpeth True Value Home Center, after 54 years, closed permanently on April 1, 2026. Carnation Ace Hardware, 56 years old, closed on October 25, 2025. Do it Best acquired the bankrupt True Value in late 2024, becoming the world's largest hardware cooperative, but even members reported closures due to inflation, unaffordable rents, and declining foot traffic. The category made deep expertise and local presence valuable—but made undifferentiated square footage cheap and expendable.

What made certain stores scarce and valuable was not inventory or convenience. It was curation, discovery, and expertise that e-commerce could not replicate at scale. Bookstores won by being experience platforms. Art and hardware stores lost by trying to compete on price and selection with omnichannel giants, and they collided with tariff shocks that erased thin margins. The survivors were those that pivoted to omnichannel leverage (Michaels, Barnes and Noble) or built credible expertise and community (independent bookstores, niche art suppliers, DIY specialists). The losers were chains caught in the middle: too expensive to be pure e-commerce, too generic to be experiential.

The evidence

  • Bookstores (2024-2025): BookTok drove 59 million print book purchases in 2024; indie bookstore openings surged 70% from 2021-2025, with 422 new indie stores in 2025 alone
  • Art Supply Retail (2025-2026): Michaels posted 40% earnings growth in Q3 2025 and 12% comparable store sales gains; online retail reached 56.8% market share growing at 7.4% annually
  • Art Supply Tariffs (2025): Raw material costs (pigments, linen, timber) jumped 22%; Joann Fabrics filed Chapter 11 in January 2025 and closed all ~815 stores
  • Hardware Retail (2024-2026): True Value filed for Chapter 11 on October 14, 2024; tariffs pushed supply costs up 15-30% with lead times stretching from 6-8 weeks to 16-24 weeks by mid-2026
  • Hardware Stores (2025): Harpeth True Value Home Center closed after 54 years on April 1, 2026; Carnation Ace Hardware (56 years) closed October 25, 2025 due to inflation and declining foot traffic

Who rides it

Barnes and Noble and Michaels—omnichannel consolidators with the scale to absorb tariffs and the sophistication to blend online and physical. Independent bookstores survived by becoming discovery platforms and community centers that TikTok and nostalgia fed. Specialty expertise retailers (niche art suppliers, local hardware shops with deep product knowledge) won by serving customers who valued advice over convenience. Home Depot and Lowe's gained ground as the default, because they had the scale to swallow tariff shocks that crushed smaller competitors.

Who's squeezed

Small independent hardware stores and specialty retailers caught between e-commerce undercutting and tariff inflation. Art supply chains like Joann Fabrics that tried to compete on breadth rather than expertise. Cooperative members who depended on stability but found True Value bankrupt and their own rents and labor costs unaffordable. Regional chains without omnichannel capability (Carnation Ace, Harpeth True Value) closed, betrayed by a model where the store was just an inventory warehouse, not a customer experience.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Tariffs are making the squeeze unsustainable even for winning retailers. Michaels and Barnes and Noble can absorb 15-30% supply cost increases; independent bookstores and art suppliers cannot. If tariffs stay at 10-12.5% for hardware and compound for craft materials, even omnichannel winners will pass costs to consumers, and price discipline will flip the DIY and bookstore booms into busts. The other risk: nostalgia is a volatile fuel. BookTok can reverse as suddenly as it appeared. If Gen Z stops treating physical bookstores as social experience and reverts to pure convenience, indie bookstores close en masse again.

In one sentence

Media & hobby retail in a nutshell

Bookstores found their way back by pivoting to curation and discovery; art supply stores and hardware stores got crushed between omnichannel consolidation and tariffs.
