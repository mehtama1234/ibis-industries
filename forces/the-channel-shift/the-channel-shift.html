The Channel Shift

The point of sale moved online; platforms and omnichannel scale win, standalone mid-market stores lose.

The store as the default place to shop is dead. Over the last five years, e-commerce has flipped from supplementary to primary, and the winners are platforms with massive logistics or brick-and-mortar chains that can do both at once. The losers are everyone else: independent stores, mid-market specialty chains, and any retailer still betting the physical location alone will pay rent.

What changed

What changed is simple and brutal: the economics of fulfillment. Amazon proved that if you own the warehouse, the truck, and the algorithm, you can deliver faster and cheaper than any store network ever could. That forced every competitor to either match Amazon's logistics footprint (impossible for most) or stop trying. The result has been a decade-long squeezing of the middle—the solo store, the small chain, the independent merchant. E-commerce in the US hit $1.38 trillion in 2026, up from $934.2 billion in 2022, a 48% jump in four years. But that growth is almost entirely going to Amazon, Walmart, and a handful of platforms like TikTok Shop (claimed $20 billion in 2026 sales). The margin compressed everywhere: e-commerce profit margins fell to 5.5-5.9% by 2025-2026, down from double digits a decade ago, because competition is ruthless and customer expectations for free shipping and instant returns are locked in. Small sellers on Amazon or Walmart Marketplace face take-rates of 30-50% after fees, logistics, and chargebacks, leaving almost no room to operate.

The bifurcation is real and it is deep. Women's clothing stores are the clearest case: online sales now run at nearly $61.8 billion (2025) versus brick-and-mortar at $66.4 billion (2026), a near-parity that would have been unthinkable in 2015. But the split is not even. Premium and specialty retailers—think Lululemon, Outdoor Voices, plus-size brands—are thriving both online and in selective physical locations because they have margins to invest in experience and fulfillment. Mass-market chains are collapsing. Store closures hit 7,323 locations in 2024 and roughly 6,000 more in the first half of 2025 alone. Forever 21 liquidated all 354 U.S. stores in March 2025. Liberated Brands (Volcom, Billabong, Quiksilver) filed Chapter 11 in February 2025 and liquidated 122 stores. Macy's announced the closure of 66 stores as part of a three-year 150-store exit. These are not anomalies. The women's clothing sector grew zero percent annually from 2021 to 2026, and that stagnation masks a violent reshuffling: thriving online segments and vanishing physical footprint. The tariff shock of August 2025—when the de-minimis exemption ended and all imports under $800 became dutiable—added $136 in estimated costs per family and forced price increases across imported apparel, which is nearly everything. Ultra-low-cost platforms like Shein and Temu, which had warehoused goods domestically to sidestep tariffs, now compete on pure volume and velocity.

Home improvement tells a similar but slightly different story. The sector is frozen by a housing crisis: low mortgage rates locked in a generation of homeowners who have no incentive to sell or move, so they are not buying new homes or renovating aggressively. Revenue in home improvement hit $294.6 billion in 2025, up only 2.3% from 2024, and growth is expected to stay flat to 2% in 2026. The category is splitting into professionals and pretenders. Lowe's reported a 15% revenue surge in professional services in 2024, outpacing DIY growth. Home Depot reported flat to negative foot traffic (negative 0.08% in Q1 2026 alone), suggesting consumers are not walking the aisles. Meanwhile, Amazon is quietly stealing share: its home improvement tool and equipment sales reached $6.1 billion in 2024, up 25% year-over-year. Tariffs are a rising cost burden—material costs up 4-6% on average, with kitchen cabinets and bathroom vanities facing 15-20% increases. Smaller competitors cannot absorb that. Wren Kitchens, a major kitchen specialty retailer, filed for bankruptcy and closed all 15 U.S. locations in 2025. Home Depot itself laid off 800 corporate employees and closed distribution facilities in 2025-2026. The omnichannel leaders—Home Depot and Lowe's—are surviving through scale and diversification, but the specialty player is extinct.

The channel shift did not destroy retail. It destroyed *store-based retail as a standalone business*. What survived, and what thrives, is omnichannel at scale: Walmart grew e-commerce 26% in Q2 2026 and store-fulfilled delivery surged 50%, with the company targeting 25% of revenue from digital by 2030. Online grocery grew 20% year-over-year for six straight quarters through Q1 2026; Kroger is expecting digital profitability in 2026. Grocery e-commerce exploded 32% in December 2025 alone when consumers discovered pickup and avoided fees. Mobile commerce now accounts for 60% of online sales. But this is also a story of new problems: return fraud has doubled in 18 months and now accounts for $76 billion in annual losses; 9% of all retail returns are now fraudulent. Small sellers and independent retailers face an impossible choice: invest in omnichannel infrastructure they cannot afford, compete on Amazon's terms (which means 30-50% take-rates), or exit. Most are exiting.

The evidence

  • E-commerce: US online retail jumped to $1.38T (2026) from $934.2B (2022) at 6.9-10.5% CAGR, but margins fell to 5.5-5.9% by 2025-2026, forcing small sellers into Amazon's 30-50% take-rate trap
  • Women's clothing: Online sales at $61.8B (2025) now rival brick-and-mortar's $66.4B (2026); mass-market stores collapsed with 13,323 closures in 2024-H1 2025, while premium segments thrived online
  • Home improvement: Market stalled at $294.6B (2025), up only 2.3% from 2024, with Amazon capturing $6.1B in tools alone (up 25% YoY) while specialty chains like Wren Kitchens filed bankruptcy
  • Tariffs: De-minimis exemption end in August 2025 added $136 per family to import costs and forced price increases across apparel, giving ultra-low-cost platforms (Shein, Temu) a localization advantage
  • Omnichannel winners: Walmart grew e-commerce 26% (Q2 2026) with store-fulfilled delivery up 50%; online grocery hit 20% YoY growth for six straight quarters into Q1 2026

Who rides it

Amazon, Walmart, and omnichannel-scaled chains. Walmart's store network became an asset instead of a liability—it can fulfill online orders from local inventory and beat Amazon on speed in many markets. Online-native and pure-platform players win too: TikTok Shop grabbed $20 billion in claimed 2026 sales by going after Gen Z directly, and Shein and Temu win through sheer velocity and tariff arbitrage (localized warehouses sidestep import duties). Premium and specialty retailers also survive if they own their own brand and margin: Lululemon, Outdoor Voices, niche athletic and plus-size labels can afford to build omnichannel and charge what customers will pay. The customer wins on speed, choice, and convenience—if they can afford it or tolerate the return fraud and data harvesting.

Who's squeezed

Independent stores and mid-market specialty chains. A solo bookstore, clothing boutique, or home-goods shop cannot compete on delivery speed, selection, or price. Small sellers on Amazon or Walmart Marketplace face take-rates of 30-50%, which erases profit. Mass-market chains like Forever 21, Macy's, and Liberated Brands could not differentiate fast enough and are liquidating. The small-city supplier and local distributor lose too: when consumers buy from Amazon or Shein directly, regional warehousing and local trucking disappear. Employment in brick-and-mortar retail is declining, with only professional services and specialty segments hiring. The tariff shock of August 2025 squeezed every import-dependent retailer, but small players had no scale to absorb the cost.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Return fraud and customer acquisition cost are spiraling and will eventually force price increases that undo e-commerce's savings promise. Retailers lose $76 billion annually to return fraud now (9% of all returns fraudulent), and the only defense is more friction (AI verification, restocking fees) which will drive customers back to … where? Also, Amazon's logistics dominance is not a competitive advantage—it is regulatory risk. Antitrust scrutiny is increasing, tariffs are being weaponized, and China's de-minimis workarounds (Shein's domestic warehousing) may trigger protectionist backlash. If tariffs spike further or Amazon faces forced divestitures, the entire omnichannel arbitrage collapses and prices spike across the board. The bigger risk: margins cannot compress any lower. When 5.5% is the ceiling and return fraud and labor costs keep rising, the economics of fulfillment break. That means either consolidation accelerates (fewer, larger players) or prices go up, and if prices go up, the whole value proposition of e-commerce—cheap, fast, frictionless—dies.

In one sentence

The channel shift in a nutshell

E-commerce won the sale, but omnichannel at scale and platforms with logistics won the customer; everyone else is closing stores and cutting jobs.
