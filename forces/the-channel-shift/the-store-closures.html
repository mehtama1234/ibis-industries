D · The tensions

The closure wave

Thousands of stores close each year; the real-estate and jobs fallout compounds.

Department stores and mid-tier retailers are closing at scale. Macy's alone closed 130 locations between 2024 and 2026. Saks Global—the merged Saks Fifth Avenue, Neiman Marcus, and Bergdorf—filed for bankruptcy in January 2026 with $3.4 billion in debt. Real estate that anchored shopping districts empties. Jobs vanish. The store itself becomes obsolete at the very moment it was supposed to survive.

What changed

The channel shift made physical stores risky for full-line retail. The math breaks. The US department store industry sits at $227.1 billion (2026) but grew only 0.3% from 2022 to 2026—economically flat. Margins compressed from 6.1% (2024) to 6.0% (2026), and that's before tariff shocks. When import duties exploded from 14.7% in January 2025 to 35.1% by December, retailers faced a choice: absorb the hit (margins drop to 3.6% if fully absorbed) or raise prices. Either path loses customers. Macy's, Nordstrom, JCPenney, and others got caught in the middle—expensive to operate compared to off-price, expensive to compete with e-commerce, too mainstream to be luxury, too legacy to pivot fast. JCPenney's revenue hit $6.6 billion by FY2024, revenue that had nowhere to go. Discount stores and off-price players pulled the same customers away. Ross Stores saw comparable store sales jump 17% in Q3 2025 while announcing 110 new openings for 2026. Nordstrom Rack and Saks Off Fifth took market share, the very off-price banners that cannibalized their parent's full-line business.\n\nReal estate became stranded. A closed Macy's or department store doesn't become a tech campus or a village green—it's a 100,000-square-foot anchor tenant hole in a mall. Mall traffic fell because there were fewer reasons to drive to a destination. By end-2026, Macy's had closed 150 stores (50 by end-2024, 66 by end-2025, 14 more in early 2026). Each closure meant empty real estate in secondary markets, devalued malls, and lost property tax revenue for municipalities. JCPenney's comeback campaign, 'Yes, JCPenney,' lifted traffic 3% in May 2025, but that one tactical win couldn't reverse the structural. Nordstrom's family-controlled founders bid $3.8 billion to take the company private in late 2025, a signal they saw the full-line business as terminal and the real future in off-price Rack expansion.\n\nEmployees bore the cost. The department store sector employed 666,500 workers (2025), declining 2.87% annually through 2028—meaning 19,000 jobs vanishing every year from payroll shrinkage alone, on top of store closures. These aren't high-wage jobs for the most part—they're hourly, retail, in suburban and secondary-market towns where alternatives are scarce. A closed store means real unemployment, not just a transition to a new mall location. Wage pressures from 21 state minimum wage hikes in 2025 and warehouse labor costs rising 7.4% year-over-year only squeezed margins further, accelerating the incentive to close rather than modernize.\n\nThe bifurcation created a hollowed middle. Affluent households kept shopping at full-price retail or luxury; lower and middle-income households traded down to discount stores at five times faster rates in 2026. That left mid-tier department stores in the worst spot: not trendy enough to be destination, not cheap enough to be treasure-hunt, not online enough to be convenient. Saks Global's bankruptcy in January 2026 was the canary. When the luxury merger of Saks Fifth Avenue, Neiman Marcus, and Bergdorf Goodman—three storied names—couldn't make the math work despite combining scale, it meant no department store was safe. Store productivity collapsed. Vendor payment delays cascaded. The bankruptcy filing sent ripples through the entire supply chain. Smaller bankruptcies followed: Rite Aid in May 2025 (second filing), At Home in June 2025 (100 store closures in home goods). The industry didn't face one shock—it faced four at once: tariffs, margin collapse, channel migration, and consumer bifurcation.

The evidence

  • Department Stores: Macy's 150-store closure plan (2024–2026) and Saks Global's $3.4B bankruptcy (January 2026) signal structural decline in a $227.1B industry growing only 0.3% CAGR.
  • Discount Retail: Ross Stores reported 17% comparable sales growth and 110 new openings in Q3 2025, winning share as consumers trade down five times faster in 2026.
  • Tariffs: Apparel import duties spiked from 14.7% (Jan 2025) to 35.1% (Dec 2025), compressing retailer margins to estimated 3.6% if fully absorbed, forcing price hikes or closures.
  • Employment: Department store payroll declining 2.87% annually through 2028 (19,000 jobs per year) as wage pressures from 21 state minimum hikes and 7.4% warehouse labor cost inflation squeeze profitability.
  • Off-Price Momentum: Nordstrom family bid $3.8B to take the company private (2025–2026) to focus on Rack expansion, while Saks Off Fifth and Nordstrom Rack cannibalize full-line traffic.

Who rides it

Off-price and discount retailers are the survivors. Ross Stores, Nordstrom Rack, TJX Companies (T.J. Maxx, Marshalls), and outlet malls are winning customers from traditional department stores. The "treasure hunt" experience—weekly-changing curated inventory at deep discounts—remains defensible against e-commerce in ways full-price mall retail is not. These players expanded aggressively: Ross added 110 stores in 2026, Nordstrom's founders invested $3.8B to take the company private and expand Rack, and discount store margins held at 6.9% (2026) even as traditional department stores compressed to 6.0%. Affluent consumers also win in one sense—luxury remains intact because it was never really competing in the mall anyway. But the real winner is e-commerce and direct-to-consumer, which didn't need to close stores because they never had the anchor tenant problem to begin with.

Who's squeezed

Department store chains and their landlords lose. Macy's, JCPenney, Nordstrom's full-line division, regional mid-tier department stores—these faces shrink or disappear. More than 666,500 retail workers employed in department stores face layoffs, wage stagnation, or forced relocation to discount formats at lower pay. Shopping mall landlords lose most of all. A closed 100,000-square-foot Macy's anchor tenant leaves a void that no lifestyle retailer wants to fill. Property values fall, operating costs rise, and the economic model of the destination mall fractures. Secondary and suburban markets lose downtown retail activity, tax base, and foot traffic.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The secondary and tertiary market real estate collapse is irreversible in a five-year window. When a Macy's closes in Des Moines, Springfield, or Allentown, there is no natural successor tenant. That real estate becomes functionally dead. Shopping centers go dark. Property tax revenue dries up for school districts and municipalities already squeezed. The social fabric of suburban retail—the meetup spot, the job entry point—erodes. At scale across hundreds of closed stores, this becomes a regional economic problem, not just a retail problem. The tariff environment may ease, margins may stabilize, but those closed stores won't reopen. The real estate damage is one-way.

In one sentence

The closure wave in a nutshell

Thousands of department and mid-tier retail stores close each year as tariffs, margin compression, and channel shift to e-commerce and off-price retail hollow out the full-line department store, leaving stranded real estate and jobs in secondary markets.
