D · The tensions

Tariffs & the de-minimis end

When the tariff loophole closed in August 2025, the price war moved offline—mass-market stores couldn't compete with Shein and Temu on cost anymore.

For years, Shein and Temu exploited a quirk in US import law: packages under $800 entered duty-free, making their unit economics unbeatable. On August 29, 2025, that ended. The de-minimis exemption closed globally, and suddenly every sub-$800 shipment faced tariffs. That same year, furniture tariffs hit 25% on upholstered goods. For mass-market retail already running on 2-3% margins, there was nowhere left to hide.

What changed

For two decades, the de-minimis loophole was the oxygen mask for ultrafast fashion and bargain electronics. Shein could ship a $3 tank top from China for $1.50 landed cost. No tariffs. No middleman. Customers in Nebraska got it in two weeks for $4.99. A store buyer paying $2.50 wholesale from a domestic vendor and selling for $12.99 couldn't touch that price. When the exemption ended in August 2025, the arithmetic inverted. A $3 Shein top now faced duty. Suddenly it cost Shein $3.50, or they had to raise the customer price to $6.99. That margin squeeze rippled backward through the entire supply chain—but here's the thing: it didn't hurt Shein the way it hurt department stores. Shein absorbed the hit, cut deeper, and passed a smaller damage to customers. A traditional mass-market retailer like Forever 21 or Value City Furniture was already operating so thin (2-3% net margin in women's clothing, 3% in furniture) that they had no buffer. The tariff didn't make them less competitive; it made them insolvent.

Forever 21 filed Chapter 11 in March 2025 and liquidated all 354 US stores. Liberated Brands (Volcom, Billabong, Roxy, RVCA, Spyder, Quiksilver) filed the same month and liquidated 122 stores. Macy's announced 66 closures in January 2025 as part of a planned 150-store exit. American Signature Inc. filed Chapter 11 in November 2025, closing Value City Furniture locations. Walker Edison Furniture and affiliates filed in August 2025 and sold for just $20 million to Twin-Star International. These weren't isolated stumbles. They were structural collapses driven by tariff pressure hitting retailers with no margin to absorb it.

The tariff also triggered a bifurcation that's still accelerating. The US women's clothing market was already split: $61.8 billion in online sales in 2025 versus brick-and-mortar staying flat at $66.4 billion in 2026 (a -0.3% annual decline since 2021). But after the de-minimis closure, traditional stores couldn't compete on price, so the online shift accelerated. Shein and Temu gobbled the price-sensitive shoppers, leaving traditional department stores and mass-market chains fighting for air. Meanwhile, premium and specialty retailers—luxury brands, niche segments like plus-size and athletic wear—kept growing. They weren't competing on $4.99 prices; they were competing on brand and fit. Employment in online women's clothing grew 5.5% annually from 2019 to 2024, while brick-and-mortar employment continued its decline. The tariff didn't create this split—but it accelerated it from a slow divergence into a cliff.

In furniture, the damage was compounded by housing weakness and tariff specificity. New home sales dropped 17.6% month-over-month in January 2026 and 11.3% year-on-year, hitting the core customer base. But the tariff was the knife: 25% duties on upholstered furniture and cabinets (effective October 2024) squeezed retailers between raising prices to consumers who were already retrencing, or eating the cost and eroding the already-thin 3% margin to zero. Online furniture captured 28% of market share by 2025 and grew 5-10% annually, with Wayfair alone holding roughly 14% of the US market. Wayfair could absorb tariff hits because scale and direct-to-consumer logistics gave it breathing room. Independent and mid-market retailers got crushed. At the same time, a premium niche emerged: 76% of consumers said they'd pay 9.7% more for eco-friendly furniture, and ergonomic gaming seating spiked as remote work held. The tariff made survival a choice between scale, brand, or niche—no middle ground.

The evidence

  • Women's clothing: Forever 21 liquidated all 354 US stores (March 2025), Liberated Brands liquidated 122 stores (February 2025), Macy's cut 66 stores (January 2025) as de-minimis closure and tariffs squeezed 2-3% margins past survival threshold.
  • Women's clothing: Online sales reached $61.8B in 2025 vs brick-and-mortar $66.4B in 2026 (-0.3% annual decline since 2021); employment in online grew 5.5% annually 2019-2024 while stores contracted.
  • Furniture: American Signature (Value City) filed Chapter 11 (November 2025); Walker Edison filed (August 2025) and sold for $20M; 25% tariffs on upholstered goods (October 2024) hit 3% margin retailers with no absorptive capacity.
  • Furniture: Housing demand collapsed 17.6% month-over-month January 2026 and 11.3% year-on-year; online furniture channels reached 28% of market share (2025) growing 5-10% annually, with Wayfair holding ~14% of US market.
  • De-minimis: August 29, 2025 closure ended duty-free entry for packages under $800 globally, forcing Shein and Temu to either raise prices (6% customer price increase) or accept margin compression; mass-market retailers with existing 2-3% margins had no escape valve.

Who rides it

Premium and specialty retailers, online platforms like Wayfair and direct-to-consumer brands, and niche segments like sustainable furniture (76% of consumers willing to pay 9.7% more for eco-friendly goods) and gaming ergonomic seating ride the channel shift. These businesses either have margin (premium brands), scale (online marketplaces), or differentiation (niche segments) to absorb tariffs and leverage the bifurcation. Shein and Temu themselves survive because they operate at massive volume and can subsume tariff costs into lower absolute prices; they out-scale traditional retailers. Remote work and consumer retrenchment favor DTC logistics and omnichannel models that cut inventory and physical footprint.

Who's squeezed

Mass-market and discount brick-and-mortar chains—Forever 21, Macy's, Value City Furniture, independent mid-market retailers—get squeezed into insolvency. They operated on 2-3% margins with the de-minimis loophole and tariffs as their only headwinds; when tariffs hit and the loophole closed, there was no margin to absorb the cost increase. Store closures accelerated: 7,323 in 2024 alone, roughly 6,000 more in the first half of 2025. Employment in brick-and-mortar women's clothing continued declining while online grew 5.5% annually. Consumers trading down to ultra-low-cost online hurt traditional mass-market the most; affluent consumers gravitate to premium brands, while price-sensitive shoppers found a cheaper path on Shein and Temu. The middle retail market—the $12-$30 apparel, the mid-range furniture—vanished.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is a second-order supply-chain contraction. If tariffs remain (or rise further) and domestic production doesn't scale fast enough, prices will ratchet higher across the board—not just for budget retailers but for omnichannel players and DTC brands too. A $70 sofa becomes $85. A Wayfair customer might switch to thrift or overseas sites using VPNs and forwarding services. Labor shortages in domestic furniture and apparel manufacturing mean replacing offshore production is slow and costly. The bifurcation could compress into a hourglass: ultra-premium at top, ultra-cheap at bottom, and almost nothing in the middle where most retail jobs exist. That's not a market; it's a hollowing.

In one sentence

Tariffs & the de-minimis end in a nutshell

The de-minimis tariff exemption closure in August 2025 and furniture tariffs gutted mass-market retail margins (2-3%), forcing store closures and accelerating the channel shift to online-only and niche brands while Shein, Temu, and DTC platforms captured price-sensitive and premium shoppers in the wreckage.
