B · The marketplace machine

The Marketplace Eats the Middle

The platform takes a cut of everything and sets the terms of survival for sellers.

When you buy online, you're increasingly buying from a marketplace platform, not a retailer. Amazon, Walmart, eBay, TikTok Shop — they all profit from the sale and from the seller's access to customers. The smaller the seller, the more punishing these economics become. Only the sellers who can afford the platform's take-rates, logistics standards, and demand for automation survive the squeeze.

What changed

E-commerce in the US hit $1,380 billion in 2026 and is growing 6.9% to 10.5% annually through the early 2030s, but that growth is not spreading evenly. Amazon's logistics supremacy forces all competitors to match delivery standards that only Amazon can afford — same-day, free, instant refunds. Walmart is fighting back with its omnichannel playbook: store-fulfilled delivery surged 50% in Q2 2026, and the company is targeting digital to be 25% of revenue by fiscal 2030. But Walmart's dominance is still regional; Amazon's remains national and unmatched. Mobile commerce now represents 60% of all online sales, and social commerce is growing 30% annually, with TikTok Shop claiming $20 billion in 2026 sales alone. This fragmentation actually favors the platforms more — sellers now have to show up on Amazon, Walmart, eBay, TikTok, and their own direct-to-consumer site, all simultaneously, all at different cost structures. The cost of being everywhere has never been higher.\n\nTariffs made the squeeze worse. The mid-2025 tariff shock — a 120% duty on Chinese goods and suspension of de minimis thresholds — forced import-dependent sellers to choose between raising prices (losing price-sensitive customers) and shrinking margins (losing money). E-commerce margins sit at 5.5% to 5.9%, already compressed by fulfillment costs, AI requirements, and platform take-rates that run 15-45% depending on the site. A small seller importing from China in May 2025 faced an instant 120% cost increase with no ability to pass it through to customers without losing them to a competitor three clicks away.\n\nReturn fraud has become its own crisis. Retailers lost $76 billion annually to fraudulent returns; 9% of all retail returns are now fake. Customers exploit 94.5% repeat-purchase standards by ordering multiples, returning the expensive ones, and keeping the cheap ones — or shipping back empty boxes. Retailers now deploy AI to detect patterns, but they face an impossible trade-off: stop the fraud and lose the customers who expect frictionless returns, or let it run and bleed margins. The platforms don't solve this; they profit from transaction volume regardless of whether goods go out and come back.\n\nIn grocery, the speeds are getting faster. Online grocery sales grew 20% year-over-year for six straight quarters through Q1 2026, and December 2025 saw a 32% surge as customers embraced pickup to avoid delivery fees. Walmart and Kroger are racing to profitability — Kroger expects digital profitability in 2026 — but profitability here means zero margin; it means they're breaking even on a volume play. For smaller grocery sellers, online channels don't exist. Only Walmart and Kroger have the store footprint and supply-chain density to make pickup pencil out. Everyone else is locked out or locked into the marketplace at whatever terms the platform sets.

The evidence

  • E-commerce: $1,380B market in 2026 with 5.5–5.9% margins, growing 6.9–10.5% annually but consolidating around platforms (Amazon, Walmart, eBay) and omnichannel incumbents
  • Tariffs: Mid-2025 duty shock (120% on China goods, de minimis suspension) forced import-dependent sellers to absorb costs or exit; e-commerce relies on imports
  • Grocery: Online grocery 20% YoY growth for six straight quarters; Walmart/Kroger targeting profitability in 2026 via store-pickup; small sellers locked out
  • Social commerce: TikTok Shop $20B+ annual sales in 2026, growing 30% annually, fragmenting seller channels and forcing multi-platform presence
  • Return fraud: $76B lost annually; 9% of returns fraudulent; retailers deploy AI while balancing fraud-prevention against repeat-purchase loyalty (94.5% of customers expect frictionless returns)

Who rides it

Walmart is the winner. Its omnichannel strategy — store-fulfilled delivery up 50% in Q2 2026, targeting 25% digital revenue by 2030 — is the only playbook that works at scale for a non-Amazon retailer. Walmart has thousands of stores; Walmart has supply chains that predate e-commerce; Walmart can absorb the fixed costs of dual fulfillment. Amazon remains the default, but Walmart is the only competitor making inroads. Amazon itself wins because competitors must match Amazon's standards to survive, and most never will. TikTok Shop wins in social commerce — $20B in 2026 sales with 30% annual growth — because it owns the attention and the checkout all at once. Small sellers lose. Big platforms win.

Who's squeezed

Smaller independent sellers are squeezed between Amazon's take-rates (15-45% depending on category), Walmart's controls, TikTok's uncertainty, and their own need to be visible on multiple channels simultaneously. Tariffs hit hardest — the mid-2025 duty shock on Chinese goods was an instant margin collapse for import-dependent sellers. They can't raise prices without losing customers to Amazon; they can't cut margins without going insolvent. Mid-market retailers without omnichannel infrastructure (stores + logistics + mobile app + returns automation) are locked in decline. Grocery is particularly brutal: only Walmart and Kroger can afford the store-pickup economics that consumers now demand. Regional and independent grocers have no online channel and no way to build one at competitive cost. The $76B in annual return fraud represents lost margin that gets passed to honest sellers who absorb higher fraud-detection costs.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that return fraud becomes systemic and unsolvable. At $76B lost annually and 9% of returns now fraudulent, retailers are deploying AI to detect patterns — but every fraud-prevention control that asks for ID, restricts refunds, or flags suspicious orders risks losing the 94.5% of customers who reward repeat purchase. If fraud accelerates (5x since 2024), retailers will either have to raise prices (killing volume) or tighten returns (breaking the loyalty loop). Smaller sellers have no AI, no fraud team, no data. They'll exit first. The platform dynamics make this worse: marketplaces profit from transaction volume regardless of fraud, so they have no incentive to solve it. The result is a system where honest sellers subsidize fraud detection, dishonest customers subsidize everyone else, and the platforms keep growing richer.

In one sentence

The marketplace eats the middle in a nutshell

Marketplace platforms profit from every sale and set the terms of seller survival — margins compress, tariffs hurt, fraud spirals, and only omnichannel incumbents or giant platforms survive the squeeze.
