A · The categories that went online first

Apparel & the try-before-you-buy problem

Clothing shifted online despite fit friction; stores became showrooms, then closed.

For decades, apparel was the one category you had to try on. You went to a store, found a changing room, and made sure the fit was right before paying. That friction kept retail alive. But somewhere between 2021 and 2026, fit stopped mattering more than price and convenience. E-commerce captured half of women's clothing sales and a quarter of family apparel. Physical stores didn't disappear—they emptied out, closed down, and transformed into showrooms for online sales nobody would make in person anymore.

What changed

The apparel market was already shifting online before 2025, but three things hit simultaneously and shattered what remained of physical retail. First: tariffs made everything cost more at the exact moment when people stopped spending. When the de-minimis tariff exemption ended on August 29, 2025, all imports under $800 suddenly had duties applied. By December 2025, apparel tariff rates hit 35.1%, nearly 2.4 times higher than the January 2025 rate of 14.7%. That forced cost increase hit consumers immediately—apparel CPI jumped 5.1% year-over-year by March 2026, and monthly spending on apparel collapsed 21.7% from late 2024 to early 2025. Consumers, especially lower-income households, simply stopped buying clothes. Retailers had to choose between absorbing the tariff hit or passing it to a customer base that was already retreating.

Second: e-commerce dominance became complete and unstoppable. Women's clothing online reached $61.8 billion in 2025 while brick-and-mortar was still $66.4 billion in 2026—nearly parity. Family clothing saw e-commerce capture 24% of fashion retail in 2026, up from roughly 21% in 2024, growing 4.9% annually while traditional brick-and-mortar sales lagged by 62%. In footwear, the shift was sharper: e-commerce penetration hit 30-35% of total sales by mid-2025, growing at 7-9% annually. The stores that survived did so by becoming distribution centers for online orders, not by selling to people walking in the door. Online women's clothing employment grew 5.5% annually from 2019 to 2024, while brick-and-mortar employment in the same sector kept declining. The channel had flipped; the question was no longer whether online would win, but how fast physical retail would shrink.

Third: the industry consolidating and collapsing told the story nobody wanted to hear. Macy's announced closure of 66 stores in January 2025, part of a 150-store exit plan. Forever 21, once a ubiquitous fast-fashion fixture, filed Chapter 11 on March 16, 2025, and liquidated all 364 stores by May. Liberated Brands (which owned Volcom, Billabong, Quiksilver, and Roxy) filed Chapter 11 in February 2025 and liquidated 122 stores. Francesca's announced closure of 400 locations. Torrid planned 180 closures. Even luxury players weren't safe: Allbirds, which had a $4 billion IPO valuation in 2021, closed all remaining full-price US stores in February 2026 after being sold for $39 million. In footwear, the endgame became visible when Dick's Sporting Goods completed its $2.5 billion acquisition of Foot Locker in September 2025—the largest pure-play shoe retailer, no longer viable as a standalone business. Mall foot traffic declined sharply for the first time since the pandemic in late 2025, with super-regional malls down 1.2% overall during the holiday season. The stores had stopped being destinations. They were inventory graveyards waiting to be liquidated.

The evidence

  • Women's clothing: e-commerce at $61.8B (2025) vs brick-and-mortar $66.4B (2026) show near channel parity and structural shift
  • Family clothing: e-commerce grew 4.9% annually through 2026, capturing 24% share while brick-and-mortar lagged by 62% growth differential
  • Footwear: e-commerce penetration hit 30-35% by mid-2025, growing 7-9% annually, while Foot Locker—the largest pure-play retailer—acquired by Dick's in September 2025
  • Tariffs: apparel rates surged to 35.1% in December 2025 from 14.7% in January, triggering 5.1% CPI inflation and 21.7% spending collapse
  • Store closures: Forever 21 (364 stores, May 2025), Liberated Brands (122 stores, Feb 2025), Francesca's (400 announced), Allbirds (all full-price US stores, Feb 2026)

Who rides it

The winners are ultra-low-cost online platforms and value retailers positioned to absorb tariff shocks. Shein and Temu, despite controversies, continued gaining share as consumers cut spending and hunted for the cheapest option. TJX Companies (which owns T.J.Maxx and Marshalls) reported 5% comp sales growth in Q3 FY2026 and raised guidance, benefiting from price-conscious consumers rotating to off-price retail. Ross Stores projected 6-7% comp growth for FY2026 for the same reason. Retailers who went omnichannel and built localized warehousing—matching online order fulfillment to neighborhood hubs—survived by becoming infrastructure, not destinations. Nike and Adidas, by consolidating wholesale and pushing direct-to-consumer, bypassed the channel problem entirely (though Nike reversed some wholesale cuts after revenue impact). Employment growth in online clothing jobs showed clear winners: 5.5% annual growth 2019-2024 in women's clothing e-commerce versus ongoing declines in brick-and-mortar.

Who's squeezed

Mass-market and discount brick-and-mortar retailers got crushed. The store closures tell the story: 7,323 in 2024 and roughly 6,000 in the first half of 2025 alone. Forever 21, Francesca's, Liberated Brands, Torrid, and dozens of smaller chains liquidated or announced major exit plans. Women's clothing had 88,112 businesses in 2026 but was shedding locations constantly; brick-and-mortar employment in the sector continued declining. In footwear, the independent shoe store became obsolete—foot traffic at malls declined for the first time since the pandemic in late 2025, particularly hurting super-regional malls where larger shoe retailers clustered. Foot Locker's forced acquisition and closure of overlap stores proved the category couldn't sustain a pure-play model anymore. Family clothing retailers faced the worst squeeze: tariffs hit 35.1%, consumer spending collapsed 21.7%, and e-commerce took an additional 3 percentage points of share in just two years. Smaller, undercapitalized chains with high debt and inflexible leases simply couldn't survive long enough to rebuild online operations.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that tariff uncertainty and escalation cause a cascading insolvency wave through 2026 and beyond. The Trump administration imposed sector-specific tariffs up to 50% on some goods in 2025-2026, and footwear remained uncertain whether it would carry additional levies beyond the base 10% universal tariff. Retailers locked into Q1-Q2 2026 inventory sourced before tariff clarity will absorb the full cost hit, potentially pushing another wave of bankruptcies. Second: if e-commerce concentration continues unchecked and mall foot traffic keeps declining, landlords of regional malls will face a death spiral of empty stores, lower rents, and tenant defaults. Apparel CPI jumped 5.1% year-over-year by March 2026; if tariffs stay elevated, price resistance could cause permanent demand destruction—consumers may simply buy less clothing, not just shift channels. The category that defined retail is no longer retail.
