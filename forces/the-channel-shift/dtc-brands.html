B · The marketplace machine

Direct-to-Consumer Brands

Small brands selling direct online are crushed by platforms and tariffs; only scaled omnichannel survives.

Direct-to-consumer was supposed to liberate brands from the middleman. Instead, the middleman became TikTok, Amazon, and Walmart. DTC upstarts that bypassed physical retail found themselves trapped in an even tighter squeeze: social commerce cannibalized discovery, logistics costs ate profits, and tariffs turned 2025 into a bloodbath. The brands that killed the store didn't survive the shift they started.

What changed

When brands went DTC in the 2010s, they promised direct relationships with customers and fat margins. That worked briefly for beauty and apparel. But by 2025, the structure inverted. TikTok Shop alone did $2.49 billion in US beauty sales in 2025, up 123 percent year-over-year, and that growth came directly from the wallets of brands still building email lists. Discovery moved to platforms; logistics became a commodity; tariffs arrived. In beauty specifically, the $69.3 billion US market in 2026 looks stable on the surface—up from $29.3 billion in 2022 under the old definition—but that's entirely Ulta and Sephora's doing. Ulta grew net sales to $12.4 billion in fiscal 2025 (ended February 2026), up 9.7 percent, because it aggregates and curates thousands of brands at scale. Smaller pure-play DTC brands didn't benefit. Glossier closed 75 percent of its stores. Pat McGrath Labs filed Chapter 11 in early 2026 after years of burning through DTC venture capital. Cover FX and Mally Beauty shut down in January 2026. Flower Beauty (Drew Barrymore's brand) closed in September 2025. All of them had followed the playbook: raise venture money, burn cash on customer acquisition, build a DTC site, hire Instagram influencers. None of it worked at unit economics that penciled.\n\nThe outdoor gear industry shows the same pattern differently. The US hiking and outdoor equipment market is now $6.3 billion in 2026, down from $7.1 billion in 2022—a real contraction after pandemic peaks. Here, Amazon and Walmart didn't just pressure margins; they moved upmarket with private-label tents and sleeping bags, and downmarket respectively, compressing the middle where independent shops and small brands live. REI, the largest outdoor co-op retailer, laid off 180 full-time and 248 part-time employees in January 2025 and shut down its Experiences division (tours and classes). In October 2024, it had cut another 257 retail jobs. Even with 23 million co-op members, REI cannot sustain the unit economics of the old model. Dick's Sporting Goods is launching elevated outdoor concepts to compete directly with REI, further fracturing shelf space. Smaller independent outdoor shops—the kind that used to cluster in mountain towns and college towns—are closing faster than they can pivot.\n\nWhat made DTC brands vulnerable was the same thing that looked like their strength: they operated in a world where the customer seemed close and loyalty felt real. But loyalty, it turned out, was just subscription frequency and Instagram algorithm reach. As soon as social commerce platforms figured out how to sell beauty and gear directly, the brands' entire moat evaporated. Tariffs in 2025 compressed margins further—health and beauty inflation cooled to 2.1 percent by mid-2025, a signal that consumers were trading down or buying less—and the venture-funded cash burn model that had sustained DTC growth hit a wall. Ulta-Target partnership ending in August 2026 eliminated a key distribution channel that was helping smaller brands get shelf space at scale. Amazon's forecast share of beauty is projected to climb from 10 percent in 2024 to 15 percent by 2030, while Sephora and Ulta's combined share dips from 20 percent to 19 percent. That's not a massive shift in the numbers, but it reflects the real fragmentation happening in customer discovery: the brands themselves lost control of the narrative the moment TikTok started selling it.\n\nThe playbook that was supposed to disintermediate retail—cut out Macy's, cut out department stores, sell your own site—actually transferred control to platforms with deeper pockets, better logistics, and network effects. Physical retail was the old tyranny. DTC was supposed to be freedom. What arrived instead was a new tyranny: algorithm dependency, customer acquisition cost inflation, and the brutal fact that margins below 40 percent cannot survive in a world where your payment processor and your logistics provider both take 20-30 percent. The brands that tried to go DTC without the scale to compete on fulfillment speed and price ended up playing a losing game against platforms that had no brand to build, only transactions to capture."

The evidence

  • Beauty & cosmetics: Pat McGrath Labs filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy in early 2026 after years of DTC-only burn; Flower Beauty (Drew Barrymore brand) closed September 2025 due to tariffs and weak DTC sales; Cover FX and Mally Beauty shut January 2026.
  • Outdoor retail: REI (largest co-op chain) laid off 180 FT and 248 PT employees January 2025 and cut Experiences division serving only 40,000 of 23 million members; Dick's Sporting Goods Public Lands collapsed from 8 to 3 stores.
  • E-commerce capture: TikTok Shop drove $2.49 billion US beauty GMV in 2025 with 123% YoY growth; Gen Z/millennials now discover 70% of beauty digitally (TikTok, Instagram, social commerce).
  • Market contraction: US hiking/outdoor equipment declined -2.6% in 2025, market is $6.3B (2026) vs. $7.1B (2022); margin compression to 3.5-4.3% (2025-2026).
  • Beauty market bifurcation: $69.3 billion US beauty retail (2026), stable but structurally hollowed; Ulta's $12.4B (fiscal 2025, +9.7% YoY) masks failure of hundreds of smaller DTC brands; Amazon beauty share forecast 10% (2024) → 15% (2030).

Who rides it

Platforms (Amazon, TikTok Shop, Instagram) and scaled omnichannel aggregators (Ulta, Sephora, Dick's Sporting Goods with elevated concepts) are the winners. They control discovery, logistics, and customer trust. Ulta in particular is leveraging its margin and scale to absorb smaller brands into its ecosystem, while platforms handle the social commerce layer directly. Amazon is moving upmarket into premium outdoor gear with private labels. These players have the infrastructure to survive tariffs and absorb customer acquisition cost inflation.

Who's squeezed

Pure-play DTC beauty brands, outdoor startups, and small independent retailers. Glossier, Pat McGrath, Flower Beauty, Cover FX, Mally Beauty all folded. REI, despite its massive co-op base, had to cut 400+ employees and shut down its experiential division. Smaller outdoor shops continue to close as Amazon and Walmart upmarket/downmarket pressure compresses the middle. Any brand without scale to compete on fulfillment speed, tariff absorption, and logistics cost gets squeezed between platform commission, rising CAC, and margin compression.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that venture-backed DTC, which promised a new model of retail, simply transferred control to platforms with worse incentive alignment—algorithm-driven discovery that changes overnight, commission rates that rise with success, and customer data that the platform owns. A beauty brand that spent five years building a TikTok audience can wake up to algorithm changes or platform policy shifts that crater its business. Meanwhile, tariffs are a live risk: they squeezed margins throughout 2025 and consumer confidence in discretionary spending (beauty, outdoor gear) is fragile. If consumer spending retracts further, the omnichannel players (Ulta, Amazon) can absorb it by cutting smaller brands loose. The infrastructure advantage of the platforms means they can outbid DTC brands on fulfillment and pricing indefinitely.

In one sentence

Direct-to-consumer brands in a nutshell

DTC brands killed physical retail but lost to platforms that could sell faster, cheaper, and with network effects they could never match.
