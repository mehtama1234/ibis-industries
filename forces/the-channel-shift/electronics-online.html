A · The categories that went online first

Electronics go online

Commodity electronics led the retail exodus — tariffs, pricing power, and the smartphone gutted specialty stores, leaving only online retailers and the smallest experiential niches.

Electronics disappeared from Main Street first. Consumer electronics stores in the US still ring up $261.62 billion in 2026, but it's not because anyone is going to a shop anymore. Amazon owns 60.5% of all e-commerce electronics and appliances. The physical stores that built the category — Best Buy, independent computer shops, camera retailers — are all in structural decline, closing hundreds of locations a year. What happened is simple: phones made cameras obsolete, e-commerce made prices transparent, and tariffs made shelves too expensive to stock.

What changed

Consumer electronics went from a destination category to a default online purchase faster than any other retail segment. In 2022, there were 42,930 consumer electronics retail establishments in the US. By 2025-2026, that number had collapsed by 4.5% annually, a death march that accelerated when tariff rates on Chinese electronics hit 35% effective (25% Section 301 tariffs plus a 10% Section 122 surcharge) in early 2026, with some components facing rates as high as 145%. The tariff shock of April 2025 hit like a price shock: phone demand fell 37%, gaming consoles dropped 58%, laptops fell 68%. Retailers couldn't absorb that cost. Consumers at lower and middle incomes simply stopped buying. Even as tariffs moderated to 13.7% by February 2026, the damage held. Best Buy still commands $41.7 billion in annual revenue and has just announced its first store expansion in a decade — but only as small-format, high-touch shops in tech-forward metros, betting survival is boutique, not breadth. Appliances have outperformed because they're still a consultation buy, still installed at home, still a ticket item. Everything else — computers, cameras, gaming gear — is purchased online or not at all.

Computer retail fractured even faster. The segment was already thin at $39.6 billion in 2026, growing just 0.4% a year over five years. But tariffs devastated the supply side: US computer imports from China collapsed 70% to $10.8 billion in 2025. Retailers were forced to source from Mexico and Taiwan, a friction that added weeks and uncertainty. IDC forecast that PC shipments would fall 11.3% in 2026, with Q4 potentially down 20% year-over-year, and memory shortages will linger until the end of 2027. The average price of a PC rose 18.3% in 2026. Budget systems — the volume segment that feeds retail foot traffic — were projected to drop 28%. These weren't aspirational buys anymore; they were postponed buys. Apple, which built the premium computer retail segment, closed three US stores in mid-2026 while opening in only the highest-growth metros (Detroit, Miami), explicitly abandoning the mall. Best Buy shrank its computer footprint by 24-40 stores per year. The computer retail category is now a slowly-dying middle: too small to finance locations, too exposed to tariff shock, too transparent online. Independent computer retailers shrank from 5,258 locations in 2025 to a category that loses 6.6% of its store base per year.

Camera retail was first to near-total extinction. Fewer than 1,000 independent camera retailers remain — down from 1,180 in 2021 and 5,676 employees by 2021. The market is worth $5.4 billion in 2026, and it's nearly all online or rented. E-commerce owns 55% of camera sales. Smartphones handle 94% of all photos. Mirrorless cameras are 90% of interchangeable-lens sales, DSLRs are effectively dead, and the only growth segment is used gear — which online marketplaces (eBay, MPB, B&H Used) have monopolized. Tariffs on camera imports started at 10% baseline in April 2025, country-specific (Japan 15%, Thailand 19-46%, Germany variable), and by February 2026 climbed to 15% flat, raising retail prices 10-15%. For a category already running 1-2% margins (historically 1.9% in 2021), that was extinction velocity. Specialty camera shops survive only in high-population artistic hubs — NYC, LA, Portland — and only by serving professionals and enthusiasts who will pay for hand-holding and rental services. The surprise growth engine was rental: LensRentals and Adorama Rentals became essential revenue streams for both online retailers and the last specialty shops, because photographers who used to buy gear now rent it, use it once, and give it back. Margin-free retail became the business model for everyone else.

The evidence

  • Consumer electronics: Amazon owns 60.5% of US e-commerce electronics/appliances (2025); Best Buy reported $41.7B in 2026 full-year revenue with first positive comp sales (+0.5%) since fiscal 2022
  • Computer retail: US tariff rates on Chinese electronics reached 35% effective by early 2026 (Section 301 + Section 122); PC shipments forecast down 11.3% in 2026 with Q4 potentially 20% year-over-year decline
  • Camera retail: e-commerce holds 55% of US camera market share; tariffs raised retail prices 10-15% (April 2025 at 10%, February 2026 at 15%), compressing already razor-thin 1-2% margins
  • Tariff shock across devices: phones down 37%, gaming consoles down 58%, laptops down 68% during peak tariff period (2025); demand did not fully recover even as rates moderated to 13.7% (Feb 2026)

Who rides it

Amazon and the largest omnichannel players like Best Buy (one-third of revenue now from e-commerce) survive by owning digital distribution and pivoting to experiential retail in high-income markets. Online marketplaces (B&H, Adorama, eBay, MPB) that consolidated used-gear sales and rental services ride the shift: used camera gear sales projected to climb 13% annually, with eBay at 9.6% market share. Consumers who can navigate online pricing and tariff volatility gain access to wider selection and often lower prices (when tariffs don't spike). Professionals and enthusiasts find survival paths through rental services and specialized online communities.

Who's squeezed

Independent computer retailers (6.6% annual store closures), camera shops (down 7% per year annualized), and small-format consumer electronics chains lost to both Amazon's scale and tariff-driven cost floors. Consumers at lower and middle incomes were squeezed hardest: when tariffs spiked prices, budget systems were projected to fall 28%, and purchasing power simply evaporated. GameStop became the symbol of obsolescence — gaming went digital, digital distribution killed the retailer, and a failed attempt to acquire eBay couldn't reverse it. Sales associates and store employees (consumer electronics employment down 2.3% annually since 2021) lost jobs; $82.3 billion in 2022 physical retail shriveled into a much smaller experiential niche.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Tariff volatility is the single biggest risk to what remains. When tariffs spiked to 27% in April 2025, phone demand fell 37% and didn't recover fully even as rates moderated. The Section 122 surcharge was set to expire around July 2026, creating near-term pricing uncertainty heading into holiday shopping (2026). If tariffs reset or climb again, retailers already at 1-2% margins have zero room to absorb cost. The supply chain is still fragile: US computer imports from China collapsed 70%, forcing sourcing shifts to Mexico and Taiwan that add friction. PC memory shortages won't clear until the end of 2027. Any new tariff shock could finish off the remaining independent retailers and force Best Buy into even more aggressive consolidation or full retreat to e-commerce.

In one sentence

Electronics go online in a nutshell

Electronics went online first because prices became transparent, margins became indefensible, tariffs made physical inventory a liability, and Amazon had no rent.
