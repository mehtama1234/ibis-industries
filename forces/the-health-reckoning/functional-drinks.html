C · The health-aligned winners

Functional & Better-for-You Drinks

Energy drinks explode past $23 billion while juice rots; only sugar-free or premium survive.

The beverage market is splitting in two. Sugar-free energy drinks are booming—hitting $23.9 billion in 2025—while traditional juice has flatlined at $13.6 billion and quietly started losing customers. The dividing line is simple: drinks people feel good about buying versus the ones they've stopped believing in.

What changed

Energy drinks have nearly doubled since 2022, growing from $11.7 billion to $23.9 billion by 2025, but the real story is not growth—it's the category's complete reinvention. Sugar-free variants surpassed full-sugar drinks in dollar sales for the first time, driven by the fact that 70 percent of consumers now actively try to limit sugar intake. This is not a niche preference. Red Bull felt the pressure and launched Red Bull Zero in November 2024. Celsius didn't just capitalize on this shift; it went on an acquisition spree. The company paid $1.8 billion for Alani Nu in April 2025, a female-focused Gen Z energy drink brand, then secured Rockstar through a PepsiCo distribution deal in September 2025. The message was clear: the growth market was no longer men grabbing a can at a gas station—it was younger women and health-conscious consumers who'd been locked out of energy drinks because traditional options felt reckless.

Juice, by contrast, is trapped in structural decline. Americans cut juice consumption by 20 percent between 2019 and 2024, a staggering shift driven by a simple fact: people stopped believing juice was healthy. The 2025–2030 federal Dietary Guidelines now actually warn against 100 percent juice, and in August 2025 the FDA proposed lowering the sugar standard for orange juice from 10.5 percent to 10 percent Brix—the first downward cut since 1963. This was not random regulatory tinkering; it reflected both relentless health pressure and Florida's decimated citrus crop, which has been ravaged by citrus greening for years. Within the juice category, the shape of survival is clear: 100 percent orange juice fell from 67 percent of beverage share in 2022 to 62 percent by 2024, while cheaper orange-flavored drinks steal market share. Cold-pressed and premium juice segments are the only growth story, expanding at 8 percent annually from 2025 to 2030, because consumers willing to pay more still exist—they just want minimal processing and no added sugar. Brands like Naked Juice captured this shift early, and new premium entrants keep launching.

Tariffs made the damage worse. Starting in March 2025, a 25 percent tariff on Mexican imports hit the industry first. In April 2025, aluminum can tariffs of 25 percent kicked in. Then in August 2025, a 15 percent duty on EU brands landed. These layered tariffs squeezed margins exactly when juice makers were already bleeding volume and juice workers at the 318 establishments (and 23,500 employees) in the industry faced pressure to cut costs. Energy drink makers fared better because they operate at higher margins and their growth narrative let them absorb cost increases. The contrast in employment tells the story: energy drink businesses climbed 10.6 year-over-year to 251 establishments by 2025, with employment growing 10.3 percent annually from 2020 to 2025 to nearly 39,000 workers. Juice employment was essentially flat over the same period, and the number of juice businesses either held steady or contracted. Distribution also shifted: convenience stores still dominate energy drink sales at 65.5 percent, but warehouse clubs now capture 19 percent as bulk-buying consumers gravitate there, and supermarkets hold 10.9 percent. This mirrors the broader consumer behavior: people are moving from impulse to planned purchase.

The evidence

  • Energy drinks: $23.9 billion market in 2025, up from $11.7B in 2022, with 251 establishments and 38,954 employees (2025), fueled by 70% of consumers seeking to limit sugar intake
  • Juice: $13.6 billion market in 2026, flat from $13.0B in 2022, with 318 establishments and 23,500 employees, hit by 20% consumption decline 2019–2024
  • Celsius: Paid $1.8B for Alani Nu (April 2025) and secured Rockstar via PepsiCo distribution (September 2025), capturing female and health-conscious consumers
  • FDA: Proposed orange juice sugar standard cut to 10% Brix in August 2025, first reduction since 1963, reflecting health pressure and Florida's citrus greening crisis
  • Tariffs: 25% on Mexican juice imports (March 2025), 25% on aluminum cans (April 2025), 15% on EU brands (August 2025), compressing margins across the collapsed juice category

Who rides it

Celsius and other sugar-free energy drink makers are winning decisively. They control the growth narrative and can charge premium prices because consumers now actively want their products and believe in them. Celsius's billion-dollar acquisitions signal that the company is capturing market share from traditional energy and from people who'd never bought energy drinks before—women, fitness enthusiasts, and health-conscious drinkers. Premium and cold-pressed juice makers also win, expanding at 8 percent annually, because they're positioned as a different category altogether. Warehouse clubs and supermarkets also gain, capturing more energy drink sales as consumers trade convenience-store impulse buys for planned purchases.

Who's squeezed

Traditional orange juice makers are squeezed on three sides: consumption collapsed 20 percent since 2019, federal dietary guidelines now warn against their core product, and tariffs on Mexican imports, aluminum cans, and EU brands hit them simultaneously. Large juice manufacturers face structural decline and margin pressure. The 318 juice establishments and 23,500 workers across the industry are not growing jobs the way energy drink makers are (growing 10.3 percent annually). Convenience stores also lose as bulk-buying consumers shift to warehouse clubs. And Florida's citrus growers remain in crisis, with production barely recovering and the state having to invest $140 million in replanting efforts that won't yield for 3–5 years.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is regulatory overshoot against all sugar-containing beverages, not just juice. The FDA's August 2025 move to cut orange juice sugar standards signals that health pressure is now baked into policy, and if standards keep tightening, even sugar-free energy drinks could face new scrutiny—artificial sweeteners, caffeine content, or marketing claims could become targets. For the juice industry, the risk is permanent demand destruction: if a generation of consumers now believes juice is just sugar, that belief may never reverse even if products improve. For energy drink makers, the risk is that acquisition costs will spiral beyond the cash flows these brands actually generate, especially if growth slows. Employment in juice (23,500 workers) faces real layoff risk if consolidation accelerates, and Florida's citrus industry remains vulnerable to disease and tariff policy shifts.
