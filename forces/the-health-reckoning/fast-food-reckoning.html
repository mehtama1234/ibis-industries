Fast Food's Reckoning

GLP-1, labor floors, and tariff-driven inflation squeeze margin so hard that chains abandon traffic for upsells, reformulate in desperation, or vanish.

Fast food chains face a three-front collapse. The appetite-suppressing effect of GLP-1 drugs is real enough that dinner spending at these chains dropped 6% among regular users—households with someone taking GLP-1 cut restaurant spending an average 5.3%. At the same time, labor costs have jumped structurally: California's $20/hour minimum for national chains (April 2024) forced operators to raise menu prices 8–12%, while tariffs and commodity inflation pushed food costs up another 34% above pre-pandemic levels. Customers aren't coming as often (traffic fell 0.3% in 2025, accelerating to -2% in Q4 2025), and when they do, they're less likely to spend big. The industry that built its empire on affordability and frequency is now locked in a death grip between shrinking visits and rising costs.

What changed

The $423.7 billion US fast food industry grew just 0.7% in 2026, dragging a structural decline backward from the 1.2% CAGR posted between 2021 and 2026. What changed is that the margin (already thin at 5.1% in 2021) collapsed under three simultaneous pressures. First came the wage floor: California's FAST Act mandated $20/hour for national chains with 60+ locations starting April 2024, an overnight 25% jump in labor costs for the largest franchisees. That forced menu prices up 8–12%, but price-sensitive customers can't absorb that without abandoning the category. Overtime was largely eliminated, hours were cut, and operators scrambled to offset lower ticket counts with higher average checks (which ticked up 3.3% year-over-year in 2025), but that's a trap—raise your burger to $10 in a category built on the $6 lunch special, and traffic falls faster.

Second came the tariff and commodity squeeze. Avian flu hammered poultry supplies, low beef inventories tightened across the board, and roughly 15% of all US food imports faced new tariff pressure. Food inflation ran 34% above pre-pandemic levels by 2025–2026. Operators absorbed much of that cost in their own margin because they couldn't pass it fully to traffic-conscious customers without losing volume they could no longer afford. Sixty-two percent of chain restaurants raised menu prices anyway, and 60% reported deteriorated business conditions in 2025. Half of all chain restaurant operators—42% of the segment—turned unprofitable.

Third came GLP-1. Dinner spending at fast food fell 6% among regular users; that's not a rounding error, that's structural demand destruction. Households with a GLP-1 user cut restaurant spending an average 5.3%. The industry can quantify it now: traffic declined 0.3% in 2025, but Q4 2025 vs. Q4 2024 the decline accelerated to -2%. That's not just maturity, that's deflation happening in real time.

The chains betting on automation—AI-driven order systems, robotic fryers, wage-replacement tech—are placing long-dated bets to survive what used to be their core margin: labor efficiency at scale. But while they automate, their customer base is shrinking, and every price hike to offset labor costs pushes more traffic away.

The evidence

  • Fast food traffic declined 2% in Q4 2025 vs. Q4 2024, driven by GLP-1 appetite suppression and consumer retrenchment (2025)
  • California's FAST Act ($20/hour minimum for national chains, April 2024) forced menu prices up 8–12%, absorbing a 25% labor cost jump
  • Dinner spending at fast food fell 6% among GLP-1 users; households with a GLP-1 taker cut restaurant spending 5.3% (2025–2026)
  • Food inflation ran 34% above pre-pandemic levels in 2025–2026; tariffs on 15% of US food imports added structural pressure
  • 42% of chain restaurant operators turned unprofitable in 2025; 60% reported deteriorated business conditions

Who rides it

Value-focused casual dining (Chili's, Texas Roadhouse) and grocery/convenience store takeout are winning. Casual dining's $12 sit-down deals undercut fast-casual's $15 limited-service entrees, pulling traffic from the middle. Grocery and convenience stores absorb the traffic that once went to QSR—at-home eating, convenience-store prepared food, and delivery apps are all cheaper and require no GLP-1 patients to leave the house. Premium fast-casual chains that reformulated their menus (higher protein, lower sugar, smaller portions that align with appetite suppression) are holding share better than legacy QSR. Automation vendors and wage-management tech are winning too: chains desperate to offset labor are placing long bets on robotic systems and AI-driven order platforms. But the real winner is the home kitchen and the convenience store—the category itself is leaking traffic.

Who's squeezed

Legacy fast food—especially the volume-dependent value player—is squeezed hardest. BurgerFi filed Chapter 11 in September 2024 after losses mounted from labor inflation and a failed 2021 acquisition; it couldn't absorb the wage shock. Casual dining took a hammer: TGI Fridays and Red Lobster both filed Chapter 11 in 2024 and closed 130+ locations combined. FAT Brands (Smokey Bones parent) filed Chapter 11 in January 2026; all remaining Smokey Bones locations closed. On The Border Mexican Grill filed Chapter 11 in March 2025; Bravo Brio filed a second time in August 2025. Even mid-market chains going private (Denny's sold for $620 million all-cash to a private-equity consortium in Q1 2026, leaving public markets after decades) signals that the operational model is broken. Franchisees in California and other high-wage states face an impossible math: they can't raise prices without losing traffic, and they can't absorb labor costs without cutting margin to zero.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is a debt spiral for overleveraged chains. Fast food is built on the franchisee model: individuals borrow against future cash flows to buy the right to operate a location. As traffic shrinks 2% per quarter and operators turn unprofitable (42% of chain restaurants in 2025), franchisees can't service debt or reinvest. Many locations will close quietly, creating a wave of underperforming assets. Large chains that double down on automation to replace labor are betting the company on technology adoption they can't fully control and customer willingness to pay for convenience—but if customers stop coming altogether, the robots run empty. The second-order risk is that as premium casual dining (Chili's, Roadhouse) captures traffic with sit-down deals, even those gains look fragile if the consumer recession deepens and households tighten further. GLP-1 adoption is also accelerating—if 5-10% of the population is on a GLP-1 analog by 2027, the demand destruction at fast food could move from a rounding error to a permanent 15–20% traffic erosion that no amount of automation or menu engineering can fix.

In one sentence

Fast food's reckoning in a nutshell

GLP-1 appetite suppression, $20/hour wage floors, and 34% food inflation have crushed the fast food model so thoroughly that chains are choosing automation over traffic, raising prices that drive away the customer base, or filing bankruptcy.
