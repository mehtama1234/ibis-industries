D · The tensions

Regulation & the Warning Label

Health warnings and sugar rules turn compliance into a repricing event.

Regulation doesn't kill the food and drink business. But it does make what you were selling yesterday suddenly cost more to sell today. A reformulation mandate, a tariff regime, or an FDA ban forces a reset: you either pay to change the formula, absorb the cost hit, or step aside. That reset is happening now, and it's remaking the energy drink and candy industries at the same time.

What changed

The US energy drink industry has doubled from $11.7 billion in 2022 to $23.9 billion by 2025—not because people drink more, but because the category is being remade. Sugar-free drinks now outsell full-sugar drinks for the first time, driven by consumers determined to cut sugar. Red Bull launched Red Bull Zero in November 2024 to keep pace. Celsius paid $1.8 billion for Alani Nu in April 2025 and then snapped up Rockstar through a distribution deal with PepsiCo in September 2025, racing to own the premium end before regulatory pressure and health consciousness make the sugar-heavy names untouchable. The FDA's ban on brominated vegetable oil (BVO) in August 2025 forced reformulation across dozens of brands—a small shock that signals bigger ones coming.

Tariffs have become a structural cost that reshapes economics overnight. When the Trump administration imposed tariffs in April 2025—10% baseline, 20% on EU goods, 31% on Swiss chocolate, and 145% on bulk shipments from China—candy production costs jumped 30% between December 2024 and September 2025. The US candy market, valued at $55 billion in 2025, is now projected to grow only 1.4% per year through 2030, a dramatic slowdown from earlier decades. That margin squeeze is forcing the industry to abandon cheap, commodity sugar drops in favor of premiumized, functional, and texture-driven innovations. Freeze-dried candy jumped to $1.31 billion globally in 2025 and is forecast to grow 12% annually through 2033, fueled by Gen Z craving crunch and seeing it on TikTok. Mars acquired Kellanov in December 2025—bringing Pringles, Cheez-It, and Pop-Tarts under the same roof—while Unilever paid $1.2 billion for the functional gummy brand Grüns in April 2026, betting that functional and premium are where the margin survives.

What makes this different from past regulatory cycles is speed and simultaneity. The BVO ban, the tariff regime, and the consumer shift all hit within months of each other, compressing the timeline for reformulation and repositioning. A company sitting in commodity sugar or standard production—the traditional playbook—faces tariff costs on imports, margin pressure on domestic production, and consumer indifference to what it's selling. A company owning a premium or functional asset, or one with the scale to absorb tariff shocks (Mars, Mondelēz), can raise prices or shift to higher-margin categories. Convenience stores, which took 65.5% of energy drink sales, are losing ground to warehouse clubs (19%) and supermarkets (10.9%) as bulk-buying and price-conscious consumers shift. The warning label—whether literal (FDA guidance on sugar and additives) or figurative (social media calling out synthetic ingredients)—has become a repricing event. Brands that can't reformulate or pivot up are stuck: too cheap to grow, too risky to hold.

The evidence

  • Energy drinks: Celsius acquired Alani Nu for $1.8B (April 2025) and Rockstar (September 2025) as sugar-free positioning becomes mandatory and regulatory risk makes legacy sugar brands untouchable.
  • Candy: Tariffs (10% baseline, 20% EU, 31% Switzerland, 145% China as of April 2025) drove production costs up 30% by September 2025, compressing margins on commodity sugar and forcing a pivot to premium and functional.
  • Regulation: FDA ban on brominated vegetable oil (BVO) in August 2025 forced reformulation across energy drinks and candies, signaling coming waves of additive and sugar scrutiny.
  • Freeze-dried candy: Jumped to $1.31B globally in 2025 and forecast to grow 12% annually through 2033, becoming a major escape route from commodity sugar positioning.
  • Consumer shift: 70% of consumers seek to limit sugar intake in 2025, and 47% of regular candy buyers actively seek healthier alternatives, making health-aligned positioning essential for survival.

Who rides it

Brands with scale and premium positioning—Mars (Kellanov acquisition December 2025, combined $36B global snacking portfolio), Mondelēz, and Celsius—can absorb tariff costs and shift to higher-margin categories. Functional and freeze-dried innovations, which grew at double digits, shelter from commodity economics. Companies that own the health-conscious narrative (Red Bull Zero, Alani Nu, Rockstar under premium distribution) and can reformulate without destroying margins survive the regulatory shock. Warehouse clubs (now 19% of energy drink sales) and supermarkets (10.9%) gain share as bulk-buying and price-sensitive consumers exit convenience stores (falling from 65.5%) seeking value in the new normal.

Who's squeezed

Mid-sized brands without reformulation budgets or premium assets get trapped: tariff shocks compress already-thin margins on commodity sugar products, and regulatory pressure makes old formulas legally or reputationally risky. Suppliers of additive-laden or high-sugar products lose shelf space as 70% of consumers demand lower sugar and brands move toward functional alternatives. Convenience stores, which relied on impulse purchases of high-margin, commodity energy drinks and candy, lose share to warehouse clubs and supermarkets. Smaller confectionery producers, already under pressure from the consolidated majors (Mars, Mondelēz, Ferrara), face tariff costs with no scale to negotiate or ability to pivot to premium positioning.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is a regulatory cascade. The BVO ban and tariff regime hit nearly simultaneously; if the FDA moves to restrict high-fructose corn syrup, further limit caffeine levels, or mandate sugar-reduction targets (as some countries have), the reformulation pipeline will choke. Brands already repricing to absorb tariffs cannot easily absorb a second round of compliance costs. Smaller producers will exit or consolidate, handing even more market power to Mars and Mondelēz. Consumer resentment at rising prices (86% notice and resent them in 2025) could trigger a retail or political backlash against tariffs, destabilizing the very cost environment companies are repricing into. Worse: if health-conscious consumers keep shrinking the addressable market for traditional candy and sugar-heavy energy drinks, the tariff-driven price increases will feel punitive rather than sustainable, leaving domestic producers with no escape route—they cannot lower costs enough to compete, and cannot raise prices enough to survive.

In one sentence

Regulation & the warning label in a nutshell

Regulation—FDA bans, tariff shocks, and the shift to cleaner labels—has made old-school sugar drinks and candies risky to hold, pushing billions into reformulation and reshuffling who survives.
