C · The health-aligned winners

What you drink now

Coffee and dairy face margin collapse; only health-aligned and premium survive.

Coffee shops and dairies are getting crushed. Arabica prices jumped 18.5% year-over-year by April 2026, tariffs keep whipsawing supply chains, and consumers won't absorb the cost. Milk prices crashed to $19.25 per hundredweight in 2026 from much higher peaks, bankruptcies surged 46% in 2025, and plant-based alternatives now own over 60% of the organic dairy market. In this squeeze, only two kinds of business survive: the premium chain that can afford to invest in technology and experience, or the health-aligned niche that owns a specific reason people choose it.

What changed

The coffee and dairy industries entered a margin death spiral in 2025-2026. For coffee shops, it started with commodity shocks: Arabica prices surged 21% year-over-year in August 2025 and stayed 18.5% higher in April 2026 compared to the year before. Then tariffs arrived—10% to 50% rates in spring 2025, removed in November, then 10% reinstated in February 2026—creating a constant whipsaw that left operators unable to predict costs or lock in pricing. Starbucks, the category's largest player, saw North America operating income fall 41% to $3.2 billion in fiscal 2025 from $5.4 billion the year before. The company tried to compensate by investing $500 million in extra shifts (500,000 additional hours in Q2 2025 alone) to handle mobile order congestion, but that only burned cash faster. Three significant bankruptcies—Compass Coffee (which closed 10 of 30 locations), Red Bay Coffee, and The Blend—signaled that independents and fast-growth concepts had no margin to absorb the shock.

Dairy faced an even sharper cliff. Milk prices collapsed from 2022 highs to $21.05 per hundredweight in 2025, then were forecast to fall further to $19.25 per hundredweight in 2026. This deflation squeezed producer margins while production actually rose 1.3% in 2026. Dairy farm bankruptcies surged 46% year-over-year in 2025, with 315 Chapter 12 filings and another 185 in the top 24 dairy states alone—structural consolidation is no longer debatable. Wisconsin, the nation's second-largest dairy state, has lost 76% of its herds over decades; what remains is consolidating at speed. Meanwhile, plant-based alternatives grew 13.8% annually and captured 62.8% of the organic dairy segment by 2026, fracturing the market. Labor made it worse: the US shed 155,000 agricultural workers between March and July 2025, and only about 5% of US farms had robotic milking versus 20% to 25% in Denmark and the Netherlands.

Into this wreckage came tariffs. In July 2026, the Trump administration imposed 50% duties on select Canadian dairy imports (whey, powders, casein) while exempting cheese and fluid milk. The tariff created winners and losers overnight: cheese demand stayed resilient (24.5% market share), but powders and whey—inputs for premium infant formula, protein products, and supplement brands—faced a cost shock that only large players could absorb. Consolidation accelerated: Leprino Foods closed old California facilities (Lemoore and Tracy) and opened a high-capacity plant in Lubbock, Texas. General Mills divested Yoplait yogurt and :ratio brands in 2025 for $2.1 billion to Lactalis USA, reshaping North American yogurt competition around scale and efficiency. The coffee industry did the same when Keurig Dr Pepper closed an $18 billion acquisition of JDE Peet's in April 2026, creating Global Coffee Co. with 50+ brands and $16 billion in sales—the world's largest pure-play coffee company.

What made it through this shakeout were two types: brands that commanded premium pricing because they offered health, performance, or experience worth the cost, or mega-players that could absorb tariffs and commodity swings through scale and vertical integration. Coffee shops that pivoted to cold brew, adaptogenic blends, or high-protein formats kept customers despite 59% of consumers buying via drive-thru and 38% via app in fall 2025. Dairy brands with a health story—organic, A2 casein, grass-fed, probiotic yogurt—captured margin while commodity milk foundered. The mass-market, low-margin, volume-dependent middle got deleted.

The evidence

  • Coffee shops: Arabica prices 18.5% higher in April 2026 than April 2025; Starbucks North America operating income fell 41% to $3.2 billion (FY2025) from $5.4 billion (FY2024).
  • Dairy farms: Milk prices forecast at $19.25 per hundredweight in 2026, down from $21.05/cwt in 2025; Chapter 12 bankruptcies surged 46% to 315 filings in 2025.
  • Consolidation: Keurig acquired JDE Peet's for $18 billion (April 2026); Leprino closed California plants and opened Lubbock, Texas expansion (2025-2026).
  • Plant-based growth: Dairy alternatives grew 13.8% CAGR and captured 62.8% of organic dairy by 2026, fragmenting traditional milk volumes.
  • Tariffs: Trump administration imposed 50% duties on Canadian whey, powders, casein in July 2026 (effective August 2026), exempting cheese and fluid milk.

Who rides it

Premium coffee chains like Starbucks that invested in technology (mobile orders 38% of sales in fall 2025, drive-thru 59%) and could absorb commodity and labor costs survived. Health-focused dairy brands—organic, A2 casein, grass-fed, probiotic yogurt—captured margin while commodity milk crashed. Mega-consolidators like Keurig Dr Pepper and Lactalis USA obtained pricing power and tariff absorption capacity that smaller competitors lacked. General Mills divested Yoplait to Lactalis for $2.1 billion, betting that scale and health positioning trumped mass-market yogurt.

Who's squeezed

Independents and fast-growth coffee concepts (Compass, Red Bay, The Blend) had no margin buffer to absorb 21% commodity spikes and tariff whipsaw. Dairy farmers producing commodity milk faced collapsing prices ($19.25/cwt in 2026) while production rose and competition from plant-based alternatives (62.8% of organic) picked off their volume. Small cheese makers and milk processors lacked the scale to navigate 50% tariffs on whey and powders. Wisconsin dairy herds, already down 76% over decades, shed farms faster as consolidation accelerated and labor remained unavailable (only 5% mechanized versus 20-25% in Denmark).

The main worry

The single biggest risk

If commodity prices don't rebound and tariffs persist into 2027, the coffee and dairy industries risk permanent volume loss to at-home consumption, convenience QSRs (which poached coffee sales via drive-thru automation), and plant-based alternatives. Coffee shops' narrow margins mean any further tariff spike could trigger a chain-reaction bankruptcy of regional players and accelerate consolidation to the top three-to-five global chains. For dairy, the risk is structural: consolidation eliminates farm diversity and pushes remaining producers into commodity-scale competition, while plant-based alternatives keep chipping away at premium segments. If the administration extends tariffs to cheese and fluid milk in 2027, consumer prices could spike and trigger lasting demand destruction.
