B · The retreat from alcohol

Spirits in Contraction

The bourbon glut, tariff wars, and younger drinkers opting out are turning a 20-year boom into a crisis of capacity and survival.

For two decades, American whiskey rode a wave of premiumization. Distilleries expanded to meet demand, aged barrels accumulated, and craft operations multiplied. But 2023 was the inflection. Whiskey sales went negative for the first time in 20+ years. By 2025, the industry wasn't just cooling—it was contracting hard, caught between massive unsold inventory, tariff cliffs, and younger consumers who'd simply stopped drinking spirits the way their parents did.

What changed

The boom built the glut. Between 2018 and 2022, the whiskey industry grew at 6%+ per year, convincing distilleries they needed to keep expanding. Production ramped, barrels piled up in Kentucky warehouses, aged inventory grew. Then demand flattened. Bourbon whiskey sales fell 4.9% by volume and 5.1% by revenue in the 12 months ending July 2025. Major brands cratered: Bulleit down 7%, Wild Turkey down 8%. The industry now sits on 16+ million barrels—a record high—with no clear off-ramp. Prices for aged barrels have fallen 30–40% in four years. Jim Beam, a century-old producer, halted production entirely at its flagship Clermont, Kentucky facility for all of 2026. MGP, one of the largest suppliers, slashed output and watched gross profits fall 68%.

But the tariff crisis is what's turned contraction into reckoning. American whiskey exports fell 3.8% to $2.37 billion in 2025. The EU's 25% retaliatory tariff—briefly suspended—didn't rekindle demand; it just moved timelines and costs. In March 2025, Canada banned US spirits from provincial retailers. Then in August 2026, the Trump administration imposed a 50% tariff on Canadian spirits, and the spiral tightened. For distilleries that counted on international sales, especially the premium segment that feeds a global luxury market, these tariffs aren't temporary friction—they're a reboot of supply relationships, pricing, and market share. Whiskey that can't ship at a profit doesn't get made.

The third wave hit distribution. RNDC, the second-largest spirits distributor in America, filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy in July 2026 with liabilities between $1 and $10 billion. The firm owed creditors roughly $400 million and lost $3 billion in annual supplier relationships in the prior three years. That's not a routine financial reset—that's systemic. Every distillery had to renegotiate distribution with Reyes, Breakthru, Martignetti, or Columbia as RNDC's book was carved up. Smaller producers scrambled. New players like Siren Spirits Group, a women-owned collective, emerged to pool distribution power. Meanwhile, younger drinkers (Gen Z) had already shifted ground. IWSR forecasted a 0.4% global beverage alcohol decline for 2025. The sober-curious wave, GLP-1 adoption, and the retreat from sugar hit spirits uniquely hard: there's no "light" bourbon, no "functional" whiskey. What grew instead was spirit-based ready-to-drink (RTD) cocktails, which jumped 16.4% in 2025 to $3.8 billion and are projected to grow 20.5% annually through 2033. Those capture younger, convenience-seeking drinkers who want pre-made, portion-controlled drinks. But RTDs eat margin.

The market size tells the story. The distilleries industry, a $36.8 billion sector in 2024, is expected to shrink to $21.7 billion revenue by 2026. Margins fell from 21.3% in 2021 to a projected 19.3% by 2026 as wage pressure and competition erode returns. The bourbon and whiskey segment specifically—$6.3 billion in 2025—contracted 2.8% year over year. Craft distillers, while growing in raw count to 3,069 by 2024, face severe margin pressure and fight for shelf space against entrenched brands. The industry is shedding jobs: Brown-Forman, a pillar, cut 12% of its workforce in 2025. Those 1.2 million jobs the spirits industry supports aren't safe.

The evidence

  • Bourbon distilleries: 16+ million barrels in storage (Jan 2025), Jim Beam halts all production for 2026, and barrel prices fell 30–40% in four years
  • Whiskey exports: fell 3.8% to $2.37 billion in 2025 as tariffs (25% EU, 50% Canada as of Aug 2026) cut global sales
  • Distribution crisis: RNDC bankruptcy in July 2026 with $1–10B liabilities and $400M in unpaid creditors disrupted every supplier relationship
  • Younger drinkers: IWSR forecasts 0.4% global beverage alcohol decline for 2025; spirit-based RTDs grow 16.4% (capturing convenience-seekers, not core spirits volume)
  • Industry contraction: Bourbon whiskey segment at $6.3B (2025), down 2.8% YoY; distilleries industry expected to shrink from $36.8B (2024) to $21.7B (2026)

Who rides it

Super-premium whiskey producers and owner-operators can survive if they have direct-to-consumer channels and deep supply chains. They're competing on quality and scarcity, not volume. Tequila and mezcal benefit by default—they don't face bourbon's specific glut, and younger drinkers see them as fresher, less dad-coded. Ready-to-drink spirits, especially those branded for women and Gen Z convenience, are winning hard: RTD cocktails posted 16.4% growth in 2025 and project 20.5% annual growth through 2033. Acquisitions by large firms like Gallo (buying Four Roses for up to $775M) and Bacardi (taking full control of Teeling Irish Whiskey) signal that size and balance-sheet strength matter when the industry contracts. New distribution collectives like Siren Spirits Group emerge to pool power and avoid the RNDC trap. Spirits that don't age (vodka, gin, clear spirits) dodge the barrel-glut problem, though tariffs still hurt exports.

Who's squeezed

Small and mid-sized craft distilleries face erasure. Three thousand craft distillers operate today, but profitability has evaporated. They compete for shelf space, fight tariff costs, and can't leverage scale like Brown-Forman or Diageo. Bourbon producers tied to export markets watch 19% export declines (2025) and 50% tariff threats (2026) swallow margins. Traditional on-premise channels (bars, restaurants) are losing share to off-premise retail, squeezing the operators who built relationships on handshake deals. Brown-Forman, an icon, cut 12% of its workforce in 2025, and a failed $30B merger with Pernod Ricard signaled even giants are under stress. Wholesalers and distributors got hammered: RNDC's bankruptcy in July 2026 was a $1–10B crater that reorganized the entire supply chain overnight. Regional distributors and smaller suppliers lost $3 billion in annual relationships. Kentucky distillers tied to barrel aging watch prices fall 30–40% in four years with no recovery in sight.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The tariff floor could stay permanent. If the 50% tariff on Canadian spirits and the 25% suspension on EU whiskey expire without renewal (the EU tariff suspension ends Feb 5, 2026, and was not renewed as of July 2026), international sales—a lifeline for premium producers—vanish. Distilleries built on export demand face stranded assets: aged barrels worth half what they cost to produce. The barrel glut is self-reinforcing. As long as 16+ million barrels sit unsold, prices stay depressed, and aging inventory ties up capital that smaller producers can't afford to lock up. There's no clear mechanism for managed drawdown. If even one major distillery fails (Brown-Forman is stressed, Sazerac rejected a $15B bid), cascade effects ripple through suppliers, distributors, and retailers. The shift to RTDs and lower-alcohol drinks is structural, not cyclical—it won't reverse. Any recovery depends on production staying cut and inventory slowly bleeding off. If demand doesn't return, the industry is smaller, permanently.

Spirits in contraction in a nutshell

Premium spirits face a perfect storm: 16+ million barrels in storage, tariffs cutting exports by a third, and Gen Z shifting to lower-alcohol drinks.
