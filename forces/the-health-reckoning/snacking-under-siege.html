Snacking under siege

The drug that changed appetite.

GLP-1 drugs didn't invent health consciousness, but they turbocharged it. When your appetite shrinks and your body signals satiation faster, indulgent snacking becomes structurally irrational. The result is a bifurcated snacking landscape where traditional high-calorie, high-sugar categories face declining volume, tariffs that make them more expensive, and consumer scrutiny that wasn't there five years ago. Only the premium, the functional, and the texture-driven are growing.

What changed

The American candy industry illustrates the exact squeeze. The total US confectionery market (candy, chocolate, imports combined) sits at $55 billion in 2025, with modest growth of just 1.4% annually projected through 2030. But inside that flat number is a collapse: traditional hard candies and soft candies are shrinking as a share of total consumption. What's growing is chocolate (now 51.7% of all confectionery sales), freeze-dried candy (which jumped from niche to $1.31 billion globally in 2025 and is forecast to grow 12% annually through 2033), and functional formats like sugar-free and vitamin-fortified gummies. The gap is wider among consumers who actively seek healthier alternatives—47% of regular candy buyers now do—meaning the remaining volume is increasingly concentrated in premium, texture-forward, and health-aligned products. Mars' definitive acquisition of Kellanov, finalized in December 2025 for $36 billion, and Ferrero's $3.1 billion acquisition of WK Kellogg in September 2025 show that scale players are abandoning the hope that traditional volume returns; instead, they are building platforms to compete across premium, health-conscious, and value segments simultaneously. Meanwhile, tariffs have become a structural cost catastrophe. Starting in April 2025, the Trump administration imposed tariffs of 10% baseline on all imports, 20% from the EU, 31% from Switzerland, and 145% on bulk shipments from China. Chocolate production costs jumped 30% between December 2024 and September 2025 alone. Eighty-six percent of consumers now notice and resent higher prices, yet domestic producers cannot absorb these costs and remain competitive; imported goods still meet roughly 23% of domestic candy demand, meaning tariffs pinch without fully protecting home producers. The industry responded by premium-ifying: Ferrara launched freeze-dried SweeTARTS, Lemonheads, and Spree in August 2025; M&Ms introduced POP'd Caramel in early 2026; and Unilever paid $1.2 billion for Grüns, a functional gummy brand, in April 2026. Ice cream tells a parallel story. The US ice cream market is $20.03 billion in 2026, growing at only 2.66% annually through 2031. Regular bulk ice cream is in decline; what's growing are single-serve impulse products (42% of market), artisanal premium formats, and plant-based alternatives. Plant-based ice cream rocketed from $4.25 billion in 2025 to $5.15 billion in 2026—a 21% year-over-year jump—driven by health consciousness and perceived alignment with wellness trends. Unilever's decision to spin off its ice cream arm as The Magnum Company in December 2025, valuing it at $9.1 billion, signals that legacy CPG giants no longer see frozen desserts as a growth engine; they are retreating from volume and allowing specialized players to dominate premium tiers. Tariff retaliation and domestic dairy input costs have compounded the pain: China imposed a 125% retaliatory tariff on US ice cream exports in 2026, while tariffs on Canadian dairy are raising milk and butter costs domestically. Americans spent $31 on ice cream in May 2025, a 26.5% month-over-month jump, but that's pure inflation pass-through, not volume growth. The cookie, cracker, and pasta sector ($68.3 billion in 2026, growing 3.8% annually) is experiencing similar bifurcation. Health-conscious offerings are exploding: gluten-free sales grew 95% year-over-year in 2025, and organic options grew 75%, driven by celiac awareness and wellness trends. Yet tariffs remain a brutal lever. The US Commerce Department proposed tariffs reaching 91.74% on Italian pasta makers in September 2025; after review, rates were reduced to 2–14% in early 2026, but the uncertainty alone forced retailers and manufacturers to choose between absorbing costs or passing them to consumers. Italian pasta exports to the US, worth roughly $800 million annually, are now in constant jeopardy. Mars, Ferrero, and Flowers Foods (which acquired Simple Mills for $795 million in December 2024) are all betting that the future of snacking lies in scale-to-many-segments, not volume growth. In each category, the structural trend is identical: appetite suppression narrows volume, tariffs make indulgence expensive, and health consciousness makes indulgence ideologically costly.

The evidence

  • Confectionery: Candy market $55B in 2025, 1.4% CAGR to 2030; 47% of buyers seek healthier alternatives; freeze-dried candy $1.31B globally 2025, 12% CAGR.
  • Ice cream: $20.03B market 2026, 2.66% CAGR; plant-based jumped 21% YoY to $5.15B in 2026 vs. 2.66% total market growth.
  • Tariffs: 10% baseline, 20% EU, 31% Switzerland, 145% China imposed April 2025; chocolate costs +30% Dec 2024–Sept 2025; 86% of consumers notice.
  • M&A: Mars-Kellanov $36B December 2025; Ferrero-WK Kellogg $3.1B September 2025; Flowers-Simple Mills $795M December 2024.
  • Pasta tariffs: US Commerce proposed 91.74% tariffs September 2025, reduced to 2–14% early 2026; Italian pasta exports to US ~$800M annually now at risk.

Who rides it

Premium, functional, and health-aligned snack makers are winning. Freeze-dried candy, plant-based ice cream, functional gummies, gluten-free and organic crackers, and single-serve premium formats are all outpacing their traditional counterparts by 5–10x. Scale players like Mars, Ferrero, and Nestlé are winning by acquiring platforms that let them compete across all segments simultaneously—they've given up on volume growth and instead optimized for margin capture in premium tiers while maintaining value shelf space. Texture-forward innovation (freeze-dried, crunch, novelty formats) is a moat that GLP-1 appetites naturally support: if you eat less, you want it to count.

Who's squeezed

Traditional high-calorie, high-sugar snack manufacturers are getting squeezed on three sides. First, volume is structurally declining as GLP-1 use spreads and health consciousness deepens; 47% of candy buyers now seek alternatives, and plant-based ice cream is growing 21% annually while regular ice cream grows 2.66%. Second, tariffs have made indulgent snacks expensive—chocolate costs jumped 30% in nine months, and 86% of consumers resent the prices. Third, retail shelf space and manufacturer investment are shifting to premium and functional tiers, meaning traditional candy, cookies, and regular ice cream are losing the war for attention and promotion. Small, regional candy makers cannot absorb tariff costs and cannot afford the scale to play across multiple segments, leaving them vulnerable to acquisition or exit.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is margin collapse for mid-market snack producers trapped between tariff cost floors and consumer price resistance. A manufacturer like an Italian pasta maker or a regional candy producer faces tariffs of 91–145% (or retaliatory exposure), cannot raise retail prices without losing volume to premiumized alternatives, and cannot cut costs fast enough to offset input inflation. Unilever's decision to spin off ice cream signals that even legacy CPG giants see frozen desserts as a value-trap; for smaller players, the trap has no exit. Consolidation into a handful of mega-platforms (Mars, Ferrero, Nestlé, Mondelēz) may be inevitable, but that process can be catastrophic for communities and supplier networks that depended on now-acquired regional businesses.

Snacking under siege in a nutshell
