B · The retreat from alcohol

The Sober-Curious Wave

Younger Americans drink less; alcohol venues face structural decline as GLP-1 adoption and wellness reshape what gets ordered.

Drinking in America just hit a 90-year low. In August 2025, adult drinking participation fell to 54%—the lowest on record. At the same time, GLP-1 medications (used by 10–15% of U.S. adults) are cutting alcohol spending by 30% when out and 22% at home. The result is a sharp split: upscale, experiential venues survive. Everyone else shrinks.

What changed

Gen Z drinks differently than Millennials did at the same age—30% less, to be precise. Since 2019, monthly wine occasions among younger drinkers have collapsed by 34%. This is not a recession effect. It is structural. The Surgeon General issued an advisory in 2025 linking alcohol to nearly 100,000 annual cancer cases, while simultaneously the 2025 Dietary Guidelines removed explicit daily drink limits. That mixed message buried the lede: consumption is falling, and when younger people do drink, they're buying fewer drinks per night (3.9 in 2025 versus 4.4 in 2024) but spending more per drink when they do.\n\nThe pain shows up first in volume. Breweries saw production drop 5.1% in 2025 and another 4% in the first half of 2026. In June 2026, 9,344 craft breweries operated in the U.S., down 1.8% year-over-year, with 481 closures in 2025 alone. Wine bars—a $3.4 billion market in 2026—grew just -0.7% that year, having only doubled from $1.9 billion in 2022. On-premise bar and nightclub visits dropped 3.4% in 2025 versus 2024. The bars and nightclubs sector, worth $38.6 billion in 2026, is projected to grow only 1.5% annualized through 2030. Margins have eroded: bar and nightclub margins fell from 6.8% in 2022 to 5.4% in 2026.\n\nThe culprit is not one force but three stacked together. First, GLP-1 adoption reduces appetite for alcohol—both the urge to drink and the budget for it. Studies show 30% drops in on-premise alcohol spending and 22% drops in home consumption among GLP-1 users. Second, younger drinkers are deliberately moderating: 65% of Gen Z report intent to reduce alcohol in 2025, citing health and wellness (71%) and price sensitivity (56%). Third, cost inflation is real. Aluminum prices jumped 15% year-over-year in 2025. Tariffs on imported spirits pushed prices 15–20% higher by mid-2026. Labor costs in nightclubs exceed $25/hour in many markets. These forces don't just thin volume; they crush independents.\n\nThe industry is bifurcating sharply. Modern wine bars scaled by private equity are growing 6.8% CAGR and achieving 22% higher customer retention than traditional formats by investing in experiential elements—staff expertise, events, ambiance. Among craft breweries, only 54% of surveyed operations reported growth in 2025–2026; 43% reported declines. The winners are large producers with automation and scale, and small operators who pivot to experiential models—taproom-first, food programs, local sourcing. The losers are mid-sized regional breweries and traditional independents squeezed by labor, tariffs, and volume decline. Non-alcoholic beer is the outlier: U.S. NA volume jumped 111% from 2021 to 2025. Alcohol-free cocktails grew 36% in 2025. These are not substitutes; they are admissions that the drinking occasion itself is under pressure."

The evidence

  • Wine bars (part of $3.4B market, 2026): customer bifurcation accelerating—Gen Z wine occasions down 34% since 2019; affluent 55+ remain loyal; GLP-1 users cut on-premise spending 30%.
  • Breweries (9,344 operating in June 2026, down 1.8% YoY): production fell 5.1% in 2025 and 4% H1 2026; only 54% of surveyed breweries report growth; 481 closures in 2025.
  • Bars and nightclubs ($38.6B market, 2026): on-premise visits dropped 3.4% in 2025; margins fell from 6.8% (2022) to 5.4% (2026); Gen Z showing 'selective premiumization'—fewer drinks per night (3.9 vs 4.4), higher per-drink spend.
  • Tariffs: aluminum up 15% YoY in 2025; imported spirits 15–20% higher by mid-2026; labor costs exceed $25/hour, squeezing mid-sized breweries and traditional bars.
  • Non-alcoholic alternatives: NA beer volume jumped 111% from 2021–2025; alcohol-free cocktails up 36% in 2025; experiential venues adding themed nights and alcohol-free offerings growing fastest.

Who rides it

Upscale, experiential venues win. Private-equity-scaled wine bars are growing 6.8% CAGR and achieving 22% higher retention by layering in expertise, events, and ambiance. Large national breweries and spirits makers with automation and scale are consolidating market share and shifting portfolio weight to premium and non-alcoholic segments. Themed bars surged 32.9% in 2025. Alcohol-free cocktail venues and non-alcoholic beer makers are the only beverage category in double-digit growth. The affluent 55+ demographic remains loyal to established venues but is aging. The winners are those who can charge more per drink or occasion and build loyalty through experience, not volume.

Who's squeezed

Mid-sized regional breweries and traditional independent bars and wine bars lose. They face a triple squeeze: volume decline, cost inflation they cannot pass through, and labor retention challenges. Smaller breweries without economies of scale watch labor costs exceed $25/hour, tariff-driven packaging costs (aluminum, cans, bottles) jump 15%+, and consumer traffic decline 3–5% annually. Gen Z price sensitivity (56% cite it as a reason to cut back) destroys the lower-end venue model. Independent wine bars stay profitable only by serving older, affluent customers—a shrinking demographic. Businesses filing for insolvency or Chapter 11 restructuring in 2025–2026 include Alamo Beer, Duck-Rabbit Craft Brewery, and Nebraska Brewing Company. The independents that survive pivot hard to experiential or close.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that the decline is structural, not cyclical—meaning no recovery on the horizon. Gen Z drinks 30% less than Millennials at the same age. Adult drinking hit a 90-year low in August 2025 and is not rebounding. GLP-1 adoption is still climbing (now 10–15% of adults, but expected to reach 25%+ within five years). Even affluent drinkers are moderating frequency and occasions. If younger cohorts do not increase consumption as they age (the historical norm breaks), the total addressable market shrinks permanently. Tariffs and labor costs may ease, but volume will not return. The margin compression is durable.

In one sentence

The sober-curious wave in a nutshell

Drinking is becoming a premium, experiential niche rather than a volume business, leaving mid-market venues and breweries structurally squeezed.
