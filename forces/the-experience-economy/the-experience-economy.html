E · The big picture

The Experience Economy

Spending shifts decisively from ownership to spectacle—who's riding the wave and who's drowning in it.

The American consumer is remaking the budget. From 2022 to 2026, the money moving to amusement parks, bars, boutique hotels, and premium entertainment is reshaping which businesses thrive and which disappear. Ownership of things was the 20th-century middle-class dream. Experiences—memories, status, escape—are becoming the new measure of living well. And that shift is brutal for some industries and lucrative for others.

What changed

The experience economy made one thing newly precious: the moment itself. When people chose to spend $33.4 billion at amusement parks in 2026 (up 9.7% CAGR since 2022), they were voting for spectacle over spare bedrooms. Disney's parks pulled in 76.5 million domestic visitors in 2024, and Universal's Epic Universe alone is projected to hit 9.2 million visits by 2026. The sheer attendance numbers tell the real story: people are willing to pay premiums—peak Disney tickets hit $224 at Disneyland in October 2025—for the promise of escape, memory, status. Amusement parks grew from $14.3 billion in 2022 to $33.4 billion in 2026, and the number of establishments jumped 7.0% annually. What this force made valuable was scale, innovation, and the ability to charge repeatedly for the same customer across a season or year.

What it made scarce was commodity appeal. Bars and nightclubs still command $38.6 billion in revenue in 2026, but real margins have compressed from 6.8% in 2022 to 5.4% by 2026. Younger drinkers are drinking less (65% of Gen Z report intent to reduce alcohol in 2025) but paying more when they do—selective premiumization. Labor costs in a typical nightclub run $70,416 per month with 50-70% annual bartender turnover, so venue owners face an impossible choice: cut service quality to preserve margin, or absorb wage inflation and hope themed experiences (up 32.9% in 2025) and alcohol-free cocktails (up 36% year-over-year) attract enough new traffic. Meanwhile, imported spirits tariffs lifted prices 15-20% above 2024 levels by mid-2026, hitting premium bottle service hardest. The bifurcation is real: upscale cocktail lounges with experiential design and strong customer loyalty are winning; casual drinks-to-go and moderation-focused venues are losing.

Boutique hotels show the same pattern in sharper relief. The luxury boutique segment held 53.5% of market revenue in 2025 with occupancy holding at 70-75% and RevPAR up 5.3%. But the independents who invented the category are vanishing. Sonder, the tech-enabled boutique hotel pioneer, filed for bankruptcy in November 2025 after Marriott terminated its deal. Revo Hospitality Group (260 hotels) filed Chapter 11 in early 2026. Fairmont collapsed under its own maturity wall. What changed is ownership. Marriott, Hilton, and other global chains now own the independent-hotel fantasy. Hilton launched its Outset Collection with 60+ properties in development targeting independent owners. Airbnb, once a disruptive threat, now partners directly with boutique hotels in New York, Los Angeles, Madrid, and San Francisco. This force made authentic, curated hospitality valuable—and made it profitable only at scale or under brand control. Labor costs per occupied room jumped 21% from Q4 2024 to Q4 2025 (to $54.98), while operating costs surged: insurance up 100%, utilities up 30%. Margins compressed from 18.4% in 2016 to 15.1% by 2026.

Movie theaters reveal the harshest truth about experience spending: it is radically bifurcated. Attendance fell 34% from 2019 to 2025 (1.225 billion tickets down to 780 million), but event films—Inside Out 2, Deadpool & Wolverine—still filled seats. The industry bounced to $8.6-8.8 billion in 2025, but only because studios and chains made hard structural moves. AMC shuttered 200+ theaters. iPic, LOOK, and CMX filed bankruptcy. Cinemark and Regal, the survivors, negotiated longer theatrical windows (45 days or more by 2026) with studios to capture both box office and downstream streaming value. Ticket prices rose 5.7% in 2025 to offset attendance loss. What this force made valuable is authenticity and occasion—seeing a film with friends, in a theater, for a movie event worth the trip. What it made cheap is everyday, casual moviegoing. A Wednesday matinee in suburbia is now a loss leader. Premium formats (IMAX, Dolby, 3D), blockbuster events, and consolidated theater chains are what survived the 2019-2026 reckoning.

The evidence

  • Amusement Parks: $33.4B market in 2026 with 9.7% CAGR, 750 establishments, and Disney reporting 76.5M domestic visitors in 2024 despite peak tickets reaching $224
  • Bars & Nightclubs: $38.6B revenue in 2026 (1.5% annualized growth through 2030) with margins falling to 5.4% from 6.8% in 2022, while labor costs per typical venue run $70,416/month and spirits tariffs added 15-20% by mid-2026
  • Boutique Hotels: $76.3B market in 2026 with luxury segment holding 53.5% of revenue, but independent owners collapsing (Sonder bankruptcy Nov 2025, Revo Chapter 11 early 2026) as labor costs jumped 21% YoY and operating costs exploded (insurance +100%, utilities +30%)
  • Movie Theaters: $8.6-8.8B in 2025 on 780M admissions, down 34% from 2019, with consolidation (AMC closed 200+ theaters, iPic/LOOK/CMX bankrupt) and studios locking in 45-day theatrical windows to compete with 1.1B SVOD subscribers
  • Experience Bifurcation: Gen Z drinking down 65% intent reduction yet consumption up to 73%; themed bars +32.9% in 2025; alcohol-free cocktails +36% YoY; selective premiumization across all venue types

Who rides it

Theme park operators, luxury hotel chains, and consolidated theater companies win decisively. Disney and Universal control the destination experience narrative and command pricing power (Disney's $224 tickets despite price sensitivity). Marriott and Hilton absorb independent boutique hotels through branded collections (Hilton's Outset Collection, 60+ in pipeline). AMC and Cinemark, the surviving theater chains, use consolidation to negotiate directly with studios, lock in longer theatrical windows, and charge premiums for immersive formats. For bars, only upscale venues with strong brand identity and experiential design (themed bars, craft cocktails, alcohol-free innovation) are thriving. The common thread: scale, brand control, and the ability to create occasion and memory at premium pricing. Chains beat independents every time.

Who's squeezed

Independent hotel owners and small boutique operators face extinction. Sonder's bankruptcy in November 2025 and Revo's Chapter 11 in early 2026 signal the end of the independent era. Casual movie theaters and suburban multiplex chains are closed or closing (AMC alone shuttered 200+, and iPic, LOOK, and CMX filed bankruptcy). Economy hotel segments are declining as luxury boutique dominates. Local and independent bars face margin collapse (down to 5.4% from 6.8%) as chains consolidate and tariffs and labor costs rise. Regional amusement parks (Cedar Fair-Six Flags merger brought $5.5B debt and down 1.4M visitors in Q2 2025) struggle under high operating costs and competition from destination parks. Small movie theater operators with no scale to negotiate studio windows or invest in premium formats are simply gone. The losers share one trait: lack of scale or brand power to negotiate costs, set prices, or create occasion at premium points.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The experience economy rests on willingness to pay, and that willingness has limits. Disney's simultaneous price increases to $224 and deeper discounts in October 2025 reveal the danger: consumers are price-sensitive about family vacations even as they spend on experiences. Gen Z's stated intent to reduce drinking (65%) contradicts actual consumption, suggesting delayed adjustment or an uncomfortable divergence between aspiration and behavior. The Cedar Fair-Six Flags merger produced a $5.5 billion debt load with declining attendance (down 1.4M in Q2 2025). Recession, inflation of experiences themselves (ticket prices up, cocktails up, hotel rooms up), or a drop in discretionary income could collapse the experience economy faster than it rose. The real risk: experience spending survives only if it remains a luxury for the affluent and selective premiumization for the middle class. If access becomes genuinely expensive (theater tickets, hotel rooms, park admissions), the volume game breaks and margins evaporate. Scale plays work today because volume is still there. Scale breaks when volume stops.

In one sentence

The experience economy in a nutshell

Scale and brand control are eating the experience economy, leaving room only for big operators who can charge premium prices while independents vanish into bankruptcy.
