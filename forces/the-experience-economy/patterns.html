The synthesis · what repeats across all 6

The patterns

People spend less on owning things and more on doing things — the money moves from the shelf to the ticket, the table, and the night out.

The experience economy made one thing newly precious: the moment itself. When people chose to spend $33.4 billion at amusement parks in 2026 (up 9.7% CAGR since 2022), they were voting for spectacle over spare bedrooms. Disney's parks pulled in 76.5 million domestic visitors in 2024, and Universal's Epic Universe alone is projected to hit 9.2 million visits by 2026. The sheer attendance numbers tell the real story: people are willing to pay premiums—peak Disney tickets hit $224 at Disneyland in October 2025—for the promise of escape, memory, status. Amusement parks grew from $14.3 billion in 2022 to $33.4 billion in 2026, and the number of establishments jumped 7.0% annually. What this force made valuable was scale, innovation, and the ability to charge repeatedly for the same customer across a season or year.

The through-line

Scale and brand control are eating the experience economy, leaving room only for big operators who can charge premium prices while independents vanish into bankruptcy.

Who wins across the board

Theme park operators, luxury hotel chains, and consolidated theater companies win decisively. Disney and Universal control the destination experience narrative and command pricing power (Disney's $224 tickets despite price sensitivity). Marriott and Hilton absorb independent boutique hotels through branded collections (Hilton's Outset Collection, 60+ in pipeline). AMC and Cinemark, the surviving theater chains, use consolidation to negotiate directly with studios, lock in longer theatrical windows, and charge premiums for immersive formats. For bars, only upscale venues with strong brand identity and experiential design (themed bars, craft cocktails, alcohol-free innovation) are thriving. The common thread: scale, brand control, and the ability to create occasion and memory at premium pricing. Chains beat independents every time.

Who's squeezed across the board

Independent hotel owners and small boutique operators face extinction. Sonder's bankruptcy in November 2025 and Revo's Chapter 11 in early 2026 signal the end of the independent era. Casual movie theaters and suburban multiplex chains are closed or closing (AMC alone shuttered 200+, and iPic, LOOK, and CMX filed bankruptcy). Economy hotel segments are declining as luxury boutique dominates. Local and independent bars face margin collapse (down to 5.4% from 6.8%) as chains consolidate and tariffs and labor costs rise. Regional amusement parks (Cedar Fair-Six Flags merger brought $5.5B debt and down 1.4M visitors in Q2 2025) struggle under high operating costs and competition from destination parks. Small movie theater operators with no scale to negotiate studio windows or invest in premium formats are simply gone. The losers share one trait: lack of scale or brand power to negotiate costs, set prices, or create occasion at premium points.

The biggest tension

The one to watch

The experience economy rests on willingness to pay, and that willingness has limits. Disney's simultaneous price increases to $224 and deeper discounts in October 2025 reveal the danger: consumers are price-sensitive about family vacations even as they spend on experiences. Gen Z's stated intent to reduce drinking (65%) contradicts actual consumption, suggesting delayed adjustment or an uncomfortable divergence between aspiration and behavior. The Cedar Fair-Six Flags merger produced a $5.5 billion debt load with declining attendance (down 1.4M in Q2 2025). Recession, inflation of experiences themselves (ticket prices up, cocktails up, hotel rooms up), or a drop in discretionary income could collapse the experience economy faster than it rose. The real risk: experience spending survives only if it remains a luxury for the affluent and selective premiumization for the middle class. If access becomes genuinely expensive (theater tickets, hotel rooms, park admissions), the volume game breaks and margins evaporate. Scale plays work today because volume is still there. Scale breaks when volume stops.
