A · Going out again

The Night Out

Social venues sell the experience even as drinking itself declines.

The bar and nightclub business is bigger than ever—$38.6 billion in 2026—but it's not about the drink anymore. Gen Z is ordering less alcohol, older drinkers are aging out, and younger people are cutting back even as they still go out. The money is moving to the venue itself: the design, the staff, the vibe, the event. Bars that just serve drinks are running out of customers. Bars that offer an experience are where the growth is.

What changed

The bars and nightclubs industry is splitting into two completely different businesses. On one side, you have upscale experiential venues—craft cocktail lounges, themed bars, wine bars with staff guidance and events—that are growing and pushing margins back up. On the other side, the traditional neighborhood bar selling cheap drinks is shrinking. The industry was $26.7 billion in 2022 and grew to $38.6 billion by 2026, but that growth is entirely concentrated in the premium experience tier. Themed bar venues surged 32.9% in 2025 alone. Experiential dining venues—restaurants offering pop-ups, chef experiences, immersive design—jumped 46% year-over-year, with restaurants adding 34% more experiences. Wine bars grew 6.8% annually when led by private-equity operators investing in the experience side, but traditional independent wine bars face collapsing margins.

The real shift is in who's drinking. U.S. adult drinking rates hit 54% in August 2025, the lowest in 90 years. Gen Z drinks 30% less than Millennials did at the same age. Monthly wine occasions among Gen Z have collapsed 34% since 2019. At the same time, GLP-1 medications (now used by 10–15% of U.S. adults) reduce on-premise alcohol spending by 30% and home spending by 22%. Even when younger drinkers do go out, they're ordering fewer drinks—3.9 per occasion in 2025 versus 4.4 in 2024—but they spend more per drink on premium options. This is called "selective premiumization": fewer drinks, better quality, higher price. The younger audience still shows up; they're just not there to drink. They're there for the night out.

Costs are squeezing the middle. Labor is brutal—a typical nightclub spends $70,416 per month on staff wages, and bartender turnover runs 50–70% annually, driving constant rehiring costs. Imported spirits tariffs pushed premium bottle prices up 15–20% by mid-2026. Domestic spirits face their own inflation from grain costs and aged-barrel constraints. Venues have to pass some of this to customers, but pricing power only works at the top. The low-end venue—the dive bar, the cheap nightclub—can't raise prices without losing what's left of its younger customer base. These venues are stuck between rising costs and a customer base that's drinking less and has less disposable income.

Private equity is consolidating chains. Sun Holdings bought Bar Louie in 2025, signaling that PE sees scale and the ability to standardize the "experience" as the playbook. The fragmented industry—70,000 venues with no chain exceeding 5% market share—is vulnerable to roll-up. Operators who can build a consistent themed environment, hire and retain trained staff, manage premium pricing, and create social media-worthy moments are winning. Operators who rely on volume and cheap drinks are losing.

The evidence

  • Bars & Nightclubs (2026): $38.6B market size, 1.5% annual growth 2025–2030, but only 5.4% margins (down from 6.8% in 2022) and 50–70% bartender turnover costing $70k/month per venue
  • Wine Bars (2026): $3.4B market, -0.7% growth, but PE-led experiential operators growing 6.8% CAGR with 22% higher retention than traditional independents
  • Themed Venues (2025): Bar venues with themes surged 32.9%; experiential dining (pop-ups, chef experiences) up 46% YoY with restaurants adding 34% more experiences
  • Generational Collapse (August 2025): U.S. adult drinking rate 54% (lowest in 90 years); Gen Z drinks 30% less than Millennials at same age; monthly wine occasions down 34% since 2019
  • GLP-1 Impact (2025): 10–15% of U.S. adults on GLP-1s; studies show 30% reduction in on-premise alcohol spending, 22% home reduction; Endocrine Society flags alcohol-use-disorder treatment potential

Who rides it

Private-equity-led upscale venues, craft cocktail bars, and experiential nightclubs—places where the venue itself is the draw. Operators who invest in immersive design, trained staff, themed events, and social media moments are seeing 6.8% growth (in wine bars) and 32.9% surge in themed venues. Older, affluent drinkers (55+) who can afford premium prices and don't face medical restrictions are still spending. Non-alcoholic premium experiences—mocktails, soft clubbing in daytime spaces—grew 36% and tap into the younger customer without the health and cost friction.

Who's squeezed

Traditional independent bars and casual chains selling cheap drinks. Margins fell from 6.8% to 5.4% industry-wide, but are worse for venues betting on volume. Younger, price-sensitive drinkers—already cutting back due to health and wallet tightness—can't afford 15–20% price hikes from tariffs on premium spirits. Gen Z wine occasions collapsed 34% since 2019. Bartenders and entry-level staff face wage inflation ($70k monthly lab for a typical venue) and constant turnover, making the low-margin neighborhood bar uneconomical. Venues relying on imported premium spirits face tariff and supply-chain volatility.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The structural demand wall is real and accelerating. U.S. adult drinking rates hit a 90-year low in August 2025. Gen Z drinks 30% less than the generation before at the same age, and GLP-1 adoption (now 10–15% of adults) is reducing on-premise spending by 30%. Even when younger people go out, they're not ordering drinks—they're ordering the experience. If the experience becomes too expensive, or if younger drinkers continue to age out of drinking habits, the entire on-premise alcohol industry faces a demand floor that's hard to engineer around. The 2025 Dietary Guidelines removed explicit daily drink limits, but the U.S. Surgeon General simultaneously issued an advisory linking alcohol to nearly 100,000 annual cancer cases. This mixed messaging will keep younger people cautious. The consolidation into upscale venues could leave entire regions underserved—if the only profitable bar is the $20-cocktail place, what happens to social venues for ordinary people?

In one sentence

The night out in a nutshell

People are going out, but they're not going out to drink—they're going out for the experience, which means venues that sell atmosphere and events are thriving while traditional bars betting on volume are squeezed.
