C · The premium night

The big screen fights back

Theaters and live events survive by being an experience streaming can't copy—and they're pricing for it.

Streaming killed casual moviegoing and made concerts too expensive for most people. But theater and event companies learned to survive anyway—not by serving everyone, but by serving the moments that matter most. They've turned the experience into a premium product, and consumers who care enough are paying for it.

What changed

Movie theaters hit bottom in 2024 but never fully climbed back. The US theater market sat at $8.6 to $8.8 billion in 2025—still down 23 to 27 percent below the 2019 pre-pandemic peak. Total ticket volume collapsed: 1.225 billion admissions in 2019 fell to 780 million in 2025, a 34 percent drop that won't reverse. What survived, though, tells the real story. Attendance split into two modes: blockbuster events like Inside Out 2 and Deadpool & Wolverine pack theaters. Everything else fails. Generic midbudget films die at the box office. This bifurcation forced a structural change. Studios learned that a 26-to-45-day theatrical window maximizes both box office revenue and downstream streaming dollars—something that didn't exist five years ago. CinemaCon 2026 saw every major studio commit to at least 45-day theatrical exclusivity. Universal extended to 7 weekends by 2027. Theater became something streaming needed, not something streaming killed.

The chains that survived rebuilt themselves around margins, not volume. AMC emerged from years of near-collapse with Q2-2026 EBITDA margins hitting 20 percent—triple the industry average of 7.3 percent. Cinemark reported 18.6 percent margins in 2025. Both did this by closing hundreds of unprofitable locations, consolidating their footprint, and raising ticket prices 5.7 percent in 2025. The weak players—iPic, LOOK, CMX—filed bankruptcy between 2024 and 2026. Theater count has dropped, but per-theater economics improved sharply. The remaining 2,200 enterprises are fighting a different war: not making movies accessible, but making certain screenings unmissable.

Concerts swung the opposite direction: booming, but hollowed out. The concert and event promotion industry hit $60.2 billion in 2026, up 7.4 percent from 2024, on record attendance and soaring ticket prices. Live Nation Entertainment alone posted record revenue of $25.2 billion in 2025, a 9 percent year-over-year jump, with 159 million fans attending 55,000 concerts globally. But money concentrated sharply. Concert ticket prices soared to an average of $144 in 2025, up 45 percent since 2019. Dynamic pricing algorithms (Ticketmaster's, primarily) pushed consumers toward extreme spending: 36 percent were willing to pay $100 to $499 per ticket, and 17 percent willing to spend up to $1,000. Yet venues below the superstar tier collapsed. Rockwood Music Hall, an iconic NYC independent venue, closed in November 2024. Avant Gardner in Brooklyn filed for bankruptcy owing $155 million in debt. The Music Hall of Williamsburg faced eviction at the end of 2026. UK grassroots venues closed at one every two weeks. The revenue growth masked a winner-take-most market where Live Nation and Ticketmaster controlled 86 percent of primary ticketing at major venues, used that control to bundle promotions with amphitheater access, and systematized overcharges of $1.72 per ticket. In April 2026, a federal jury found them liable on all antitrust counts. Damages could exceed $450 million if trebled. Yet the industry kept growing—because the top 1 percent of events still pulled in massive spending, and the middle tier simply died.

The evidence

  • Movie theaters: US chain AMC reported Q2-2026 EBITDA margin of 20% on $1.6B revenue (+14.2% YoY), after shuttering 200+ unprofitable locations during 2024-2025 consolidation.
  • Concerts: Average US concert ticket prices hit $144 in 2025 (up 45% from 2019), with Live Nation reporting record $25.2B revenue on 159M fans at 55,000 concerts globally in 2025.
  • Streaming shift: Studios committed to 45-day minimum theatrical windows at CinemaCon 2026 after research showed 26-45 day windows maximize both box office and downstream SVOD revenue.
  • Consolidation: US theater chains fell to ~2,200 enterprises with 193,000 employees; iPic, LOOK, CMX filed Chapter 11 (2024-2026); Cinemark survived with 18.6% EBITDA margin (2025).
  • Venue collapse: Independent concert venues—Rockwood Music Hall (NYC), Avant Gardner (Brooklyn), The Music Hall of Williamsburg (Brooklyn)—filed bankruptcy or faced eviction in 2024-2026, while Ticketmaster maintained 86% market share at major venues (April 2026 antitrust verdict).

Who rides it

Theater chains that cut locations and raised margins win—AMC and Cinemark now operate slimmer, higher-margin businesses focused on blockbuster events and premium formats. Concert promoters riding the top tier of a winner-take-most market win big: Live Nation hit record revenue while the entire grassroots venue sector collapsed. Studios win by getting theatrical windows back as a window into streaming—the window is now a feature, not a threat.

Who's squeezed

Casual moviegoers and mid-tier concert fans lose. Theater attendance dropped 34 percent overall, and those with modest budgets face ticket prices that have climbed to $144 for concerts and premium formats for films. Independent venues—the 1,000s of small music halls, art houses, and specialty theaters—are dying faster than they're being born. Smaller concert promoters and independent artists lose direct access to fans as Live Nation's bundle of promotions and venue access squeezes them out. The 64 percent of concert-goers who can't justify $100+ tickets priced themselves out of the market.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that the premium experience economy bifurcates completely—blockbuster events and superstar concerts thrive while everything else withers. If ticket prices keep rising 45 percent per decade and consolidation keeps squeezing independent venues, live experiences stop being a mass market and become a luxury good for the top 20 percent of income earners. Meanwhile, streaming gets stronger, SVOD subscriber bases locked in at 2.2 billion globally (2x pre-pandemic), and the regulatory heat on Live Nation and Ticketmaster could fracture the business before the true middle market has a chance to rebuild.

In one sentence

The big screen fights back in a nutshell

The big screen and live events survive by abandoning the middle market—they now serve blockbusters and superstars at premium prices, while independent venues and casual fans disappear.
