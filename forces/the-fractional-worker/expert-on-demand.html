A · Renting the expert

The expert, on demand

Companies now rent senior judgment by the call or the project instead of hiring it full-time.

You used to hire a partner-level strategist for three years. Now you call them once, pay $2,000–$5,000 per hour, get what you need, and hang up. The expert networks business—which connects companies to on-demand senior talent for specific projects or judgments—is scaling on this shift. And it is being rebuilt from the inside by AI right now.

What changed

For decades, expert networks made their money one call at a time. A hedge fund needed to understand a biotech CEO's strategy; they called an expert network, got matched with a retired pharma exec, paid $2,000–$5,000 for the call, and got an hour of judgment. By 2025, the market for this—expert networks alone—sat at $1.8 billion in the US, growing about 4.3% that year, with an estimated 5,000–6,000 people working across roughly 65 independent companies. Five major players controlled 60% of it. The margins held at about 14%, down slightly from 15.6% in 2016 because labor and compliance costs had climbed. It was a steady, high-margin, boutique business.

Then AI broke the model. In June 2024, AlphaSense—the largest expert networks company by volume—bought Tegus, merging the two biggest libraries of past expert call transcripts under one roof. Suddenly, instead of scheduling a new expert call at $2,000–$5,000, a client could ask: "Give me the insights from every pharmaceutical CEO call we've ever taken." Within hours, an AI system could synthesize thousands of hours of conversation into a research memo. The marginal cost dropped from $2,000 to near zero. Research projects that used to take 2–5 days now take 2 hours. AlphaSense's CEO said openly that the future is not scheduling calls but building AI systems that learn from past conversations at scale. By April 2026, just two years later, AlphaSense had raised $650 million in new funding at a $4 billion valuation—almost entirely on the strength of this AI-powered consolidation story. Investors were betting not on expert networks as a labor business but on expert networks as a data monopoly.

The ripple is reshaping both ends of the market. First: the expert side. Sixty-one percent of expert networks now use AI-driven expert matching, meaning an algorithm picks the right person faster than a human coordinator did in 2020. Fifty-six percent can schedule a consultation within 24 hours. And by 2026, agentic AI systems—software agents that work autonomously—began automating entire end-to-end research workflows, cutting sourcing time from 2–5 days down to 2 hours. You no longer hire an expert for deep judgment; you rent the platform's ability to synthesize what it already knows. Second: the consulting side felt the pressure acutely. The US management consulting market was $370 billion in 2026, growing 4–5% a year, but hollowing from within. By 2025, for the first time in history, AI roles outnumbered entry-level consultant roles at top firms—a complete inversion from 2015, when there were nearly four junior consultants for every AI role. Hiring across consulting was down 20% from 2023 peaks. Consultant-specific roles were down 40%. Meanwhile, federal agencies cut roughly $5 billion in consulting contracts in early 2025, forcing immediate layoffs at Deloitte, Accenture, and IBM. Clients, exhausted by hourly billing, began demanding measurable ROI and pushing toward outcome-based pricing, subscriptions, and contingency fees. Traditional junior work—the on-ramp to the profession—was evaporating.

The consolidation accelerated. AlphaSense bought Tegus in 2024. Slingshot Insights bought Noble Insights in June 2025. Bridger bought Deepbench in February 2024. Levine Leichtman Capital Partners bought The Expert Institute's legal library in September 2024. The strategy was the same each time: control the transcript library, automate the matching, and undercut the pay-per-call model with AI synthesis. Compensation in consulting inverted: AI specialists commanded 56% wage premiums over traditional software roles by 2025, while junior consultant salaries stalled. The industry stratified into winners and losers fast. Big Four firms controlled the federal access and the scale to absorb the AI transition. Boutique specialists won on depth and agility, offering outcomes instead of hours. Mid-market consulting firms got squeezed between the two.

The evidence

  • Expert networks: $1.8B US market in 2025, but AI-driven consolidation (AlphaSense/Tegus, June 2024) reshaping the model from pay-per-call to transcript-library monopoly.
  • Management consulting: $370B US market in 2026 (4–5% CAGR), but AI roles now outnumber entry-level consultants at top firms for the first time—a reversal from 2015.
  • Federal spending collapse: Pentagon and US agencies cut $5B in consulting contracts in early 2025, forcing layoffs at Deloitte, Accenture, IBM.
  • Expert matching: 61% of expert networks use AI-driven matching by 2025; agentic workflows automate sourcing in 2–5 days down to 2 hours by 2026.
  • Consulting stratification: Big Four control scale; boutique firms win on agility; mid-market squeezed. AI specialists earn 56% premiums; junior consultant salaries stalled (2025).

Who rides it

Elite consulting firms and expert network platforms that own large call-transcript libraries win big. AlphaSense's $4 billion valuation (April 2026) signals that data monopolies on expert knowledge are worth more than labor. Boutique specialists who offer bespoke outcomes (not hours) and AI experts who can synthesize existing knowledge into actionable patterns capture wage premiums and job security. Senior strategists with rare judgment also benefit—they get paid well per call and no longer have to work full-time. Platforms that consolidate transcript libraries and automate matching reduce overhead and pricing pressure simultaneously.

Who's squeezed

Junior consultants and mid-market consulting firms absorb the worst of it. Entry-level consulting roles have been flat or declining as AI takes over routine analysis work. Federal austerity cut $5 billion in contracts in 2025 alone, forcing sudden layoffs. Mid-market firms lack the federal access of Big Four and the specialized depth of boutiques, so they are squeezed on both margins. Traditional expert networks that rely on one-off calls at $2,000–$5,000 each are being undercut by platforms that offer past research synthesized by AI at fraction of the price. Individual experts who are not rare or senior also lose—they compete with transcript libraries and AI agents that can search decades of prior calls for answers.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that AI synthesis of past expert calls creates a false sense of knowledge while hiding how out of date the underlying data is. If a platform like AlphaSense synthesizes insights from 10,000 pharma CEO calls recorded over the past five years, the answer sounds authoritative but may miss recent strategy shifts, new competitors, or regulatory changes that happened in the past quarter. Companies paying for AI-synthesized expert research instead of live calls could miss market inflection points. A second risk is consolidation around a few platforms means a few companies now control who gets asked for expertise and how expertise is monetized, creating new rent-seeking choke points.

In one sentence

The expert, on demand in a nutshell

The expert networks industry, which rents out senior expertise by the hour, is consolidating fast as AI makes past call transcripts instantly searchable and actionable.
