C · The tensions

The benefits gap

Fractional workers don't get health insurance or disability coverage — and the carriers are repricing the risk for those left behind.

When someone works as a fractional employee, contractor, or gig worker, they stop being covered by their employer's health plan or disability insurance. The employer stops paying for those benefits. The carrier stops seeing them as part of a stable group risk pool. The worker has to buy everything themselves — on an individual market where the prices are climbing fast and the subsidies are running out.

What changed

For decades, health insurance and disability coverage were locked together with a full-time job. The employer bought group plans from an insurer, and everyone in the group got the same premium. The risk was pooled — the healthy people subsidized the sick ones, and the young subsidized the old. Fractional work unraveled this. When a worker bounces between gigs, consulting clients, or fractional roles, there is no single employer to buy a group plan. The worker has to find their own coverage, alone, on the individual market. This happened gradually, but 2025-2026 is the moment the numbers broke. The US health insurance market was already $1.6 trillion in 2026 — and insurers just regained pricing power by hiking premiums 9% in 2025, the highest in 15 years, while ACA marketplace premiums jumped 18% for 2026 and benchmark plans soared 26%. Even worse: enhanced ACA subsidies expired on December 31, 2025. That means an individual who was paying $888 a year in 2025 will pay $1,904 in 2026 — more than double, overnight. These people had been surviving on subsidies. Now they face a cliff. Meanwhile, disability insurance — the income protection that catches you when you can't work — has barely touched the gig economy. Only 5% of the 70+ million gig workers in America have short-term disability access. The disability market is $20.2 billion as of 2025, and carriers like Prudential just launched gig-focused products, but they don't cover the fractional workers yet. The real dynamic is that fractional work makes a certain kind of worker — the one bouncing between clients — invisible to group plans and too risky for carriers to price fairly. Insurers are responding by withdrawing from individual markets entirely or repricing aggressively. MetLife and Unum deployed AI claims-automation platforms in 2024-2025 that cut processing time 25-30% and reject more cases using algorithmic underwriting. California raised its disability insurance rate from 1.2% to 1.3% in 2026, while New York cut it 28%, signaling that state regulators are treating gig and fractional workers as an occupational risk that varies wildly by region. The Medicaid enrollment shrinkage in 2025 (down 7.6%) is also significant — those people were getting coverage through government programs, but new work requirements in the 2025 reconciliation bill are pushing them off the rolls. They're joining the individual market just as it becomes less affordable.

The evidence

  • Healthcare: ACA marketplace premiums jumped 18% for 2026, with benchmark plans rising 26%, while enhanced subsidies expired December 31, 2025, causing annual premiums to double from $888 to $1,904 for subsidized individuals
  • Healthcare: Employer-sponsored premiums rose 9% in 2025, the highest in 15 years, as insurers like Anthem, UnitedHealth, and Humana regain pricing power after 2023-2024 margin erosion
  • Disability Insurance: Only 5% of 70+ million US gig workers have short-term disability access as of 2025, with Prudential launching its first gig-focused product only recently
  • Medicaid: Enrollment dropped 7.6% in 2025, the first decline since COVID, driven by work and reporting requirements in the 2025 reconciliation bill
  • State Insurance: California raised disability insurance rates from 1.2% to 1.3% in January 2026; New York cut rates 28% (from $24.75 to $17.68 per person annually), showing divergent state-level pricing of occupational risk

Who rides it

The big health insurers — Anthem, UnitedHealth, Humana, Aetna — win. They control the group market, which is stable and profitable, and they're raising premiums 9% in 2025. They're also benefiting from the expiration of ACA subsidies, which pushes more people into the individual market where prices are higher and margins fatter. Tech platforms like Amazon are also winning by carving out specific profitable segments — Amazon launched its GLP-1 management program in April 2026 with prices as low as $25 a month, undercutting traditional insurers on the most profitable drug categories. Disability carriers like Metlife and Prudential win by deploying AI claims automation; they process claims 25-30% faster and reject more cases using algorithmic logic. They're also starting to price gig-worker disability products at premiums that reflect the actual risk.

Who's squeezed

Fractional workers and gig workers lose. They don't have an employer buying them into a group plan, so they face individual market premiums that just jumped 18-26% in a single year. They also lose subsidies — the enhanced ACA tax credits that were propping up affordability expired December 31, 2025, so someone who was paying $888 a year is now paying over $2,000. They have almost no disability insurance; only 5% of gig workers have coverage. If they were on Medicaid, they're getting pushed off by new work requirements. They also lose to the new entrants like Amazon, which sounds good until you realize Amazon is only targeting the most profitable segments — GLP-1 drugs and primary care — which leaves the complex, expensive patients to traditional insurers, which then raise premiums further on the fractional worker who remains.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that the individual insurance market collapses under its own unsustainability. Premiums have now more than doubled for subsidized ACA enrollees, and enrollment is shrinking. Medicaid is shrinking due to new work requirements. Employer-sponsored plans are still rising. The only people left in the individual market are the ones who can't afford group coverage — the fractional workers, the self-employed, the gig workers. As that pool gets worse and worse, insurers will either withdraw entirely or price so high that nobody can afford it. That's already starting to happen in some states, and when it does, the fractional worker has nowhere to go. They can't get on an employer plan because they don't have a full-time job. They can't afford the individual market. They're not eligible for Medicaid anymore. That's when the risk becomes systemic — emergency rooms fill up with uninsured people, preventive care collapses, and the healthcare system has to absorb the cost.

In one sentence

The benefits gap in a nutshell

Fractional work stripped away employer health insurance and disability coverage, and now the insurers are charging the worker for the risk — while platforms like Amazon skim the most profitable segments off the top.
