A · Renting the expert

Advice as a rented service

Whole functions like HR, marketing, and compliance now rent from expert consultants instead of building permanent teams—and the cut keeps shrinking.

Companies stopped hiring permanent staff for specialist functions. Now they rent. HR departments rent compliance advice from consultants. Marketing teams rent campaign strategy from agencies. Environmental risk gets outsourced to specialized firms. The effect is predictable: consultants multiply, but each one makes less.

What changed

The unbundling of permanent work into rented services made three things scarce and changed how advice works. First, in-house expertise became a luxury only the largest firms could keep. By 2025, HR consulting was a $39.4 billion market (up from $27.3 billion in 2022)—growth that came almost entirely from companies externalizing functions they used to staff. Instead of hiring a director of compliance, a firm rents compliance help by the hour. Instead of building a marketing team, they rent strategic guidance from an agency. Marketing consulting alone is now an $88.4 billion market (up from $63.4 billion in 2022), with 323,000 agencies competing for work. Environmental consulting sits at $27.4 billion (up from $23.6 billion in 2022), driven by EPA rules and corporate climate targets that are too dense for most companies to navigate alone.

But renting advice made the consultants' margins cheaper. HR consulting margins used to run 25-40% for mid-market work; by 2026, they'd dropped to 15-40% because competition flattened prices and three simultaneous headwinds hit consultants. First, software bundles like TriNet, Rippling, and Paychex now combine payroll, compliance, and employee analytics in a single platform—eroding the old advisory moat. Second, AI spending jumped 50% year-over-year in 2025 while overall HR budgets shrank, forcing consultants to pivot from selling system selection (easier, higher margin) to change management and upskilling (harder, lower margin). Third, regulatory density exploded: 48 states changed wage or leave laws in 2025, and pay transparency rules are now law in 30+ jurisdictions. This kept demand high but turned compliance advice into commodity work. Marketing consultants faced similar pressure: only 28% raised prices from 2024 to 2025 (down from higher rates in 2021–2022), while digital agencies saw net margins fall to 13% from historical highs, as clients demanded outcome-based fees instead of time-based rates. Environmental consultants got pinched too: tariff escalation in 2025 pushed steel and aluminum duties to 50%, stretching timelines and squeezing margins to 8-9% EBITDA as labor costs spiked and licensed engineers stayed scarce.

What became newly valuable was implementation and speed. When 78% of global organizations used AI by 2025 and 71% adopted generative AI, clients stopped buying advice on whether to adopt—they bought advice on how to execute it fast. McKinsey cut 3,000–4,000 jobs in 2025, concentrated in junior research roles where generative AI compressed delivery time. But the same consultancies pivoted hard to implementation-heavy work: IT consulting on AI strategy grew 89% year-over-year in 2025. Compliance consulting stayed valuable, but for a different reason: regulations multiplied and diverged. 20 US state privacy laws, GDPR, CCPA, and 30+ pay-transparency jurisdictions meant that clients needed expert translation of rules—but that work was ripe for automation and offshoring. The risk shifted. Renting advice made the firm's talent flexible (no payroll overhead), but it meant that advice quality depended on someone else's bench strength, not your own hiring and training. And the consultant's risk flipped: instead of managing long-term client relationships with stable margins, they competed in a commoditizing market with outcome-based pricing, regulatory complexity that required constant upskilling, and AI tools that automated junior work faster than they could hire.

The evidence

  • HR consulting hit $39.4 billion in 2025, up 44% from $27.3 billion in 2022—but growth slowed to 1.4% in 2026 as software platforms like Rippling and TriNet bundled away the advisory moat.
  • Marketing consulting is now $88.4 billion in 2025, with only 28% of agencies raising prices from 2024 to 2025—brutal price compression even as demand remained high because clients shifted to outcome-based fees.
  • Environmental consulting grew to $27.4 billion in 2025 but margins squeezed to 8-9% EBITDA due to tariff escalation (steel/aluminum duties to 50% in 2025) and acute engineer shortages described as 'the tightest in a generation'.
  • 48 states changed wage or leave laws in 2025 and 30+ jurisdictions enacted pay transparency rules—keeping compliance consulting demand high but turning it into commodity work competitors could undercut.
  • IT consulting on AI strategy grew 89% year-over-year in 2025, while McKinsey cut 3,000–4,000 jobs focused on junior research where generative AI compressed delivery—showing shift from advice to implementation.

Who rides it

Implementation specialists and niche expertise consultancies. Firms that built depth in one domain—AI change management, regulatory translation, climate risk modeling—won because they could charge more and automate less. McKinsey's cuts targeted junior research roles; their remaining headcount shifted to client execution and strategic transformation. Environmental consultancies that merged (WSP acquired Power Engineers for $1.78 billion in February 2025) won because they could knit together geographic reach and specialist depth. Small consultancies that stayed focused on one thing—one industry, one regulatory area, one AI risk—remained defensible. And the platforms themselves won: software bundles that reduced advisory need (Rippling, TriNet, Paychex) won because they automated the low-end advisory work and captured recurring revenue.

Who's squeezed

Generalist consultants and junior talent. The 10,000 US marketing jobs eliminated in the first seven months of 2025 due to AI automation were mostly junior roles. Agencies that sold execution rather than strategy lost margin because clients started doing basic execution in-house with AI tools. Independent consultants who competed purely on availability or time got undercut by software bundles and platforms. And the traditional training pipeline broke: two-thirds of agency owners worried about the disappearance of entry-level roles in 2025, meaning fewer junior consultants meant fewer senior consultants to train, which meant less depth for mid-market firms that couldn't afford the expensive specialists.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that advice becomes both commoditized and ungovernable at once. Regulations multiplied (48 states changed wage laws, 30+ enacted pay-transparency rules, EPA expanded PFAS standards), but the cost of keeping up with them fell on the consultant and the client jointly—and the consultant's margins can't absorb it. When only 28% of agencies could raise prices in 2025, the incentive is to cut corners or hire cheaper labor overseas. But if compliance advice gets outsourced to the lowest-cost provider, the client's risk exposure rises. Similarly, when implementation became more valuable than advice (89% AI-strategy growth), consultants faced pressure to promise faster execution with smaller teams, which means higher execution risk and lower quality. The worry is a two-tier market: expensive, brand-name consultants who capture the implementation work and can train their own people, and cheap commodity consultants competing on cost with growing risk of missed deadlines, compliance gaps, and botched executions that expose clients to liability.

In one sentence

Advice as a rented service in a nutshell

Renting expert advice from consultants instead of hiring permanent staff made expertise abundant and cheaper, but it also made margins thinner, execution riskier, and junior careers disappear.
