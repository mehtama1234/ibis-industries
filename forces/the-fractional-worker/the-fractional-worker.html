The Fractional Worker

Permanent jobs unbundle into gigs, fractional roles, and expert networks — the firm rents talent by the hour, and the platform in the middle takes the cut.

For a hundred years, the employment contract was simple: you showed up, stayed put, got paid. Now it's splintering. The firm no longer wants to buy your time; it wants to rent your task. This shift is unfolding across three distinct markets—expert networks, management consulting, and business coaching—each showing the same pattern: permanent roles are being replaced by on-demand fractional work, platforms are racing to consolidate the data and the rolodex, and the burden of stability has shifted from the employer to the worker.

What changed

The fractional-work economy is being built on three layers, each with its own physics. At the top, expert networks—a $1.8 billion US market in 2025 growing 4.3%—are shifting from scheduling one-off $2,000–$5,000 phone calls with human experts to AI-driven transcript synthesis and agentic research workflows that compress a 2–5 day project into 2 hours. This inflection hit in mid-2024 when AlphaSense acquired Tegus, merging the two largest libraries of expert call transcripts under one roof and triggering a consolidation wave: Slingshot acquired Noble Insights, Bridger acquired Deepbench, and Levine Leichtman acquired The Expert Institute's legal library. By 2026, 61% of expert networks now use AI-driven expert matching and agentic AI systems are automating end-to-end research workflows—cutting sourcing time from days to hours. Margins are compressing (down from 15.6% in 2016 to ~14% in 2025), and the industry is consolidating rapidly: five major players now control 60% of a market with only ~65 independent companies and 5,000–6,000 workers. What used to be a network of thousands of accessible experts has become a data-moat play where those who own the transcripts win.\n\nIn management consulting, a $370 billion US market (2026), the pressures are more visceral. AI has become the industry's existential event. By 2025, AI roles outnumber entry-level consultants at top firms for the first time—reversing a 2015 baseline where there were nearly four junior consultants per AI role. Hiring is down 20% from 2023 peaks; consultant-specific roles are down 40%. Simultaneously, the U.S. federal government cut roughly $5 billion in consulting contracts in early 2025, forcing layoffs at Deloitte, Accenture, and IBM. Accenture announced an $865 million restructuring in April 2025, cutting 11,000 roles while doubling AI specialist headcount to 80,000—a stark rebalancing of the labor mix. Clients have also stopped buying hours: CEOs now demand measurable ROI and are pushing firms toward outcome-based pricing and subscription models instead of hourly billing. Compensation is inverting—AI specialists command 56% wage premiums over traditional software roles, while junior consultant salaries have stalled. The Big Four control scale and access, boutique specialists win on depth and agility, and mid-market firms are squeezed in between.\n\nBusiness coaching—a $20 billion US market (2026) growing 2.9%—is following a parallel arc, but in reverse. Instead of shrinking, the market is exploding in supply. Coaches numbered 71,000 in 2019 and jumped to 145,500 by 2024, mostly solo practitioners and small firms (88%+ are nonemployers). AI coaching segments grew at 22–28% annually in 2024–2025, far outpacing human coaching's 3–5% baseline. Yet here, workers are proliferating, not consolidating—entry barriers have collapsed because there is no US licensing requirement; anyone can call themselves a coach. The catch: 91% of coachees perceived AI coaching as effective as in-person delivery, and platforms now offer \"cyborg coach\" hybrid models bundled into HR software at $100/month. ROI has also become table stakes—86% of companies now report positive ROI from coaching, and it has shifted from discretionary C-suite perk to a strategic leadership-development tool embedded in succession planning. What this means is that coaching is being commoditized and stratified at once: a vast pool of fractional coaches competing for platform minutes, while those embedded in enterprise relationships (Korn Ferry, Heidrick & Struggles) or credentialed under new ICF Minimum Skills Requirements (raised in January 2026) are widening the gap.\n\nAcross all three markets, the pattern is identical: the unit of work is shrinking from the job to the task, platforms are racing to own the data (transcripts, call libraries, assessment histories), and the burden of sourcing, vetting, and staying constantly available has migrated entirely onto the worker. Expert networks used to build relationships with experts; now they run algorithms over transcripts. Consulting firms used to grow junior consultants into senior partners; now they hire AI specialists and outsource the rest to fractional labor or boutique shops. Coaching was once a luxury for the C-suite; now it's a commoditized \"cyborg\" service bundled into HR software. The commonality is that permanence is gone. Workers are paid by the task, platforms take the cut, and the risk of income volatility, benefits, and obsolescence sits entirely on the individual.

The evidence

  • Expert Networks (Business Services): $1.8 billion US market in 2025, consolidating rapidly after AlphaSense acquired Tegus (June 2024) to merge the two largest transcript libraries; five major players now control 60% of the market with only 5,000–6,000 workers and ~65 independent firms.
  • Management Consulting (Business Services): $370 billion US market in 2026 but hollowing—consultant-specific hiring down 40% from 2023 peaks as Accenture doubled AI specialist headcount to 80,000 in April 2025 while cutting 11,000 traditional roles; federal consulting contracts cut ~$5 billion in early 2025.
  • Business Coaching (Business Services): $20 billion US market in 2026, but supply exploded from 71,000 coaches in 2019 to 145,500 by 2024; AI coaching segments grew 22–28% annually in 2024–2025, yet 91% of coachees perceived no difference from human coaching.
  • Expert Networks AI Adoption: 61% of expert networks now use AI-driven expert matching by 2026; agentic AI systems automate end-to-end research workflows, compressing sourcing from 2–5 days to 2 hours and threatening the historical pay-per-call revenue model.
  • Consulting Labor Inversion: By 2025, AI roles outnumbered entry-level consultants at top firms for the first time, reversing the 2015 baseline where there were nearly four junior consultants per AI role; AI specialists command 56% wage premiums over traditional software roles.

Who rides it

Platforms and data-rich consolidators are the clear winners. In expert networks, AlphaSense (valued at $4 billion in April 2026) and five major players controlling 60% of the market win by owning transcripts and running AI synthesis at near-zero marginal cost, undercutting pay-per-call models and independent experts. In consulting, the Big Four and AI-focused boutiques win by controlling client relationships and AI capabilities; mid-market and commodity labor lose. In coaching, platform-embedded \"cyborg coach\" models and credentialed coaches embedded in enterprise relationships (Korn Ferry, Heidrick & Struggles) win, while solo practitioners fractionalizing into platform minutes lose bargaining power. Specialists who own irreplaceable depth—senior partners with deep client relationships, credentialed coaches with ICF credentials (raised in January 2026), niche industry experts—remain valuable. But everyone else competes as interchangeable fractional labor.

Who's squeezed

Ordinary workers lose on every dimension. Entry-level consultants and junior roles are being eliminated (hiring down 40%); expert networks employment is locked into 5,000–6,000 workers across five firms instead of a distributed network; 145,500 business coaches are competing for fractional minutes on platforms at commoditized rates. Stability evaporates: there is no pathway from junior to senior anymore in consulting (because juniors are being replaced by AI); no benefits or long-term income security for coaches or fractional experts; no incentive for employers to invest in training or career development when workers can be rented by the task. Mid-market consulting firms are squeezed between the Big Four's scale and boutiques' agility. And workers in these roles now bear all the risk: income volatility, benefits gaps, the burden of staying current on tools and AI capabilities, and the constant pressure to rebrand themselves as \"specialists\" to compete with commoditization. The premise of the employment relationship—stability and investment in you—is gone.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is the creation of a two-tier labor market where a small cohort of high-credentialed, high-data-access specialists (AI-trained consultants, platform-embedded coaches, expert networks embedded in consolidators) command rents while the vast majority of fractional workers are locked into a low-wage, low-power, perpetually contingent status. Evidence: Accenture doubled AI headcount to 80,000 while cutting 11,000 traditional roles; coaches jumped 105% in five years but are competing on platforms for $100/month slots; expert networks are consolidating around five firms with data moats. The path from commodity fractional labor to high-credentialed specialist is narrow and requires continuous re-skilling, which the market has no incentive to fund. Meanwhile, platforms and consolidators hold all the data and the client relationships, so bargaining power flows entirely upward. The true risk is not job loss—it's the normalization of permanent precarity, where \"flexibility\" and \"fractional work\" become a one-way ratchet toward lower incomes and zero security.

In one sentence

The fractional worker in a nutshell

The unit of work is shrinking from the job to the task, platforms and specialists are consolidating around data moats, and stability for ordinary workers is evaporating.
