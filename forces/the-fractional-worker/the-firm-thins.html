The Firm Gets Thinner

Professional firms replace permanent staff with contractors, part-time experts, and outsourced work to survive an expense-demand mismatch.

Law and accounting firms used to run on a core of permanent staff—associates, junior accountants, paralegals—who built institutional knowledge and advanced up the ladder. That's breaking. Expenses are climbing faster than client demand, so firms are shedding permanent headcount and replacing it with a thinner core of senior people plus an outer ring of fractional workers, contract specialists, and offshored labor. The risk isn't obvious from the profit line yet. But it's coming.

What changed

Law firms celebrated record profits in 2025, but the windfall was built on rate hikes, not efficiency. Fees worked per lawyer jumped 16.8% in 2025—the firms simply charged more. Meanwhile, demand growth came in at only 1.9%, and expenses exploded 9.1%. That mismatch is the whole story. Firms are burning cash on AI infrastructure (tech budgets up 9.7% to 11% in 2025 alone) to replace junior work, yet 59% of corporate clients report seeing no clear savings from outside counsel using these tools. In-house legal teams, squeezed on budget, are moving work away from premium Am Law 100 firms to lower-cost alternatives and alternative legal service providers. The response has been consolidation—59 mergers in 2025, up 18% from 2024, with at least 16 more deals announced for 2026. Hogan Lovells and Cadwalader combined to create a $3.6 billion revenue firm. Firms are getting bigger, but the permanent payroll is not.

Accounting services face the same squeeze from a different angle. The Big Four (Deloitte, EY, KPMG, PwC) are expanding their talent pools, but the rest of the industry is hollowing. AI is automating 80%+ of individual tax returns and cutting document analysis time in half—junior accountant work is disappearing. The CPA shortage keeps getting worse: 87% of finance leaders report talent shortages, CPA exam participation dropped 30% since 2016, and open positions now sit vacant for months instead of the old 30-45 days. Mid-market accounting firms are caught. Too small to invest in AI platforms like the Big Four, too slow to compete with automation, they are losing solo-practice clients to self-service software and small-business clients to mega-platforms. Private equity is aggressively buying up smaller and mid-market firms (Blackstone, Cherry Bekaert, Aprio, Baker Tilly, Citrin Cooperman) at 4-7x EBITDA multiples, consolidating them into platforms, and squeezing out inefficiency by replacing junior staff with fractional specialists and offshore teams. KPMG launched KPMG Law in 2025 (the first Big Four law firm under Arizona regulatory reform), expanding the suite of services but also the reliance on specialist contractors for specific legal work. KPMG's tax revenue grew 7.5% in 2025, far outpacing rivals (EY ~5.5%, Deloitte ~5.5%, PwC ~1%), but the workforce growth isn't at the junior level—it's expert networks and fractional consulting roles.

In both industries, the permanent firm is not being replaced by remote work or asynchronous collaboration. It's being replaced by a hub-and-spoke model: a small permanent core of senior partners, directors, and relationship managers at the center, ringed by fractional tax specialists, contract litigators, part-time audit leads, and offshore junior labor. The US legal services industry spans 450,000+ establishments and 1.4 to 1.5 million employees as of 2025, but employment growth has slowed to 1-1.5% annually, even as the industry hits record revenue—a sign that revenue is being captured by fewer people, often through higher leverage, not broader hiring. Accounting meanwhile spans 95,421 firms and 609,491 employees, with headcount growth stalled at 0.77% annually through 2027, despite market expansion. The fracturing is invisible in aggregate numbers but clear in the firm structure: permanent payroll is the fixed cost firms are shedding first.

The evidence

  • Law firms: Fees worked per lawyer up 16.8% in 2025, but demand growth only 1.9%—a sign firms are thinning staff while raising rates (Thomson Reuters 2026 State of the US Legal Market)
  • Accounting: 87% of finance leaders report talent shortages; CPA exam participation down 30% since 2016, forcing mid-market firms to hire fractional specialists and offshore labor (KPMG, Deloitte, EY 2025-2026 reports)
  • Law: 59 mergers in 2025, up 18% from 2024; Hogan Lovells-Cadwalader deal created $3.6B firm—consolidation in place of organic growth (Fairfax Associates, June 2026)
  • Accounting: AI is automating 80%+ of individual tax returns and cutting document analysis time 50%, eliminating junior-level permanent roles (KPMG 2025 technology adoption data)
  • Professional services: US legal industry at 1.4-1.5M employees with 1-1.5% annual growth despite record revenue; accounting at 609,491 employees with 0.77% growth through 2027—permanent staff is not keeping pace with fee growth

Who rides it

Large firms and consolidators (Big Four accounting, Am Law 200 players, PE-backed platforms) are thriving. They have the capital to invest in AI, automation, and global delivery networks. KPMG's 7.5% tax revenue growth, Deloitte's expansion, and the 59 law firm mergers in 2025 show that scale and efficiency drive the winners. Private equity platforms buying mid-market accounting firms at 4-7x EBITDA are capturing the upside of automation and junior-staff replacement. Alternative legal service providers and legal process outsourcing firms are capturing deflected work from in-house teams cutting Am Law 100 spend. Fractional experts and contract specialists (tax, IP, audit, litigation) are capturing more work, though at lower rates and with less stability than permanent roles.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market and regional firms, especially in accounting, are squeezed between automation, talent shortages, and mega-platform competition. Solo practitioners and small practices are losing clients to self-service software and cheap offshore labor. Junior accountants and paralegals face narrowing permanent roles—the entry-level ladder is fracturing. Permanent employees in these firms face thinner advancement paths, lower compensation gains, and uncertainty as their firms shed headcount or merge to survive. In-house legal teams are winning on rate leverage (forcing premiums down), but senior partners at mid-size firms face margin compression and forced consolidation.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The structural risk is that professional services firms are optimizing for short-term profit by thinning permanent staff, but this hollows out institutional knowledge, training pipelines, and culture. In law and accounting, the permanent staff used to train the next generation; now that function is decoupled. Firms are converting senior-level work to fractional and contractor models, which is efficient but leaves no one accountable for long-term client relationships, regulatory risk, or ethics. If a recession hits and demand drops below the expense baseline (as it did in 2009-2011), firms will have shed so much permanent capacity that they cannot quickly rehire or rebuild. The AM Law 100 margin spread (14-78% among top firms) also suggests that premium pricing is fragile—clients see through the AI mirage and are walking to cheaper alternatives. That pressure will force deeper cuts, not just in junior staff but in senior consultant headcount.

In one sentence

The firm gets thinner in a nutshell

Professional services firms are shedding permanent staff to offset an expense-demand mismatch, replacing the traditional career ladder with fractional experts and offshore labor, and consolidating into mega-platforms that can automate and scale.
