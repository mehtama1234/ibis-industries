B · The platforms in the middle

Who places the talent

Recruiters and brokers become the bottleneck as work fragments — they control the match and take 5 to 15 percent off the top.

When work unbundles from permanent jobs into fractional gigs, someone has to do the matching. Recruiters, freight brokers, and staffing platforms have become indispensable middlemen. They take a cut of every placement—and they're tightening their grip as demand for flexible work grows and regulation bears down on fraud.

What changed

The executive search market shows what happens when matching becomes scarce and scrutiny tightens. The sector was valued at $10.2 billion in 2026, down 3.3 percent annually from 2021–2026, yet the work itself is fragmenting. CEO succession hit an 8-year high in 2025 at 12.5 percent of S&P 500 seats, but the nature of placement is shifting: fractional and interim executives have exploded to 120,000 by 2024, double the 60,000 in 2022. Firms still capture 10 to 15 percent margins on profitable placements, but they're squeezed from both sides. Clients demand shortlists in under one week, forcing speed over rigor. Smaller recruiters can't compete with large firms deploying AI-augmented workflows and global networks. Recruitment Process Outsourcing providers are eating into director and selective C-suite roles at lower margins, and in-house HR plus LinkedIn have eroded the monopoly on executive intel that recruiters once owned. The firms adapting survive: 35 percent of top performers now offer coaching alongside placements, and 30 percent bundle in advisory consulting. Recent consolidation—Advent acquiring Heidrick & Struggles for $1.3 billion in Q4 2025, Spencer Stuart buying Meyler Campbell, ZRG acquiring Aspen Leadership—signals that scale and specialization are the only defensible moats.\n\nThe freight brokerage market reveals the same pattern but at industrial scale and with regulatory teeth. The broad market was worth $169.8 billion in 2026, but the narrow freight brokerage slice was $20.64 to $22.39 billion, growing 4.48 percent annually from 2025 to 2032. The real story is in the capacity crunch and the regulatory noose. Spot freight rates jumped 43 percent year-over-year in June 2026, with dry van rates up 66 cents per mile, and tender rejection rates exceeded 15 percent—the highest since early 2022. This spike reveals how tight the matching becomes when capacity is scarce: brokers can dictate terms because they control access to the load. Simultaneously, double brokering fraud spiraled to over 8,000 complaints in 2025, costing the industry $700 million to $1 billion annually. The FMCSA cracked down by doubling surety bond minimums from $75,000 to $150,000 and mandating real-time load tracking starting January 2026. Margins compressed from 5.6 percent in 2021 to expectations of 6.3 percent by 2026, yet digital transformation—platforms like Flexport, transportation management systems, AI-driven optimization—moved from competitive advantage to table stakes. The firms with scale and technology survive; the rest are squeezed. The industry expanded to approximately 124,000 establishments by 2026 from 107,631 in 2021, and headcount grew from 395,612 to 495,000 to 500,000, yet consolidation is accelerating: RXO acquired Coyote Logistics from UPS for $1.025 billion in September 2024, becoming the third-largest freight broker, and Echo Global Logistics acquired ITS Logistics in January 2026.\n\nWhat these two industries share is the same structural shift: fractional and gig work requires constant matching, and matching requires a platform or network. The middleman becomes essential—and therefore powerful. Margins are modest (5 to 15 percent, depending on the service), but the volume scales with fragmentation. As permanent roles unbundle, the matching layer grows fatter and more centralized. Larger platforms with AI, global reach, or compliance infrastructure have moats; smaller brokers and recruiters face commoditization or acquisition. Regulation—whether FMCSA surety bonds or SEC rules on executive placement practices—creates barriers to entry that protect incumbents while squeezing smaller players. The matching economy isn't about creating value; it's about controlling scarcity and taking the cut."

The evidence

  • Executive search firms: CEO succession at 8-year high (12.5% of S&P 500 in 2025), external hires nearly doubled to 33% of successions, yet fractional/interim executives exploded to 120,000 by 2024 (2x the 60,000 in 2022).
  • Freight brokers: Spot rates surged 43% year-over-year in June 2026, tender rejection rates hit 15% (highest since Q1 2022), revealing capacity constraints and the middleman's newfound pricing power.
  • Executive search consolidation: Advent acquired Heidrick & Struggles for $1.3 billion (Q4 2025); Spencer Stuart bought Meyler Campbell; Southfield Capital took majority stake in Metric Search ($100 million), signaling PE-driven scale play.
  • Freight broker M&A: RXO acquired Coyote Logistics for $1.025 billion (Sept 2024); Echo Global acquired ITS Logistics (Jan 2026), as digital platforms (Flexport, TMS) moved from advantage to table stakes.
  • Regulatory squeeze: FMCSA doubled surety bonds ($75K→$150K) and mandated real-time load tracking (Jan 2026) to fight double brokering fraud (8,000+ complaints in 2025, $700M–$1B annual cost).

Who rides it

Large platforms with scale, AI, and compliance infrastructure win. In executive search, firms diversifying into coaching (35% of top performers) and advisory (30%) survive consolidation waves. Venture-backed and PE-backed platforms like Flexport in freight capture growth as digital becomes table stakes and regulatory burden rises. Small independent recruiters and brokers are getting crushed: they can't match the speed or technology of large networks, they can't weather regulatory overhead, and they have no defensible moat. The consolidation is brutal and accelerating.

Who's squeezed

Small and mid-size recruiters and brokers are squeezed from all angles. In executive search, internal HR plus LinkedIn eroded the monopoly, Recruitment Process Outsourcing providers undercut on price, and clients now demand impossible speed and rigor simultaneously. In freight, compliance overhead (doubled surety bonds, real-time tracking) creates barriers that small brokers can't afford. The 5,293 executive search firms and 124,000 freight brokerage establishments are fragmenting: scale wins, boutiques lose, and the work is hollowing out into commodity matching with razor margins.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The regulatory regime creates a perverse incentive: as compliance cost rises, it concentrates power in the hands of firms that can bear it—which then control the matching and extract rents. Fraud in freight brokerage spiraled to 8,000+ complaints and $700 million to $1 billion in annual losses because the fragmented market had too many bad actors and no transparency. FMCSA's response (doubled surety bonds, mandatory tracking) is necessary but will push more work through fewer, larger platforms. Similarly, executive search is consolidating upmarket into a handful of mega-firms while fractional and interim work (120,000+ by 2024) is splintering into unregulated gig networks. The result: a two-tier system where the matching layer is either massive and opaque or tiny and compliant. Workers in both tiers lose visibility and leverage—they're matched by algorithm, priced by the platform, and have no individual bargaining power.

In one sentence

Who places the talent in a nutshell

As work fragments, the platforms that match talent to opportunity become gatekeepers—they take 5 to 15 percent, control the flow, and their power grows as regulation and technology raise the cost of entry.
