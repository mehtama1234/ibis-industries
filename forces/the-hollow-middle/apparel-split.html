B · Apparel & footwear bifurcate

Apparel splits

Fast-fashion and luxury grow; mid-price apparel chains close.

The American clothing industry is splitting in two. Shoppers at the top and bottom of the market are thriving—luxury brand DTC sites and ultra-cheap platforms like Shein and Temu pull customers and margin. Everyone else is crushed. The stores selling undifferentiated mid-market sweaters and jeans to average Americans are vanishing. Tariffs doubled import costs overnight. E-commerce captured a quarter of all fashion sales. And consumer spending on apparel just collapsed by a fifth in early 2025.

What changed

The apparel market has split into two incompatible worlds, and the middle is being evacuated. The family clothing retail market—the backbone of US mall shopping—reached $226.5 billion in 2026, but growth is nearly flat at 0.4%, masked by violent bifurcation below the surface. Women's clothing stores, which hit $66.4 billion in 2026, have exactly one story: premium and specialty retailers soaking up online growth (5.5% annual employment gains in online segments 2019-2024) while brick-and-mortar employment contracts year after year. Men's dedicated clothing stores are even worse off, declining at -1.7% in 2026 and shrinking to a $17.8 billion market from $8.3 billion in 2022—a deceptive compression that masks store closures and margin squeeze.

The tariff shock of late 2025 broke what remained of the middle. On August 29, 2025, the de-minimis tariff exemption expired globally, meaning every imported garment under $800 became subject to duty. Import tariff rates on apparel surged from 14.7% in January 2025 to 35.1% by December 2025—a 138% jump in nine months. McKinsey estimated that sourcing costs jumped 35% for apparel importers. A single family's cost of living went up $136 overnight. Apparel CPI reached 135.8 by March 2026, up 5.1% year-over-year, the fastest clip in years. Consumer response was immediate and brutal: apparel spending collapsed 21.7% month-over-month between Q4 2024 and Q1 2025. Price-sensitive households—the traditional customer for mid-market chains—pulled back furthest as inflation squeezed their budgets.

E-commerce has carved out a permanent quarter of the market. Online fashion sales reached $61.8 billion in 2025 and now account for nearly half of women's clothing sales overall. The growth isn't going to traditional department stores or branded websites, though. It's going to ultra-low-cost platforms like Shein and Temu that offer $5 blouses and $8 jeans. These platforms have invested aggressively in US warehousing to dodge tariffs and shipping delays. Meanwhile, department stores like Macy's—which still moved $22.5 billion in 2025 revenue—absorb menswear and women's brands at tighter margins, offering one-stop convenience that specialty retailers can't match. Amazon and brand-direct sites offer infinite selection and price transparency that small chains can't compete with.

The mid-market chains are gone or going. Forever 21 filed Chapter 11 on March 16, 2025, and liquidated all 364 stores by May 2025. Liberated Brands (the portfolio of Volcom, Billabong, Quiksilver, Roxy, RVCA, Spyder) filed in February 2025 and liquidated 122 stores. Macy's announced 66 store closures in January 2025, part of a 150-store exit over three years. Francesca's announced the closure of 400 locations. Torrid plans to shutter 180 stores. Store closures hit 7,323 in 2024 and approximately 6,000 in the first half of 2025 alone. The only retailer investing heavily is Tailored Brands, which filed for IPO in 2026 and plans to add 500+ stores over the next decade—but its strategy is to move upmarket into premium menswear and rental subscriptions, not defend the middle. Their fiscal 2025 revenue was $2.53 billion, up only 2.1% year-over-year, but gross margin expanded 145 basis points to 48.2%, meaning they are winning by shrinking the addressable market to affluent men willing to rent suits and use AI fit technology.

The evidence

  • Women's clothing retail in the US: $66.4B in 2026, growing -0.3% annually, as luxury and specialty online pull upmarket while Shein/Temu steal value customers from brick-and-mortar chains.
  • Men's clothing retail: contracted to $17.8B in 2026 (-1.7% in 2026), down from $8.3B in 2022, squeezed by department stores, e-commerce giants, and the normalization of casual athleisure (+10% annually).
  • Tariff shock: apparel import duties surged from 14.7% (January 2025) to 35.1% (December 2025), with McKinsey estimating 35% sourcing price increases, forcing retailers to raise prices 30-38% or absorb losses.
  • Consumer pullback: apparel spending collapsed 21.7% month-over-month (Q4 2024 to Q1 2025); apparel CPI hit 5.1% year-over-year by March 2026, fastest in years.
  • E-commerce penetration: 24% of fashion retail in 2026 (up from 21% in 2024), growing 4.9% annually; ultra-low-cost platforms (Shein, Temu) capturing share from traditional mid-market chains via aggressive US warehousing.

Who rides it

Discount and luxury retailers are the only winners. Value players like TJX Companies (TJ Maxx, HomeGoods) and Ross Stores are thriving: TJX reported 5% comp sales growth in Q3 FY2026 and raised full-year guidance, while Ross projects 6-7% comp growth for FY2026, both benefiting directly from the bifurcation—affluent customers want discounts more than they need brands, and price-conscious shoppers have nowhere else to turn. At the top, luxury and specialty DTC sites are growing online at 5.5% annually in employment terms, pulling wealthy customers into premium segments and niche categories like plus-size and athletic wear where margin is real.

Who's squeezed

The mid-market chain is being executed. Forever 21 (364 stores), Liberated Brands (122 stores), Francesca's (400 closures), and Torrid (180 closures) are the visible casualties, but the real victim is anyone dependent on selling undifferentiated apparel to average-income American families. Store closures totaled 13,323 in the 18 months from 2024 through mid-2025. Employment in women's brick-and-mortar clothing retail is contracting as specialty online grows. Men's dedicated clothing stores shed 1,126 jobs from 2022 to 2026 (38,126 → 37,000 estimated), a symptom of the category's irrelevance in a hybrid-work world where casual athleisure (growing 10%+ annually) has replaced suits and slacks. Retailers holding mid-market inventory face margin compression: net margins in family clothing sit at 2.3-5%, the thinnest in retail.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The tariff regime has created a structural price floor that may not reverse. A tariff rate of 35.1% on apparel imports is not temporary—it reflects the Trump administration's reciprocal tariff doctrine and shows no signs of easing. Retailers have until late Q1 or Q2 2026 for existing inventory to work through the supply chain, meaning the full tariff hit lands on Q1-Q2 2026 shipments. For retailers already operating on 2-5% net margins, there is no room to absorb a 35% cost increase. Either prices rise 30-38% (further crushing demand among price-sensitive customers) or they exit the category. The only escape valve is nearshoring to USMCA-qualifying countries like Mexico, Vietnam, and India, but production ramp-up is slow and won't relieve pressure until H2 2026 at the earliest. The tariff creates a lasting incentive for ultra-low-cost platforms like Shein and Temu to localize warehousing in the US, permanently locking in their competitive advantage against traditional retailers.

Apparel splits in a nutshell

The US apparel market bifurcates into thriving discount and luxury poles while mid-tier chains collapse under tariffs and e-commerce pressure.
