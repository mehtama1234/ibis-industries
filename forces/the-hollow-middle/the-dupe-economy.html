Value's Revenge: The Dupe Economy

Cheap copies and private labels destroy the middle tier in beauty and supplements.

The mainstream middle in beauty and health retail is caving. Shoppers are abandoning the middle brands—the $40 serums, the mid-tier vitamins—for either cheap dupes and generics or for luxury. Meanwhile, the entire middle-market infrastructure that sold these goods is collapsing.

What changed

Beauty retail in the US hit $69.3 billion in 2026, but that headline number masks a brutal bifurcation. Ulta Beauty—the category's dominant player—posted $12.4 billion in net sales for fiscal 2025 (ended February 2026), up 9.7% year-over-year with same-store sales climbing 5.4%, because it's successfully climbing upmarket: higher average ticket, new premium store formats, and exclusive luxury brands. But the middle is hollowing out. Glossier closed 75% of its stores. Pat McGrath Labs filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in early 2026 and auctioned off its assets. Flower Beauty (the Drew Barrymore brand) shut down in September 2025. Cover FX and Mally Beauty closed via parent company restructuring in January 2026. These weren't fringe players—they were the $30–$80 premium-adjacent brands that defined the specialty beauty consumer for millennials and Gen Z. What killed them is not that premiumization ate their lunch (it did), but that value ate their breakfast. TikTok Shop—barely a player two years ago—posted $2.49 billion in US beauty GMV in 2025 alone with 123% year-over-year growth, almost entirely from cheap dupes, unknown Asian brands, and bulk discounts. Amazon is forecast to climb from 10% of beauty market share in 2024 to 15% by 2030. Sephora and Ulta's combined share is expected to dip from 20% to 19% in the same window. Gen Z discovers 70% of beauty digitally, and that discovery increasingly happens on TikTok and Instagram where a $3 lip tint that works "just like Charlotte Tilbury" spreads faster than any retailer could ever stock it. The Ulta-Target partnership ended in August 2026, eliminating in-store concessions that had been a key funnel for mainstream middle brands into Target's 1,900 stores.

Health and supplements tell the same story but with tariffs as the accelerant. The market hit $60.7 billion in 2025, growing 3.8% year-over-year—but online supplements grew 11.4% annually while brick-and-mortar stagnated. The Vitamin Shoppe, the category's biggest specialty chain, was acquired by private equity in April 2025 for just $193.5 million after its parent Franchise Group filed for bankruptcy in November 2024. GNC, the other regional titan, is still in bankruptcy proceedings as of 2026, seeking a rescue acquisition from a China-backed buyer. The physical footprint of brick-and-mortar specialty retail is shrinking 15–25% through 2026. Why? Consumers don't differentiate. A vitamin is a vitamin. They buy generic magnesium from Amazon at 40% less than what The Vitamin Shoppe charges, and the supplement works the same. Tariffs imposed in 2025 at 20–34% on imported raw materials (China 34%, Taiwan 32%, India 27%)—ingredients that account for 75–80% of US supplement imports—forced retailers to choose between raising prices to $12 for a basic B-complex or accepting margin compression that was already down to 5.5% in 2022. Meanwhile, upstream consolidation among supplement manufacturers created private-label entrants that bypass specialty retail entirely. Target launched an exclusive personalized vitamin line in 2021–2022. Online D2C brands grew in the fragmented middle while brick-and-mortar chains died. Personalized nutrition formats (gummies, subscription boxes) grew at 15% annually—exactly what specialty stores struggled to stock efficiently. The industry grew $72.9 billion overall in 2025 (+5.5% year-over-year), but that growth went to online channels, D2C brands, and private label. None of it went to the middle.

What made the middle scarce is exactly what made the dupes and generics newly valuable: brand credibility is now fully severed from retail distribution. Ten years ago, you had to walk into Sephora or Ulta to discover a new mid-tier brand. The brand's retail presence was its proof of legitimacy. Now a 19-year-old TikTok creator with 2 million followers can make a lip tint in Shenzhen, ship it directly to a US consumer, and that consumer never touches a specialty retail store. Luxury brands—Chanel, Dior, Tom Ford—can still command the retail floor and the price premium because they're aspirational. But the $50 serums and $25 eyeshadow palettes that used to justify a store visit? Replaced by a $5 Amazon dupe that does 85% of the job. For health, the commodification was even faster. Consumers learn that ascorbic acid is ascorbic acid. A 1,000 mg tablet of magnesium from Nature Made costs $6 at Costco. A specialty retailer's proprietary blend costs $14. The specialty retailer dies. The middle survives only if it differentiates—personalization, format innovation, subscription, expert curation. But that's expensive. Glossier tried curation and directness. It failed because it couldn't compete with price and convenience. The survivors will be Ulta (because it's premiumization + scale + logistics) and Amazon (because it's price + convenience + selection). The middle gets trapped between them.

The evidence

  • Beauty retail: Ulta Beauty's fiscal 2025 net sales reached $12.4 billion (+9.7% YoY), with same-store sales +5.4%, while specialty mid-tier brands (Glossier, Pat McGrath Labs, Flower Beauty) failed or filed bankruptcy (2025-2026).
  • Beauty retail: TikTok Shop captured $2.49 billion in US beauty GMV in 2025 with 123% YoY growth, nearly entirely from cheap dupes and value brands, threatening traditional specialty retail (2025).
  • Health retail: Online supplements grew 11.4% annually while brick-and-mortar stagnated; The Vitamin Shoppe acquired by private equity for $193.5 million in April 2025 after parent bankruptcy; GNC remains in bankruptcy (2025-2026).
  • Health retail: US tariffs imposed 20-34% on supplement raw materials in 2025 (China 34%, Taiwan 32%, India 27%), affecting 75-80% of imported ingredients and forcing margin compression or price hikes (2025).
  • Health retail: Supplement industry grew $72.9 billion in 2025 (+5.5% YoY), but growth concentrated entirely in online channels and D2C brands, not brick-and-mortar specialty stores (2025).

Who rides it

Ulta Beauty, Amazon, and D2C dupe brands win. Ulta wins because it's successfully premiumizing—higher average ticket, exclusive luxury brands, new store formats—while leveraging scale and logistics to compete with Amazon on convenience. Amazon wins because it has infinite selection and unbeatable price on generics and dupes. D2C dupe brands win because they can ship directly to consumers at $3–$5 and compete on social proof (TikTok reviews, creator endorsements) rather than retail shelf space. Private-label supplement makers win because they can bypass specialty retail entirely and sell direct to consumers or through mass market channels like Target and Costco.

Who's squeezed

The middle market gets squeezed from both sides. Glossier, Pat McGrath Labs, Flower Beauty, Cover FX, Mally Beauty, and smaller specialty beauty startups are shut or filed bankruptcy. The Vitamin Shoppe and GNC—the category leaders in health retail—collapsed into private equity and bankruptcy. Smaller multi-brand retailers and specialty store chains lose because they can't compete on price (Amazon) or prestige (luxury brands). Consumers in the middle income band lose too: they came to specialty retail for curation and discovery. Now they're trapped buying generics they don't understand or paying luxury prices they can't afford.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that tariffs and margin compression accelerate store closures faster than Amazon can build logistics to serve smaller cities and towns. If brick-and-mortar specialty retail dies entirely before Amazon builds out last-mile delivery and returns infrastructure to rural and secondary markets, there's a 2–3 year window where entire regions lose access to beauty and health products at any price. Second, regulatory scrutiny on supplement safety and efficacy—especially on imported Chinese ingredients—could create a bifurcated market where cheap dupes face recalls while consumers panic-buy at inflated prices.
