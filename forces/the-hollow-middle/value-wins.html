A · The stores splitting apart

The Value Floor

Trading down powers dollar, convenience, and value formats.

When wallets tighten, shoppers don't just buy less—they move down. The value tier is where that traffic lands: dollar stores, convenience stores remaking themselves around cheap fresh food, ethnic supermarkets losing ground to mainstream copycats. The floor is crowded and it's profitable enough to hold, but it's no longer where growth happens.

What changed

Dollar stores and convenience stores moved from niche low-income destinations to mainstream trade-down venues. In 2024 and 2025, as inflation persisted and wage growth stalled, middle-income households began shopping dollar stores and convenience stores alongside existing low-income customers. Consumer sentiment tanked to a record low of 48.2 in May 2026, with Dollar General customers telling the company their own financial situation had worsened—a structural shift, not a cycle. The industry saw this coming: by 2025, 87% of Dollar Tree stores sat within five miles of a Walmart, proof that the format had matured and traffic was reshuffling, not growing. Dollar stores grew only 1.4% annually from 2021 to 2026, reaching $123.5 billion in revenue by 2026 (up from $101.3 billion in 2022), but the growth had gone flat. Convenience stores fared worse—only 0.2% growth over the same period, stuck at $43.7 billion in 2026. Neither format was dying, but both were gasping for air in a market where large discount chains (Walmart, Target) and Amazon offered better selection at comparable prices, squeezing the value middle from all sides.

Costs ravaged margins. Tariffs on imports hit hardest in early 2025: dollar and ethnic supermarkets saw collections on food and agricultural goods jump 647% compared to 2024, a shock that compressed already-thin margins (dollar stores at 4.7–5%, convenience stores at 1.5–2%). Organized retail crime cost the industry $47.8 billion in 2025 and was forecast to hit $55 billion by 2028—convenience stores, with their 24/7 cash-heavy operations, were prime targets, absorbing 6% of all robberies nationally. Labor kept shrinking profit: wages had grown 70% over a decade, making hiring difficult even as convenience stores desperately needed workers to keep shelves stocked and prepared-food stations running. Convenience store operators tried to fight back: those who deployed AI-powered fuel pricing reported 3.4% profit gains, but that was a Band-Aid. The real margin lifeline came from prepared food, which accounted for 39% of in-store profit, yet in-store foodservice sales sat flat in 2025 at $97.3 billion (out of $341.2 billion in total in-store sales).

Ethnic supermarkets faced a worse squeeze. The category had nearly stopped growing—just 0.1% in 2025, down from 1.9% in 2024—while the count of independent ethnic grocers fell 11.3% annually. Mainstream supermarkets (Kroger, Albertsons) had stolen their thunder by launching private-label ethnic foods, making the specialty itself a commodity. Tariff shock hit even harder for ethnic stores because their mix skewed heavily toward imported staples (Asian noodles, spices, fish sauce, Korean prepared foods) that bore the full tariff load. Large regional ethnic chains like Vallarta (49,000 sq ft flagship in Arizona, 2026; planning 15 stores over the next decade) and 99 Ranch (37,000 sq ft flagship in Flushing, 2026; expanding to Las Vegas) responded by going big: food halls, destination appeal, scale plays. But they were racing Amazon and Walmart on delivery networks they could never match.

The real reshuffling was customer behavior. Dollar stores responded to margin pressure and market saturation by investing heavily in fresh produce—DG Fresh, Dollar General's produce brand, had rolled into ~7,000 locations by 2025, a slow but deliberate pivot to compete on freshness and basket size. The company poured $150 million into labor and store remodels in Q1 2025 alone, driving a 5.2% same-store sales bump and a 61 basis point gross margin boost. Convenience stores, sensing that fuel was a race to the bottom (pump prices fell 5.9% in 2025 from $3.30 to $3.11 per gallon), bet their future on prepared food—80% of operators expected foodservice to improve in 2026, betting on personal pizzas and coffee to offset gas-margin collapse. The Couche-Tard attempt to acquire 7-Eleven (bid raised to $47.2 billion in early 2025, then withdrawn in June 2025) signaled that consolidation was the only survival path for smaller players. And it was starting: 88.9% of convenience-store M&A deals in 2025 went to strategic buyers, not financial players—a sign that the era of mom-and-pop c-stores was closing.

The evidence

  • Dollar stores: $123.5 billion market (2026), 1.4% annual growth 2021–2026; 9,400 companies, ~39,000 stores, 600,000+ workers; margins 4.7–5%
  • Convenience stores: $43.7 billion market (2026), 0.2% CAGR 2021–2026; 58,033 stores, ~175,000 employees; margins 1.5–2%, prepared food 39% of in-store profit
  • Ethnic supermarkets: $58.9 billion (2025), near-zero growth at 0.1% in 2025; 16,042 businesses (down 1.3% from 2024); annual closure rate 11.3% for independents
  • Tariffs: 647% spike in food/ag collections early 2025; organized retail crime $47.8 billion (2025), forecast $55 billion (2028)
  • Fuel margin compression: gas prices fell 5.9% in 2025 ($3.30 to $3.11/gallon); AI pricing automation yielded 3.4% profit gains for c-store operators

Who rides it

Walmart and Target win by weaponizing scale: they undercut dollar stores on price while offering fresher produce, prepared foods, and destination appeal that smaller value retailers cannot match. Large regional ethnic chains (Vallarta, 99 Ranch, H Mart) that can swing investment in flagships and food halls expand and consolidate territory, banking on brand loyalty and destination shopping. Convenience store consolidators—strategic buyers picking up small chains in 88.9% of 2025 M&A deals—win by eliminating redundant labor and standardizing operations to survive in a margin-compressed world.

Who's squeezed

Independent ethnic supermarkets and dollar stores without scale are getting crushed. Ethnic store closures are accelerating (11.3% annually), with mainline grocers stealing their ethnic-product differentiation through private labels (Kroger's Hispanic lines, Korean and Chinese meal kits, 2026) and Amazon/Walmart offering delivery at speed these stores can never match. Convenience store owners face a choice: consolidate or die. Mom-and-pop c-store operators are being pushed out as wage inflation and organized theft make labor and security unaffordable on thin 1.5–2% margins. Tariffs have weaponized import-heavy businesses—ethnic supermarkets bearing 647% tariff spikes cannot absorb the cost into already-thin 3% margins.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that the value floor itself collapses if consumer sentiment doesn't stabilize. May 2026's record low of 48.2 signals that shoppers' financial strain is structural, not cyclical—if incomes continue to stagnate while inflation persists, even the value tier becomes unaffordable for millions, meaning traffic that should hold the format instead shifts to even cheaper formats (buy-in-bulk clubs, secondhand, food banks) or simply vanishes. A consumer recession would prove that even the floor has room to fall.

In one sentence

The value floor in a nutshell

The value floor stays crowded but stops growing—Walmart and large chains trade down from their upper tiers while small independents and undifferentiated mid-tier operators close or consolidate away.
