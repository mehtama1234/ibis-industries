B · Apparel & footwear bifurcate

Footwear & the niche

Specialty and athletic footwear thrive; generic mid-tier shoe retail shrinks.

The footwear market is splitting down the middle. Premium athletic stores and specialty labels are growing, while undifferentiated shoe retailers—the ones selling decent but unremarkable mid-priced sneakers—are disappearing. Tariffs, brand consolidation, and online channels have made it impossible to survive in the mushy middle.

What changed

For twenty years, shoe retail was simple. Nike and Adidas flooded wholesale, mall-anchor shoe stores sold volume, and margins stayed thin but predictable. That model broke in 2025-2026.

Start with tariffs. The Trump administration's 20-27% tariff regime on Chinese footwear (imposed April 2025, invalidated by the Supreme Court February 20, 2026, then reimposed under alternative authority) created three rounds of supply-chain chaos. A shoe that cost $50 to land now costs $62. Retailers with 3.8-3.9% EBIT margins cannot absorb that. They raised prices, watched traffic drop, and scrambled. Specialty running stores held steady because they offer fit expertise that online cannot match—57% of performance-oriented buyers still come to them. But generic shoe retailers collapsed.

Next, the brands took back control. Nike and Adidas spent three years cutting thousands of small retailers out of their wholesale networks to push customers toward their own websites and apps (higher margins, direct feedback, no middleman). Nike ended relationships with DSW, Urban Outfitters, and Olympia Sports in 2025. When Nike finally realized it was cannibalizing wholesale revenue, it reversed some cuts—but the damage was done. The largest pure-play shoe retailer, Foot Locker (which operated thousands of stores selling $17 billion worth of shoes in the 2021-2022 period), could not survive standalone. Dick's Sporting Goods acquired it for $2.4-2.5 billion in September 2025, absorbing 275 Foot Locker closures and 125 Champs Sports shutters (62.5% of that chain) by end of 2026. This is the end of the independent shoe retailer era.

Meanwhile, the market is hollowing out. E-commerce penetration in footwear reached 30-35% of total sales by mid-2025, growing 7-9% annually while physical stores flatlined. Mall foot traffic declined for the first time since the pandemic in the second half of 2025—particularly crushing the super-regional malls where mid-tier shoe chains clustered. The US shoe retail market, worth $41.8 billion in 2021, reached only $67.5 billion in 2025 but is now in structural decline (-1.0% projected for 2025 according to IBISWorld). Bankruptcies cascaded: Sneakersnstuff (Swedish parent SNS AB) filed for Chapter 11 in January 2025, closing stores in New York and Los Angeles. Soleply filed Chapter 11 in March 2025, closing 4 of 6 locations. Even Allbirds, the DTC-native shoe brand that went public for $4 billion in 2021, sold itself for $39 million in February 2026 after closing all full-price US stores.

What got scarce was the middle. You cannot compete as a mid-tier shoe store. Luxury and heritage brands (Cole Haan, Allen Edmonds, Allbirds-as-outlet) and online-first insurgents moved up or down. Specialty athletic stores and independent running boutiques survived because they sell expertise, community, and fit—not just shoes. Dick's now controls 3,200+ stores globally, bundling footwear with sporting goods so no single category has to carry margin pressure alone. The $32-42 billion athletic shoe retail segment grew 2.7-5.9% CAGR through 2030-2034, but only because consolidation and specialty focus are forcing out the undifferentiated players.

The evidence

  • Athletic Shoe Stores: $32-42 billion market (2025-2026) with 2.7-5.9% CAGR through 2030-2034; but only 3,678 locations (2025, +1.5% from 2024), meaning consolidation is squeezing unit count despite dollar growth
  • Foot Locker collapse: 275 of 1,200+ Foot Locker stores closing by end 2026 plus 125 Champs Sports closures (62.5% of chain), per Dick's September 2025 acquisition announcement
  • Nike wholesale pullback: Nike ended relationships with DSW, Urban Outfitters, Olympia Sports in 2025; Sneakersnstuff (SNS AB) filed Chapter 11 January 2025; Soleply filed Chapter 11 March 2025
  • Allbirds collapse: Sold for $39 million in February 2026 after IPO at $4 billion in 2021; closed all full-price US stores due to inability to compete mid-market
  • Tariff cascade: 20-27% Trump tariff on Chinese footwear (April 2025), Supreme Court invalidation (February 20, 2026), then reimposition under alternative authority—three rounds of supply chain disruption

Who rides it

Specialty athletic retailers and independent running stores are thriving. They sell expertise—gait analysis, fit guidance, community events—that Amazon cannot replicate. Fifty-seven percent of performance-oriented buyers still visit them because the right shoe prevents injury. Dick's Sporting Goods won by consolidating scale: it acquired Foot Locker's 3,200+ stores and leveraged bundled sporting goods to dilute margin pressure across categories. Heritage brands and luxury lines (Cole Haan, Allen Edmonds) survived by moving upmarket. DTC-native upscale brands like Skims and Savage X Fenty are capturing high-income customers willing to pay for design and brand. Even subscription and loyalty models are working for niche players like Adore Me (now part of Victoria's Secret) because they lock in repeat purchase. The winners all share one trait: they are not selling undifferentiated shoes to whoever walks in.

Who's squeezed

Mid-tier shoe retailers got crushed. Foot Locker, which once defined shoe retail and generated $17-19 billion in US volume (2021-2022), is now being dismantled. Smaller chains and independents selling Nike and Adidas at full price cannot compete when Nike itself sells direct online. Margin compression from tariffs (landed costs up 20-27% from April 2025) combined with e-commerce growth (30-35% penetration, growing 7-9% annually) left them no room to cut. Malls where these stores clustered lost foot traffic for the first time since the pandemic in late 2025. Brand-specific retailers—the ones betting their entire model on Nike or Adidas inventory—watched those brands pull wholesale relationships. Specialty chains without expertise advantage, without direct relationships with customers, and without the scale to bundle categories simply disappeared.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that specialty stores survive the tariff moment but then get crushed by Amazon penetration. Online footwear is already 30-35% and growing 7-9% annually. Specialty running stores hold 57% of performance customers now, but that assumes customers still value fit expertise enough to visit a store. If that assumption breaks—if younger shoppers accept misfit shoes because they are cheap online, or if VR fitting improves—then even the specialty segment disappears. Tariffs also risk spreading to tariffs on footwear from Vietnam and India, the secondary suppliers filling Nike and Adidas factories. If those tariffs land in 2026-2027, the landing cost squeeze deepens and even niche retailers with loyal customers may not survive. The hollowing out may be permanent.
