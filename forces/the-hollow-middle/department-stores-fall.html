A · The stores splitting apart

The department store falls

Mid-tier anchors of American malls hollow out as tariffs, luxury bankruptcy, and bargain hunting rip shoppers away.

The department store used to be the middle ground—not fancy, not discount, just normal. In 2025 and 2026, that middle ground vanished. Shoppers fled to luxury or bargain basements, tariffs compressed already-thin margins to breaking point, and Saks Global's bankruptcy in January 2026 proved even the merged mega-players couldn't hold the line.

What changed

The US department store industry sits at $227.1 billion in 2026, growing at only 0.3% annually and contracting 0.4% in 2026 alone. That flatness masks a violent hollowing: the market is cleaving into a luxury tier and a value tier, leaving everyone in the middle gasping. Start with tariffs. Apparel import duties exploded from 14.7% in January 2025 to 35.1% by December 2025—a 138% spike in one year. Even after the Supreme Court struck down country-specific rates in February 2026 and rates settled to a 10% flat China rate plus 15% EU ceiling by July 2026, the damage was permanent. Department store margins, already thin at 6.0% in 2026, had to absorb those tariff shocks. Retailers faced an impossible choice: eat the cost and watch profits evaporate, or pass it to shoppers and lose customers. Macy's chose the retreat. The company closed 50 stores by end-2024, then 66 more in 2025, with another 14 planned for 2026. Yet Q3 2025 showed modest comp sales growth of 2.5% to 3.2%, suggesting some stabilization—but only through aggressive pruning. The canary in the coal mine was Saks Global. In January 2026, the mega-merger of Saks Fifth Avenue, Neiman Marcus, and Bergdorf Goodman filed Chapter 11 with $3.4 billion in debt. The theory was consolidation would save the luxury tier. Instead, store productivity cratered, vendor payments got backed up, and the merged entity couldn't survive. Even at the top of the market, the middle-market logic was broken. Meanwhile, shoppers were voting with their wallets. Discount department stores grabbed market share relentlessly. The off-price channel—Nordstrom Rack, TJX stores, Marshall's, Saks Off Fifth, outlets—became the place where real customers shopped. Ross Stores reported comparable sales up 17% in Q3 2025 and announced 110 new store openings for 2026. That's not marginal growth; that's velocity. Inflation, which pushed consumer price expectations up 4.1% year-over-year as of May 2026, was the accelerant. Affluent households absorbed higher prices and kept shopping full-price. Everyone else traded down five times faster, abandoning the mid-tier department store for bargain basements or going without. The industry lost 2.87% of its 666,500 employees annually through 2028, a steady bleed as stores closed and foot traffic never came back. The $227.1 billion market of 2026 looks almost unchanged from 2022's $139.6 billion growth projection, but the breakdown is everything: luxury collapsed, value exploded, the middle got wiped out.

The evidence

  • US department store industry: $227.1B market size in 2026 growing at 0.3% CAGR and contracting 0.4% in 2026; Macy's holds 7.8% share ($22.6B 2025 revenue) with 150 stores closing by end-2026 (2025-2026)
  • Apparel tariff rates spiked from 14.7% (January 2025) to 35.1% (December 2025), then settled to ~10% flat China rate plus 15% EU ceiling by July 2026 after Supreme Court ruling (2025-2026)
  • Saks Global filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy January 2026 with $3.4B debt after consolidating Saks Fifth Avenue, Neiman Marcus, and Bergdorf Goodman; store productivity and vendor payments both collapsed (2026)
  • Off-price retail (Ross, TJX, Nordstrom Rack) captured market share: Ross comparable sales +17% Q3 2025 with 110 new openings planned; Nordstrom family launched $3.8B bid to take Nordstrom private while expanding Rack (2025-2026)
  • Inflation eroding purchasing power at 4.1% PCE year-over-year (May 2026) drove bifurcated consumer behavior: affluent absorbing prices, lower/middle-income trading down to discount stores at five times faster rates (2026)

Who rides it

Off-price retailers and true discounters win decisively. TJX, Ross, and outlet chains grab shoppers fleeing mid-tier department stores. The treasure hunt experience—curated inventory that changes weekly—proves defensible against e-commerce. Nordstrom's decision to go private and focus on expansion of Nordstrom Rack signals how the family is willing to abandon full-line department stores entirely to bet on off-price. Discount department stores as a sector stabilized at $107.2 billion in 2026, barely growing but holding ground, while traditional department stores hollow out around them.

Who's squeezed

Mainstream department store chains get squeezed from both ends. Macy's, JCPenney, and regional players are stuck in the middle: not premium enough to attract luxury shoppers, not cheap enough to compete with Ross or Walmart. JCPenney's 'Yes, JCPenney' comeback lifted traffic by 3% in May 2025 but revenue still fell to $6.6 billion in FY2024. Vendors lose too—payment backlogs cascade through the system (Saks Global proved that even merged players can't pay on time). Store employees hemorrhage: 2.87% annual job losses through 2028. The $227.1 billion market is essentially flat, but the floor is collapsing beneath the middle.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The structural problem is that tariffs made mid-tier retail unprofitable and consumers have already learned to shop elsewhere. Even if tariffs settle (they did, to ~10% flat by July 2026), that damage is permanent. Shoppers won't come back to Macy's because prices normalized. They've already switched to Ross or Amazon or stopped shopping department stores altogether. The second risk is cascading: if Saks Global—the desperate merger of the three biggest luxury/aspirational department stores—couldn't survive $3.4 billion in debt and vendor payment chaos, then regional and smaller chains have almost no runway. More bankruptcies will follow, accelerating the exodus.

In one sentence

The department store falls in a nutshell

The middle-market department store became undefendable in 2025-2026 when tariffs compressed margins beyond recovery and shoppers split into bargain-hunting and luxury, leaving undifferentiated mid-tier players like Macy's and JCPenney to close stores and hope for modest stabilization.
