Hotels pick a lane

Budget and luxury boutique hotels boom while mid-scale motels face margin squeeze.

The US hotel industry split into two businesses in 2025. Luxury boutique hotels grabbed a six percent revenue jump with average daily rates hitting $452. Extended-stay properties grew four percent in supply—the biggest jump in four years—riding remote work and housing shortages. Meanwhile, the traditional mid-scale motel got caught in the middle, crushed by wages that rose 25.6 percent since 2019 and shrinking occupancy.

What changed

For decades, American hotels operated on a simple formula: rent rooms by the night, keep costs flat, watch margins grow. That model broke in 2024 and 2025. The fracture happened on two fronts.

First, labor destroyed the economics of mediocrity. Hotel wages climbed 25.6 percent against 2019 levels, and labor cost per occupied room jumped 21 percent from Q4 2024 to Q4 2025. Operating costs followed—insurance spiked one hundred percent, utilities up thirty percent. Those numbers sound abstract until you do the math: a mid-scale hotel operator paying $54.98 in labor cost per occupied room cannot absorb that squeeze while charging $110–130 per night. Luxury properties charging $452 per night could absorb wage increases and pass them to customers. Budget operators targeting $65–85 per night had already engineered skeleton crews with sixty to eighty percent annual turnover. The middle got destroyed.

Second, the demand split became irreversible. Luxury boutique hotels hit 70 to 75 percent occupancy in 2025 while economy segments flatlined. Extended-stay hotels filled a structural need: eighteen million Americans identify as digital nomads, four million homes vanished from the US housing supply, and over twenty-one million renters spend more than thirty percent of income on housing. Extended-stay properties expanded supply four percent in 2025—the most in four years—and bounced back to 76.7 percent occupancy by May 2026 after a rough 2025. Independent boutique owners watched their category become hot, but only if you had scale. Sonder—the poster child for boutique hotel tech—folded in November 2025 after Marriott killed its deal citing financial constraints. Revo Hospitality Group, operating 260 properties, filed Chapter 11 in January 2026 as refinancing walls peaked. What killed them was simple: small and mid-scale operators could not compete on brand distribution once metasearch and Google Hotel Ads commanded $8.2 billion in annual spend, and they could not absorb $21 percent labor jumps on $100–150 average daily rates.

The chains responded by converting the market, not building it. Economy properties shifted to extended-stay formats. Hilton launched Apartment Collection by Hilton in January 2026 with Placemakr, racing to offer furnished apartments at scale and compete head-to-head with Airbnb, which now offers one to two bedroom units in urban markets two times cheaper than hotel equivalents. Marriott opened StudioRes, a budget extended-stay brand, with eighty-five properties in the pipeline. IHG recorded a milestone: opening four hundred forty-three new hotels in Q1 2026 and passing seven thousand operating properties. The industry shifted from organic development to rapid conversion—lower capital expense, faster reopenings, and instant modern amenities. That path is not available to independent operators or mom-and-pop motels.

The evidence

  • Luxury boutique hotels achieved 6% revenue gain in 2025 with $452 average daily rates (IBISWorld), while mid-scale properties contracted.
  • Extended-stay hotels grew 4% in supply in 2025 and bounced to 76.7% occupancy by May 2026, the largest supply increase in four years (STR data).
  • Labor cost per occupied room jumped 21% from Q4 2024 to Q4 2025, hitting $54.98; wages up 25.6% versus 2019 (hotel operator surveys).
  • Sonder (Marriott-backed boutique operator) filed Chapter 7 liquidation in November 2025; Revo Hospitality Group (260 properties) filed Chapter 11 in January 2026.
  • Hilton launched Apartment Collection by Hilton in January 2026 and now operates 1,000 luxury and lifestyle hotels globally; IHG opened 443 new hotels in Q1 2026 and passed 7,000 operating properties.

Who rides it

Luxury boutique and extended-stay operators win. They have pricing power to absorb wage and operating-cost inflation. Boutique luxury hit 6 percent revenue growth in 2025 when the broader industry barely moved. Extended-stay hits 76.7 percent occupancy and 5.4 percent demand growth in Q1 2026 because they solve a real housing and work-pattern crisis. Large chains win by acquiring independents and converting portfolios: Hilton, IHG, and Marriott all rolled out collection brands and extended-stay formats to capture the split. Chains also win on distribution—metasearch and Google Hotel Ads now own the customer interface.

Who's squeezed

Mid-scale motels and independent boutique owners get squeezed from both sides. Wages rose 25.6 percent but they cannot raise room rates $300 more per night to compensate. Operating costs climbed across the board—insurance one hundred percent, utilities thirty percent—while occupancy stayed flat. Independent boutique operators who built a category watched Sonder and Revo implode on maturity walls and refinancing crises. Those still standing either sell to a chain (surrendering brand independence) or join a collection brand at scale. The mom-and-pop motel operator has no path forward except conversion to extended-stay or acquisition.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that the bifurcation locks in and regional mid-market hotels disappear entirely. Once extended-stay and luxury capture the demand, volume-play economy properties become a commodity business with razor-thin margins. Chains can absorb losses on economy by cross-subsidizing from luxury. Independents cannot. If Airbnb continues undercutting hotel prices by two times in urban markets while digital nomads and housing refugees support extended-stay growth, the whole mid-tier category—not just weak players—could vanish within three to five years.

In one sentence

Hotels pick a lane in a nutshell

The US hotel industry bifurcated in 2025: luxury and extended-stay captured demand and pricing power, while mid-scale operators caught between wage inflation and flat occupancy face extinction or absorption into a chain.
