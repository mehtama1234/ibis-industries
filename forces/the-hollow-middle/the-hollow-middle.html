The Hollow Middle

Shoppers are splitting into two tribes—bargain hunters and the wealthy—and everyone selling the undifferentiated stuff in between is getting crushed.

The American retail landscape is splitting in two. On one side, bargain hunters are flooding dollar stores and Walmart looking for survival deals. On the other, the wealthy are still spending on luxury goods and premium experiences. Everyone stuck in the middle—department stores, chain restaurants, mid-tier jewelry shops—is getting squeezed from both directions at once. The math is brutal: if your margin is thin to begin with and your customers are choosing either cheaper or fancier, your business just stops working.

What changed

The middle market collapse happened because three forces hit simultaneously in 2025-2026. First, tariffs exploded. Apparel tariffs jumped from 14.7% in January 2025 to 35.1% by December 2025, forcing retailers to either eat the cost (crushing thin margins) or raise prices (driving price-sensitive shoppers away). Jewelry tariffs on Chinese imports hit 145% by late 2025, adding 18-22% to wholesale costs overnight. These weren't small headwinds—they were margin killers for anyone selling undifferentiated goods where customers compare prices obsessively. Second, gold prices surged approximately 60% annually in 2025, peaking near $4,400 per ounce in October. That simple fact pushed entry-level jewelry from around $99 to the $200-$500 range, cutting off the mass market entirely. Consumers didn't stop buying jewelry—the wealthy bought lab-grown diamonds (now 56.8% of engagement rings) and investment pieces, while everyone else just stopped. The middle customer evaporated.

Third, consumer finances bifurcated. Wage stagnation and persistent inflation created two shopping behaviors: survival shopping at dollar stores and Walmart, or quality shopping for things that last. In May 2026, consumer sentiment hit a record low of 48.2, signaling that middle-income shoppers were actually getting worse off, not better. Dollar stores saw trade-down behavior from middle-income households become structural—87% of Dollar Tree stores sit within five miles of a Walmart, intensifying the cannibalization. Meanwhile, department store margins compressed to 6.0% in 2026 as discounting escalated. Saks Global filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy in January 2026 with $3.4 billion in debt, collapsing the luxury tier that had once anchored department stores. Chain restaurants saw 42% of operators become unprofitable in 2025, with labor costs up 39% since pre-pandemic and food costs 34% above pre-pandemic levels. The casual dining segment—full-service restaurants at mid-price points—got hit hardest: TGI Friday's, Red Lobster, On The Border, and Bravo Brio all filed bankruptcy. Fast-casual's premium pricing ($15+ entrees) drove customers down to Chili's and Texas Roadhouse, who competed on sit-down deals at $12. But those deals killed margins too.

The bifurcation created two stable, opposite outcomes. At the low end, dollar stores and discount retailers locked in a resilient customer base making trade-down choices: 5.2% sales growth at Dollar General in Q1 2025, with gross margins actually expanding by 61 basis points from labor and store remodeling investments. Walmart and Costco weaponized their scale to undercut dollar stores while offering fresher food and better quality—they won the value game decisively. At the high end, the wealthy stayed confident spenders, keeping luxury jewelry and premium goods alive, though even luxury had to weather the tariff shock. What broke was the undifferentiated middle: retailers selling ordinary department store apparel at ordinary prices, or chain restaurants serving $15 burgers to price-conscious families. The US department store industry—$227.1 billion in 2026—grew only 0.3% annually through 2026 and actually contracted 0.4% in 2026 alone. Macy's executed a controlled retreat, closing 50 stores by end-2024, 66 in 2025, and 14 more planned for 2026. JCPenney launched a comeback campaign and saw 3% traffic lift in May 2025, but revenue still fell to $6.6 billion for FY2024. Jewelry stores saw the sharpest contraction: 12.1% decline in 2025 and 2.2% in 2026, with 167 US stores closing in Q1 2025 alone (21% increase from Q1 2024).

This isn't a cycle—it's a structural reshuffling. Tariffs punished importers of standard goods (apparel, jewelry) far more than they punished premium or domestic suppliers. Inflation hurt the price-conscious middle more than the wealthy. E-commerce and mobile commerce (over 60% of online jewelry sales) bypassed the middle-tier retail format entirely, moving volume straight to either Amazon and discount marketplaces or to direct-to-consumer luxury brands. The middle market wasn't squeezed by one competitor or one trend. It was squeezed by the fact that the customer it depended on—the middle-income American willing to spend a normal amount on a normal good—is either trading down to free up cash or trading up to buy something worth the price. The margin math no longer works for anyone selling the ordinary stuff in between.

The evidence

  • Department Stores: Saks Global filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy in January 2026 carrying $3.4 billion in debt; Macy's closed 150 stores (50 by end-2024, 66 in 2025, 14 more planned for 2026); tariff rates hit 35% on apparel by December 2025.
  • Dollar Stores: 87% of Dollar Tree stores are within five miles of a Walmart; consumer sentiment collapsed to 48.2 in May 2026; Dollar General Q1 2025 saw 5.2% sales growth and +61 basis point margin expansion (2025-2026).
  • Jewelry Retail: Gold prices surged 60% annually in 2025; tariffs on Chinese imports reached 145% by late 2025; Claire's filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy in August 2025 (second bankruptcy in seven years) (2025-2026).
  • Chain Restaurants: 42% of operators unprofitable in 2025; minimum wages now $16-20 per hour in major states; TGI Friday's, Red Lobster, On The Border, and Bravo Brio filed bankruptcy in 2024-2025 as full-service casual dining segment collapsed (2024-2025).
  • Consumer Bifurcation: Wealthy remained confident spenders in luxury; middle-income households experiencing wage stagnation and persistent inflation shifted to survival shopping; lab-grown diamonds now 56.8% of engagement ring market as luxury consumers trade up while mass market exits (2025-2026).

Who rides it

Discount retailers and high-end luxury both won. Dollar stores and Walmart captured structural trade-down behavior from middle-income households—Consumer sentiment at record lows meant survival shopping became the norm. Off-price retailers (Nordstrom Rack, outlets) thrived as customers wanted deals. The Nordstrom family launched a $3.8 billion bid to take the company private in late 2025, betting on off-price expansion. At the top, wealthy consumers kept buying premium goods and lab-grown diamonds (56.8% of engagement rings), insulating luxury from the middle market collapse. E-commerce and direct-to-consumer brands bypassed traditional retail formats entirely, capturing 25% of jewelry purchases globally and growing 13% annually.

Who's squeezed

Mid-tier retailers selling undifferentiated goods got destroyed. Department stores with 6.0% margins in 2026 couldn't survive tariff shocks or discounting wars. Chain restaurants with 39% labor cost increases and 34% food inflation couldn't raise prices without losing customers—42% were unprofitable in 2025. Jewelry stores faced the sharpest contraction: 12.1% in 2025, 2.2% in 2026, 167 closures in Q1 2025 alone. Full-service casual dining (TGI Friday's, Red Lobster, Bravo Brio, On The Border) filed bankruptcy because their $15-20 entrees were too expensive for trade-down shoppers and not premium enough to command luxury margins. The middle customer—willing to spend normal money on normal goods—either doesn't exist anymore or is trading to one extreme.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that this bifurcation becomes permanent, not cyclical. If wage stagnation continues and consumer sentiment stays at record lows (48.2 in May 2026), the trade-down behavior locks in structurally. That means middle-income households stay locked into dollar stores and discount retail for survival, unable to graduate back to normal spending. Meanwhile, tariffs on imported goods remain high or ratchet higher, making it impossible for mid-tier retailers to ever rebuild margins through volume or pricing power. The retail labor force—666,500 in department stores alone, declining 2.87% annually—keeps shrinking. If the middle market never heals, we don't just lose retail jobs; we lose the economic engine that once connected small cities and suburbs to national consumer markets. The hollowed-out retail landscape becomes a symptom of a hollowed-out middle class.

In one sentence

The hollow middle in a nutshell

The middle market is collapsing as tariffs, inflation, and consumer bifurcation create a two-tier consumer economy where mid-tier retailers face margins so thin they can barely survive.
