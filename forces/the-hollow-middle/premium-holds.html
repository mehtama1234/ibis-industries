A · The stores splitting apart

The premium ceiling

Affluent shoppers keep spending on premium jewelry and beauty, but the market splits—and middle-tier retailers fall apart.

Shoppers didn't stop buying jewelry or beauty products. But the people who buy them split into two groups, and the middle disappeared. Premium brands that cater to affluent consumers are thriving. Everything else is dying.

What changed

Gold prices nearly doubled in 2025, surging about 60% annually and peaking near $4,400 per ounce in October. That pushed entry-level jewelry prices from around $99 to $200-$500. Suddenly the mass market couldn't afford it. Store-after-store closed—167 jewelry stores shut down in Q1 2025 alone, a 21% jump from the same quarter the year before. By the end of 2024, the total was 747 discontinuances. The jewelry business contracted 12.1% in 2025 and another 2.2% in 2026, shrinking from a $61.7 billion market to something much smaller. Two major chains filed for bankruptcy: Claire's in August 2025 (its second bankruptcy in seven years) and Lugano Diamonds in November. Margins compressed to 3.0-3.4%, squeezed by tariffs, gold prices, and the shift to online shopping.

Beauty told a different story on the surface, but the mechanics were identical. Ulta Beauty, the premium player, posted $12.4 billion in sales for fiscal 2025 and grew 9.7% year-over-year, guided to 6-7% growth in 2026. But smaller specialty beauty brands evaporated. Pat McGrath Labs filed Chapter 11 in early 2026. Flower Beauty shut down in September 2025. Cover FX and Mally Beauty closed through their parent company's restructuring in January 2026. Glossier closed 75% of its stores. The market size—$69.3 billion in 2026—looked stable at first glance, but that hid a brutal consolidation where one player (Ulta) captured growth while dozens of smaller brands simply vanished.

Tariffs on Chinese imports hit 145% by late 2025, forcing wholesale costs up 18-22% through spring 2025. For jewelry, that meant $25-30 million in additional costs if applied to a full fiscal year. For beauty, tariffs became another squeeze on already-thin margins for anyone not large enough to absorb the hit. Online channels captured an estimated 25% of jewelry purchases globally by 2026, with mobile accounting for over 60% of online transactions. TikTok Shop alone posted $2.49 billion in US beauty GMV in 2025, a 123% jump year-over-year. Amazon's beauty share is forecast to climb from 10% in 2024 to 15% by 2030, while Sephora and Ulta's combined share dips from 20% to 19%.

Lab-grown diamonds reshaped the entire jewelry category. They captured 56.8% of the US engagement ring market by late 2025, fundamentally changing both product mix and what consumers would pay. The average lab-grown diamond went from 1.31 carats in 2019 to 2.45 carats in 2025—buyers got twice as much for the same or less money. That destroyed margins on traditional mined diamonds and forced traditional jewelers to retrain staff, restock, and reposition. Anyone stuck selling mid-tier jewelry got crushed between affluent buyers who wanted luxury and value buyers who wanted lab diamonds at deep discounts. The middle emptied out.

The evidence

  • Jewelry retail: $61.7B market (2025) down 12.1% YoY, with 167 store closures in Q1 2025 alone and gold prices up 60% annually to $4,400/oz in October 2025, pricing out the mass market.
  • Jewelry bankruptcy wave: Claire's filed Chapter 11 in August 2025 (second time in seven years) and Lugano Diamonds filed November 2025, as brick-and-mortar contraction accelerated.
  • Beauty premiumization: Ulta Beauty posted $12.4B in fiscal 2025 sales (up 9.7% YoY, guiding 6-7% growth in 2026), while Pat McGrath Labs filed Chapter 11, Flower Beauty closed (September 2025), and Glossier shuttered 75% of stores.
  • Tariff shock: Tariffs on Chinese imports hit 145% by late 2025, raising wholesale beauty and jewelry costs 18-22% as of spring 2025, with $25-30M annual impact per affected retailer.
  • Lab diamonds: Lab-grown diamonds captured 56.8% of the US engagement ring market by late 2025 (vs. negligible share in 2019), with average carat weight doubling from 1.31 to 2.45 carats, undercutting mined diamond margins.

Who rides it

Ulta Beauty and premium-focused brands thrive. Ulta's fiscal 2025 sales hit $12.4B with 9.7% growth, comp sales up 5.4%, and guidance for 6-7% growth in fiscal 2026. Amazon and TikTok Shop capture discovery and repeat purchases—TikTok Shop posted $2.49B US beauty GMV in 2025 with 123% YoY growth. Affluent consumers keep spending on status goods and premium beauty regardless of tariffs or gold prices. Lab-grown diamonds win, too—they capture 56.8% of engagement rings and let buyers get more carats for less money, reshaping the entire category.

Who's squeezed

Mid-tier jewelry and beauty retailers get crushed. Smaller specialty brands—Pat McGrath Labs, Flower Beauty, Cover FX, Mally Beauty, Glossier—collapse or nearly vanish. Traditional jewelry stores close by the hundreds. Margins compress to 3.0-3.4% for jewelers, a fraction of historical norms. Retailers stuck selling undifferentiated mid-tier goods can't compete upmarket against Ulta's scale and brand power, and they can't compete downmarket against online channels and lab diamonds. The Ulta-Target partnership ended in August 2026, further squeezing distribution for competitors.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The tariff shock is far from over. At 145%, tariffs on Chinese imports forced costs up 18-22% by spring 2025, and those pass through to consumers in 2026 and beyond. If tariffs stick or rise further, even affluent consumers may retrench on discretionary purchases. Jewelry and beauty both depend on discovery and impulse buying, and online channels (Amazon, TikTok) are redirecting that spending away from traditional retail. Lab-grown diamonds will keep undercutting mined-diamond margins. There's no price floor for mid-tier retailers—they have no cost advantage, no premium positioning, and no channel lock-in.

In one sentence

The premium ceiling in a nutshell

Premium jewelry and beauty retailers thrive while tariffs and gold prices destroy the middle market, leaving small brands and traditional stores to close or file bankruptcy.
