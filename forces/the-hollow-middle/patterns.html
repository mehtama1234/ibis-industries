The patterns

The middle market collapses — shoppers split into premium and value, and anyone stuck selling undifferentiated mid-tier goods gets squeezed from both sides.

The middle market collapse happened because three forces hit simultaneously in 2025-2026. First, tariffs exploded. Apparel tariffs jumped from 14.7% in January 2025 to 35.1% by December 2025, forcing retailers to either eat the cost (crushing thin margins) or raise prices (driving price-sensitive shoppers away). Jewelry tariffs on Chinese imports hit 145% by late 2025, adding 18-22% to wholesale costs overnight. These weren't small headwinds—they were margin killers for anyone selling undifferentiated goods where customers compare prices obsessively. Second, gold prices surged approximately 60% annually in 2025, peaking near $4,400 per ounce in October. That simple fact pushed entry-level jewelry from around $99 to the $200-$500 range, cutting off the mass market entirely. Consumers didn't stop buying jewelry—the wealthy bought lab-grown diamonds (now 56.8% of engagement rings) and investment pieces, while everyone else just stopped. The middle customer evaporated.

The through-line

The middle market is collapsing as tariffs, inflation, and consumer bifurcation create a two-tier consumer economy where mid-tier retailers face margins so thin they can barely survive.

Who wins across the board

Discount retailers and high-end luxury both won. Dollar stores and Walmart captured structural trade-down behavior from middle-income households—Consumer sentiment at record lows meant survival shopping became the norm. Off-price retailers (Nordstrom Rack, outlets) thrived as customers wanted deals. The Nordstrom family launched a $3.8 billion bid to take the company private in late 2025, betting on off-price expansion. At the top, wealthy consumers kept buying premium goods and lab-grown diamonds (56.8% of engagement rings), insulating luxury from the middle market collapse. E-commerce and direct-to-consumer brands bypassed traditional retail formats entirely, capturing 25% of jewelry purchases globally and growing 13% annually.

Who's squeezed across the board

Mid-tier retailers selling undifferentiated goods got destroyed. Department stores with 6.0% margins in 2026 couldn't survive tariff shocks or discounting wars. Chain restaurants with 39% labor cost increases and 34% food inflation couldn't raise prices without losing customers—42% were unprofitable in 2025. Jewelry stores faced the sharpest contraction: 12.1% in 2025, 2.2% in 2026, 167 closures in Q1 2025 alone. Full-service casual dining (TGI Friday's, Red Lobster, Bravo Brio, On The Border) filed bankruptcy because their $15-20 entrees were too expensive for trade-down shoppers and not premium enough to command luxury margins. The middle customer—willing to spend normal money on normal goods—either doesn't exist anymore or is trading to one extreme.

The biggest tension

The one to watch

The biggest risk is that this bifurcation becomes permanent, not cyclical. If wage stagnation continues and consumer sentiment stays at record lows (48.2 in May 2026), the trade-down behavior locks in structurally. That means middle-income households stay locked into dollar stores and discount retail for survival, unable to graduate back to normal spending. Meanwhile, tariffs on imported goods remain high or ratchet higher, making it impossible for mid-tier retailers to ever rebuild margins through volume or pricing power. The retail labor force—666,500 in department stores alone, declining 2.87% annually—keeps shrinking. If the middle market never heals, we don't just lose retail jobs; we lose the economic engine that once connected small cities and suburbs to national consumer markets. The hollowed-out retail landscape becomes a symptom of a hollowed-out middle class.
