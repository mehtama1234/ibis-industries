Paying for the Experience

Consumers splurge on blockbuster moments while casual experiences die—leaving mid-tier venues trapped.

Consumers are trading goods for moments. Amusement parks, concert venues, and movie theaters are pulling in record dollars—but the money is clumping at the extremes. A Disney guest pays $224 for a peak-day Disneyland ticket in 2025, while regional parks hemorrhage attendance. Concerts now average $144 per ticket, but small independent venues are disappearing. Movie theaters rake in billions, yet half the industry's independent chains filed for bankruptcy.

What changed

The shift from casual consumption to selective indulgence created a new scarcity: authentic, differentiated experiences. What happened is that pent-up leisure demand rewarded the spectacular and starved the ordinary. Disney's domestic parks pulled 76.5 million visitors in 2024—a nominal success—but the company is using a two-price strategy that screams bifurcation: peak-day tickets hit $224 at Disneyland and over $200 at Magic Kingdom (October 2025), while simultaneously offering the deepest discounts in years to maintain traffic. This is not margin expansion; this is elasticity breaking. Meanwhile, the Cedar Fair-Six Flags merger (completed July 2024, creating a 27-park combined operator) is drowning: a $5.5 billion debt load and Q2 2025 attendance down 1.4 million year-over-year. Pent-up demand is real, but it is not equally distributed. It flows to new spectacles—Universal's Epic Universe opened in May 2025 with five themed worlds (Harry Potter, Super Nintendo World, How to Train Your Dragon) and is projected to pull 5.2 million visitors in its opening year, climbing to 9.2 million by 2026, potentially boosting Universal Orlando's total attendance by 50%. That demand is stolen from everyone else. Concert and event promotion is experiencing a similar split. Live Nation reported record revenue of $25.2 billion in 2025 (9% year-over-year growth) and 159 million fans attending 55,000 concerts globally, with ticket prices averaging $144 (up 45% since 2019). The willingness to pay is there: 36% of consumers are willing to spend $100-$499 per ticket, and 17% will spend up to $1,000. But this premium consumption is a trap for the mid-tier venue. Rockwood Music Hall, an iconic NYC independent venue, closed in November 2024. The Music Hall of Williamsburg faces eviction at the end of 2026. Avant Gardner filed for bankruptcy owing $155 million. These were not marginal spaces—they were the scaffolding that built audience loyalty across genres and price points. Without them, the industry is a two-tier market: stadium-sized blockbusters with dynamic pricing algorithms and algorithmic yield management, and streaming alternatives at $15 per month. Movie theaters show the collapse most starkly. The industry recovered to $8.6–8.8 billion in 2025 but remains 23–27% below the 2019 pre-pandemic peak. Attendance dropped 34% (from 1.225 billion in 2019 to 780 million in 2025), but ticket prices rose 5.7% in 2025 to offset volume loss. AMC shuttered 200+ theaters; iPic, LOOK, and CMX filed Chapter 11. The survivors—Cinemark and Regal—were the low-leverage operators that could absorb years of margin compression. What remains is event-driven (Inside Out 2, Deadpool & Wolverine) versus everything else fails. This is not a recovery; it is a bifurcation with funeral pyres. The drivers are economic and structural. Labor costs consume 35% of operating expenses in amusement parks, with wage inflation stuck at 2.9% annually. Tariffs on imported ride systems and equipment push capital costs higher. Streaming made casual moviegoing obsolete—1.1 billion SVOD subscribers are now permanent (2x pre-pandemic), and studios have committed to 45-day theatrical windows or longer, forcing a détente that disciplines box office while protecting streaming. Yet all three industries are caught in the same squeeze: nominal growth masks a hollowing-out of the middle. Pent-up demand and willingness to pay fund the premium. Everyone else is forced to compete on price or disappear.

The evidence

  • Amusement Parks: Disney's peak-day Disneyland tickets reached $224 (October 2025) while offering deepest discounts in years; Universal's Epic Universe projected 9.2M annual visitors by 2026 (50% attendance boost to Orlando resort), while Cedar Fair-Six Flags faces $5.5B debt and Q2 2025 attendance down 1.4M.
  • Concert & Event Promotion: Live Nation $25.2B revenue in 2025 (9% YoY growth) with ticket prices averaging $144 (up 45% since 2019); April 2026 antitrust verdict found monopolistic ticketing practices (86% market share) while iconic venues like Rockwood Music Hall (Nov 2024) and Avant Gardner (bankruptcy) collapse.
  • Movie Theaters: Industry revenue $8.6–8.8B in 2025 but attendance down 34% (780M vs 1.225B in 2019); ticket prices up 5.7% in 2025 offsetting volume loss; AMC closed 200+ theaters, iPic/LOOK/CMX filed Chapter 11, survivors like Cinemark (18.6% EBITDA margin, 2025) thrive on consolidation.
  • US Streaming: 1.1 billion SVOD subscribers now permanent (2x pre-pandemic), forcing theatrical window détente; all major studios committed to 45-day exclusivity or longer (CinemaCon 2026) to protect downstream revenue.
  • Amusement Parks Labor: 35% of operating expenses consumed by labor; wage inflation sticky at 2.9% annually; tariff-driven equipment costs mounting pressure on regional operators with thin margins.

Who rides it

Premium operators and blockbuster experiences are printing money. Disney raised ticket prices to $224 while maintaining traffic through selective discounting. Live Nation's 2025 revenue hit $25.2 billion despite (or because of) $144 average ticket prices and willingness to pay soaring 45% since 2019. Universal's Epic Universe is cannibalizing regional park attendance but succeeding because it is genuinely new—five themed worlds that justify the premium. AMC strengthened cash to $778 million by Q2 2026; Cinemark reported 18.6% EBITDA margins in 2025 after paying down $700 million in COVID debt. The winners are the operators with scale, differentiation, or low leverage to absorb margin compression. They can afford tariffs, labor inflation, and intermittent closure (hurricanes Helene and Milton cost Disney $120 million in October 2024). They can invest in new spectacles. They own the consumer's discretionary moment.

Who's squeezed

Mid-tier and independent operators are being crushed. Cedar Fair-Six Flags, the second-largest regional park operator, carries $5.5 billion in debt and lost 1.4 million visitors in Q2 2025. Regional amusement parks lack the marketing capital or innovation velocity to compete with Disney and Universal. Independent concert venues are disappearing: Rockwood Music Hall closed in November 2024; The Music Hall of Williamsburg faces eviction in 2026; Avant Gardner filed bankruptcy owing $155 million. These venues once built audience loyalty across genres and price points. Now the industry is a two-tier market: stadium-sized blockbusters with dynamic pricing and streaming alternatives. Movie theater chains that were not Cinemark or Regal got eliminated. iPic, LOOK, CMX filed Chapter 11. AMC shuttered 200+ locations. The mid-market theater is now defined by event-driven movies (Inside Out 2, Deadpool & Wolverine) and nothing else. Casual moviegoing is dead. Labor costs (35% of operating expenses) and tariff-driven equipment expenses make it impossible for a mid-tier operator to compete on both experience and price.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that pent-up demand is not sustainable and that price elasticity is finally breaking. Disney's two-price strategy (peak $224 tickets plus deepest discounts in years) is a visible fracture. Concert ticket prices are up 45% since 2019, but 2025 data shows 36% of consumers willing to pay $100–$499 and 17% willing to spend $1,000—this is still early-adopter willingness, not mass-market endurance. If economic conditions soften (recession, job loss, credit card debt burden rising), the premium consumer will retrench, and there will be no mid-tier to catch them. The industry has eliminated the ladder. Labor costs will not deflate, tariffs may persist or climb, and capital-intensive attractions (new themed worlds, state-of-the-art theaters) will only remain economically viable at the largest scales. Climate risk is also invisible: hurricanes Helene and Milton cost Disney $120 million in October 2024. Centralized destination parks (Florida, Orlando) carry concentration risk.

In one sentence

Paying for the experience in a nutshell

Experiential spending splits into premium blockbusters and budget value, squeezing mid-market operators between pent-up demand and sticky costs.
