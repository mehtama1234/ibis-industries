Fuels & Petrochemicals

Wholesalers trapped between collapsed crude prices and razor-thin margins.

Gasoline and petroleum wholesale is a pass-through business: buy crude, sell refined products to retailers, keep a slice. But when crude prices whip around—from $113 a barrel in mid-2026 after Middle East tensions to an EIA-forecast $66 in the year ahead—wholesalers eat the gap because they price with a lag and have zero negotiating power. The industry is now in structural decline, squeezed by EVs and consolidating fast.

What changed

Crude oil volatility used to be absorbed by refinery networks and regional fuel chains. In 2025-2026, it has become the industry's throttle. Brent crude fell from $81 a barrel in 2024 to a forecast $66 in 2026, but geopolitical risk premiums pushed it to $90 in some windows and $113 in March 2026 when Middle East tensions spiked. Wholesalers cannot hold the risk. They buy crude, sell products to gas stations and retailers within days or weeks, and pass through the commodity move with a built-in lag—sometimes a week, sometimes longer. That lag is their death: crude falls, they are stuck holding product bought at yesterday's price. Margins in the whole wholesaling chain are 1.7% and have stayed stable, which means there is no room to absorb shocks. The industry is mature and shrinking. Gasoline wholesaling in the US was worth $798.6 billion in 2022 and fell to $791.9 billion in 2026, a decline masked by price but real in volume. Growth is negative: -2.8% in 2026 alone. The structural reason is clear: EVs and better fuel economy are eating gasoline demand. Yes, EV tax credits expired in September 2025 ($7,500 for new cars, $4,000 for used), which caused a 45% drop in EV sales that quarter and pushed people back to gas cars. But that is temporary. The long-term math is brutal. To survive, the industry is consolidating. Thirty-five deals closed in 2025, up 25% from 2024. The flagship move was Sunoco's acquisition of Parkland in October 2025 for $9.1 billion, creating the largest independent fuel distributor in the Americas. Parkland brought a refinery, extensive fuel logistics, and US convenience stores. That is not a bet on fuel; that is a bet on scale and the ability to absorb margin compression through volume and vertical integration. Private equity and mid-cap buyers are pushing consolidation because major oil majors systematically exited wholesaling decades ago to focus on higher-margin refining. Now regional distributors and smaller players fill geographic pockets but are outmatched on cost and reach. Arko Petroleum bought U.S. Petroleum Partners, a Great Lakes distributor, in 2026 for roughly $205 million—the kind of bolt-on that used to happen quietly but now signals an existential race to get bigger or die. The workforce is aging and stable. The industry had 49,499 employees in 2022 and is projected to grow just 1.0% annually, meaning consolidation is not adding jobs. Wholesalers are doing more with fewer people—automation at distribution centers, better logistics software, tighter inventory turns. For employees, that means wages hold but job growth stalls.

The evidence

  • Gasoline & Petroleum Wholesaling: $791.9 billion market size (2026), -2.8% growth, 1.7% margin—the industry is hollowed out and consolidating.
  • Fuel wholesalers: Brent crude volatility from $81 (2024) to $66 (forecast 2026) to $113 (mid-2026 spike) forces daily pricing recalculations; pass-through lag leaves them absorbing losses.
  • Consolidation wave: 35 M&A deals in 2025 (up 25% from 2024); Sunoco paid $9.1 billion for Parkland in October 2025 to scale cost and absorb margin pressure.
  • EV tax-credit expiration: September 2025 credits ended, causing Q4 2025 EV sales to drop 45% YoY and extending gasoline demand but confirming structural decline into the 2030s.
  • Regional fuel distributors: Arko Petroleum bought U.S. Petroleum Partners (Great Lakes) for ~$205 million in 2026; small players consolidate or exit.

Who rides it

Consolidated fuel distributors like Sunoco and Arko can spread margin loss over higher volume and absorb crude swings via vertical integration (owning refinery, storage, logistics). Private equity buyers and large independent operators are the only winners because they can finance the M&A and operate at scale. They pass through cost shock faster because they control more of the supply chain. Retailers who buy in bulk from consolidated suppliers pay less per gallon but lose negotiating power on terms.

Who's squeezed

Regional fuel distributors that stayed independent are squeezed to nothing: they buy at spot, sell at margin, and have no volume cushion when crude spikes. Small convenience store chains that buy direct are hit hardest because they pay retail prices for fuel and compete on convenience, not price. Employment growth is flat (1.0% annually) even as consolidation happens—wholesalers are replacing labor with logistics software and automation, so workers see wage stagnation, not raises. Drivers benefit short-term from falling crude but will pay more as consolidation hands market power to big distributors.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Geopolitical shocks can spike crude above $113 overnight, and wholesalers have zero buffer. A refinery outage, a port closure, or a sudden tariff on refined products could turn a 1.7% margin into a 2% loss across a distributor's entire portfolio before they can reprice. Consolidation means fewer, larger firms—if one major distributor falters under a shock, supply chains break and regional fuel shortages follow. The industry is also betting that EVs slow down, but Trump-era tax-credit expiration is a temporary reprieve; long-term demand is falling, and wholesalers are consolidating into a shrinking market.
