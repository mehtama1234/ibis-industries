Commodity Whiplash

The extraction economy runs on violent price cycles set by geopolitics and weather — hedgers and consolidators win, small single-commodity players get buried.

Mining, drilling, and farming are no longer businesses of steady margins and patient capital. Geopolitical shocks, tariff whiplash, and supply disruptions from weather and conflict compress the extraction economy into feast-or-famine cycles that split winners from losers in real time. The US still runs on what it digs, pumps, and grows — but only the consolidated, diversified players with hedging tools and capital reserves can survive the swings.

What changed

The violent price swings in gold, fertilizer, and farm commodities have made three things newly scarce, cheap, and risky in the US extraction economy over the past 18 months. First, stable margins are gone. Gold ore mining saw prices surge 65% in 2025 and hit $5,200 per ounce in 2026—a 144% rally from 2022 lows—yet miners face all-in sustaining costs above $1,500/oz due to fuel, labor, and equipment inflation, squeezing profit margins despite headline prices. Fertilizer makers had a sharper whip: urea jumped from $389/ton in early 2025 to $450/ton by early 2026, while diammonium phosphate soared 36% in eight months to nearly $800/ton, and anhydrous ammonia spiked nearly $300/ton between February and April 2026. CF Industries' 2025 revenue jumped 22.8% to $7.08 billion with net earnings of $1.46 billion, and the company expects 84% earnings growth in 2026—but the DOJ opened a price-fixing investigation in March 2026, and those soaring prices are crushing farm profitability. Agribusiness commodity prices actually collapsed the other direction: corn trades at $4.20 to $4.62 per bushel and soybeans at $10.71 to $11.09 in 2026, insufficient for mid-size farmers to cover debt service.

Second, geopolitics and policy have become the binding constraint, not geology. The April 2025 US reciprocal tariff regime (10% baseline, 20% on EU, up to 32% on Asia) disrupted soybean and grain export flows overnight, forcing American exporters to lose market share to South America even as China committed to buying 25 million metric tons of US soybeans annually through 2028. Tariff uncertainty on nitrogen and phosphate was lifted after seven months, but it kept traders nervous and importers hedged. The Iran conflict disrupted Strait of Hormuz shipping in early 2026, taking 50% of traded sulfur and 36% of traded urea offline. China extended phosphate export restrictions through August 2026. Russia continues quota limits on outbound fertilizer. These shocks ripple through pricing in weeks, not quarters. Gold mining felt it too—trade policy became the single largest risk factor for the industry's investment decisions by mid-2025, as tariff uncertainty and stagflation fears reshaped where new capital goes.

Third, consolidation and capital concentration are the survival strategy—and that's hollowing out the middle. In gold mining, the Nevada Gold Mines joint venture between Newmont and Barrick collapsed into a formal Notice of Default in April 2026, with Barrick spinning off NGM as an IPO candidate by Q4 2026. Meanwhile, Coeur Mining announced a $7 billion all-stock acquisition of New Gold in early 2026, transitioning to 'senior producer' status with a $20 billion market cap target. In agribusiness, large multinationals (Tyson, JBS, Cargill, Smithfield) already control 70% of meat processing and are building $1 billion-plus automated facilities. In farming itself, Chapter 12 bankruptcies rose 46% in 2025, operating loans are 30% larger than a year ago, and interest expenses are forecast to hit $33 billion in 2026. Mid-size family farms are being forced into contract production for stability or consolidation. Independent ag dealerships are disappearing (30% reduction expected). Meanwhile, agtech deal volume surged 19% in Q1 2025 (25 transactions), with precision agriculture deals leading—but large operations are racing into autonomous equipment, AI crop monitoring, and precision systems (agtech raised $7+ billion in 2025), while small farms cannot amortize the capital cost. The bifurcation is structural and accelerating.

The evidence

  • Fertilizer manufacturing (Manufacturing): CF Industries 2025 revenue $7.08 billion, up 22.8% year-over-year, with 84% earnings growth expected in 2026; urea prices jumped from $389/ton to $450/ton, and anhydrous ammonia spiked nearly $300/ton between February and April 2026.
  • Agribusiness in the US (Agriculture): $2.9 trillion global agribusiness with 0.9% US domestic growth forecast; Chapter 12 bankruptcies rose 46% in 2025, operating loans 30% larger than a year ago, with farm interest expenses forecast to hit $33 billion in 2026.
  • Tariff shock (April 2025): US reciprocal tariff regime (10% baseline, 20% EU, up to 32% Asia) disrupted soybean and grain export flows; anhydrous ammonia tariffs kept traders hedged; China extended phosphate export restrictions through August 2026.
  • Gold mining consolidation (2026): Coeur Mining $7 billion acquisition of New Gold announced in early 2026; Nevada Gold Mines joint venture collapsed with formal Notice of Default from Newmont to Barrick; Barrick spinning off NGM as IPO candidate.

Who rides it

The big winners are consolidated commodity producers with hedging tools and diversified portfolios. CF Industries and Nutrien printed money on fertilizer spikes—CF expects 84% earnings growth in 2026 off 22.8% revenue gains in 2025. Coeur Mining is buying New Gold to build a $20 billion market cap senior producer that can absorb price swings. Large agribusiness firms (Tyson, JBS, Cargill, Smithfield) controlling 70% of meat processing are building $1 billion automated facilities to lock in margins through efficiency, not gambling on commodity prices. Gold miners with low all-in costs and operational leverage to tariff realities—those with central bank demand hedges and ETF flows—are sitting on $3,200+ per ounce margins in 2026 even as costs surpass $1,500/oz.

Who's squeezed

Mid-size and single-commodity players are getting squeezed into extinction. Farm bankruptcies jumped 46% in 2025; Chapter 12 filings are accelerating as operating loans swell 30% larger and interest expenses balloon toward $33 billion in 2026. Commodity prices are hostile—corn at $4.20-$4.62/bushel and soybeans at $10.71-$11.09 in 2026 are too low for mid-size operators to cover debt without government help. Independent ag dealerships are vanishing (30% reduction expected). Family farms are forced into contract production or consolidation. In gold mining, joint ventures are collapsing (Newmont v. Barrick) as the small and mid-size players get absorbed. Agtech adoption is locked behind capital costs that only large operations can amortize—small farms fall further behind.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that geopolitical shocks and tariff policy have become the primary driver of profitability, not operational excellence. The April 2025 reciprocal tariff surge disrupted soybeans and grains overnight. The Iran conflict in early 2026 took 50% of traded sulfur and 36% of traded urea offline in weeks. China extended phosphate restrictions through August 2026. Russia continues quota limits. Trade policy, not mining geology or harvest yields, now sets the margin envelope—and policymakers have demonstrated they will use tariffs as a tool repeatedly with little warning. A consolidator hedged across geographies and commodities can absorb these shocks. A single-region gold miner or Midwest family farm cornered by tariff policy and debt cannot. If tariff uncertainty persists through 2027-2028, the rush to consolidation will accelerate, and mid-size players will face a binary choice: sell to a larger competitor or default.

In one sentence

Commodity whiplash in a nutshell

The US extraction economy is splitting into consolidated hedgers who absorb geopolitical whiplash and single-commodity players crushed by tariff uncertainty, commodity price volatility, and debt service they can no longer cover.
