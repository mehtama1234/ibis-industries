Mining the metals

Precious metals and industrial base minerals are caught between record prices and the hard reality that you can't dig faster than geology and permits allow.

Global metal prices have shattered decades of norms. Gold hit $5,200 per ounce in 2026 and silver jumped 144% in 2025. For US miners, the money has never been better—but they can't spend it fast enough to keep up with the world's hunger for metals, and the supply crunch is becoming the hard ceiling.

What changed

Gold and silver ore mining in the US grew 12.0% in 2025 alone, lifting the sector to $15.3 billion in market size, a jump of $4.1 billion from 2022's $11.2 billion baseline. Gold prices surged 65% in 2025 and continued climbing to $5,200 an ounce in 2026—a 144% rally from 2022. The trigger was a perfect economic storm: tariff uncertainty, central bank gold accumulation, and ETF inflows all collided at once. Miners printed money. All-in sustaining costs climbed above $1,500 per ounce in 2025 due to fuel, labor, and equipment inflation, but miners still enjoyed $3,200-plus per ounce margins in 2026. The problem is that money can't solve the real bottleneck: labor. Mining leaders report that 71% of their firms face talent shortages, and 220,000 workers are expected to retire by 2029. A five-year shortage of 27,000 skilled workers looms, while demand for tech-forward roles—AI, IoT, drones—vastly outpaces supply. The gold industry is fractured and unstable. Newmont issued a formal notice of default against Barrick Gold in April 2026 over the Nevada Gold Mines joint venture, alleging resource piracy and systematic mismanagement. Barrick is now spinning off Nevada Gold Mines as a standalone IPO candidate for Q4 2026. Meanwhile, Coeur Mining announced a $7 billion all-stock acquisition of New Gold in early 2026, aiming to become a senior producer with a $20 billion market cap.\n\nCopper, nickel, lead, and zinc mining face an even starker paradox: prices are record-high but supply is contracting. Copper futures hit $6.58 per pound in May 2026—a 40.86% gain over the prior 12 months—yet a 500,000-tonne supply shortfall was forecast for 2026. The culprit is electrification demand colliding with permitting gridlock. Electric vehicles use 2.9 times more copper than gas cars, and renewable energy infrastructure requires vast quantities of metal wiring and components. US copper ore grades are declining, and major expansion projects like Resolution Copper remain years away from production (mid-2030s target). The market size for US copper, nickel, lead, and zinc mining sits at $13.8 billion in 2026, actually down 12.7% from 2022's $15.8 billion baseline, despite higher prices—a sign that production volumes are falling even as revenues hold because of tariff protection and pricing power. Tariff policy arrived late but hard. On August 1, 2025, the Trump administration imposed a 50% tariff on semi-refined copper imports under Section 232, designed to protect domestic smelting. A proposed 15-30% broad copper import tariff is looming for 2027. These tariffs are meant to keep domestic smelting alive, but they can't accelerate ore permitting or reverse ore-grade decline. The sector saw global mining M&A surge to $93.7 billion in 2025 on consolidation hunger, but US base metals consolidation remains modest. Lundin Mining's exit from US nickel underscores the challenge: in December 2025, Lundin sold the Eagle nickel-copper mine in Michigan—the only primary US nickel producer—to Talon Metals for $84 million. Rio Tinto's December 2025 first copper production at Johnson Camp using proprietary low-carbon Nuton technology signals that innovation can chip at the problem, but permitting timelines remain the binding constraint.\n\nWhat the commodity whiplash has made scarce is time and productive capacity. Miners have money to invest but can't build mines faster than environmental and permitting approvals allow. Labor is the second scarcity: skilled workers command premium wages, and the pipeline of new workers is drying up as older workers retire. What it has made cheap is indecision—the high prices and tariff uncertainty force every mining firm to make permanent bets on capacity now. What it has made risky is long-term contracts: copper demand is real, but if the EV boom cools or tariff policy reverses, miners holding excess capacity face years of losses. What it has made newly valuable is innovation in mine technology and labor efficiency: low-carbon smelting, AI-driven ore grade prediction, remote automation, and drone-based surveying all offer a way to squeeze more production out of fewer workers and less ore."

The evidence

  • Gold ore mining: $15.3B market (2025), up from $11.2B (2022); gold surged $5,200/oz (2026) on central bank/ETF demand and tariff fears, 65% rise in 2025 alone
  • Copper/nickel/lead/zinc: $13.8B market (2026), down from $15.8B (2022); copper hit $6.58/lb (May 2026) but supply shortfall of 500,000 tonnes forecast; EVs use 2.9x more copper than gas cars
  • Labor crisis: 71% of mining leaders report talent shortages; 220,000 workers retiring by 2029; 27,000-worker shortage expected over five years; demand for AI/IoT/drone roles far outpacing supply
  • Tariff protection: 50% Section 232 tariff on semi-refined copper imports (August 1, 2025); proposed 15-30% broad copper tariff for 2027; designed to protect domestic smelting amid permitting delays
  • Joint venture crisis: Newmont defaults Barrick Gold (April 2026) over Nevada Gold Mines, alleging mismanagement; Barrick planning Q4 2026 spinoff; Coeur Mining acquiring New Gold ($7B, Jan-May 2026) to become 'senior producer'

Who rides it

Mining companies with precious metals exposure and large scale win hard in this environment. Gold miners printed $3,200-plus margins per ounce in 2026 even with inflation-driven costs. Consolidators like Coeur Mining, which acquired New Gold to reach senior producer status with a $20 billion market cap target, capture pricing power and labor efficiency gains. Tariff-protected US copper and nickel operators capture domestic pricing power, at least in the near term. Equipment suppliers and technology vendors focused on low-carbon mining, AI-driven ore prediction, and remote automation win as miners scramble to do more with fewer workers. Labor-intensive roles in mining—engineers, geologists, equipment operators—command premium wages."

Who's squeezed

Smaller, remote mining operations like Lundin's Eagle mine get squeezed out: Lundin sold for just $84 million in December 2025, unable to sustain smaller-scale nickel production. Fringe operations with high all-in costs and declining ore grades face margin pressure the moment prices cool. Downstream industrial users of copper, nickel, lead, and zinc—automotive parts suppliers, appliance makers, construction firms—face input cost inflation that tariffs only amplify. Emerging-market copper and nickel producers lose export volumes to tariff walls. Long-term mining workers planning retirement face a labor crunch that keeps wages high but jobs precarious once the commodity cycle turns."

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is permitting gridlock meeting speculative collapse. Miners are investing billions on the assumption that copper demand from EVs and renewables will stay at 2026 levels. But if EV adoption falters, tariff policy reverses, or a recession dampens electrification spending, they'll be left holding excess capacity with multi-year lead times to scale back. The Nevada Gold Mines joint venture crisis—Newmont's default notice and Barrick's planned spinoff—signals that even industry giants are fragile when commodity cycles turn. Miners are also trapped in a labor-cost spiral: they can't solve the worker shortage by offering higher wages indefinitely, because wage growth outpacing productivity gains erodes margins the moment prices fall. Tariff protection is temporary and conditional; once tariffs are unwound or EV demand cools, the supply glut will be sudden and devastating."

In one sentence

Mining the metals in a nutshell

Metal prices are at historic highs and miners are printing cash, but they can't dig faster than permits allow, can't hire workers who don't exist, and are betting everything on electrification demand that could evaporate overnight.
