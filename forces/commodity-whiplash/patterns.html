The patterns

Mining, drilling, and growing move on violent price cycles set by geopolitics and weather — the extraction economy the country forgot it still runs on.

The violent price swings in gold, fertilizer, and farm commodities have made three things newly scarce, cheap, and risky in the US extraction economy over the past 18 months. First, stable margins are gone. Gold ore mining saw prices surge 65% in 2025 and hit $5,200 per ounce in 2026—a 144% rally from 2022 lows—yet miners face all-in sustaining costs above $1,500/oz due to fuel, labor, and equipment inflation, squeezing profit margins despite headline prices. Fertilizer makers had a sharper whip: urea jumped from $389/ton in early 2025 to $450/ton by early 2026, while diammonium phosphate soared 36% in eight months to nearly $800/ton, and anhydrous ammonia spiked nearly $300/ton between February and April 2026. CF Industries' 2025 revenue jumped 22.8% to $7.08 billion with net earnings of $1.46 billion, and the company expects 84% earnings growth in 2026—but the DOJ opened a price-fixing investigation in March 2026, and those soaring prices are crushing farm profitability. Agribusiness commodity prices actually collapsed the other direction: corn trades at $4.20 to $4.62 per bushel and soybeans at $10.71 to $11.09 in 2026, insufficient for mid-size farmers to cover debt service.

The through-line

The US extraction economy is splitting into consolidated hedgers who absorb geopolitical whiplash and single-commodity players crushed by tariff uncertainty, commodity price volatility, and debt service they can no longer cover.

Who wins across the board

The big winners are consolidated commodity producers with hedging tools and diversified portfolios. CF Industries and Nutrien printed money on fertilizer spikes—CF expects 84% earnings growth in 2026 off 22.8% revenue gains in 2025. Coeur Mining is buying New Gold to build a $20 billion market cap senior producer that can absorb price swings. Large agribusiness firms (Tyson, JBS, Cargill, Smithfield) controlling 70% of meat processing are building $1 billion automated facilities to lock in margins through efficiency, not gambling on commodity prices. Gold miners with low all-in costs and operational leverage to tariff realities—those with central bank demand hedges and ETF flows—are sitting on $3,200+ per ounce margins in 2026 even as costs surpass $1,500/oz.

Who's squeezed across the board

Mid-size and single-commodity players are getting squeezed into extinction. Farm bankruptcies jumped 46% in 2025; Chapter 12 filings are accelerating as operating loans swell 30% larger and interest expenses balloon toward $33 billion in 2026. Commodity prices are hostile—corn at $4.20-$4.62/bushel and soybeans at $10.71-$11.09 in 2026 are too low for mid-size operators to cover debt without government help. Independent ag dealerships are vanishing (30% reduction expected). Family farms are forced into contract production or consolidation. In gold mining, joint ventures are collapsing (Newmont v. Barrick) as the small and mid-size players get absorbed. Agtech adoption is locked behind capital costs that only large operations can amortize—small farms fall further behind.

The biggest tension

The one to watch

The single biggest risk is that geopolitical shocks and tariff policy have become the primary driver of profitability, not operational excellence. The April 2025 reciprocal tariff surge disrupted soybeans and grains overnight. The Iran conflict in early 2026 took 50% of traded sulfur and 36% of traded urea offline in weeks. China extended phosphate restrictions through August 2026. Russia continues quota limits. Trade policy, not mining geology or harvest yields, now sets the margin envelope—and policymakers have demonstrated they will use tariffs as a tool repeatedly with little warning. A consolidator hedged across geographies and commodities can absorb these shocks. A single-region gold miner or Midwest family farm cornered by tariff policy and debt cannot. If tariff uncertainty persists through 2027-2028, the rush to consolidation will accelerate, and mid-size players will face a binary choice: sell to a larger competitor or default.
