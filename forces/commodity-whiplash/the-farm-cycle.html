The Farm Price Cycle

Weather, tariffs, and commodity crashes leave mid-size farms crushed while processors race to cut costs.

American farmers are caught between two forces they cannot control: global commodity prices that have crashed to six-year lows, and tariff shocks that keep erasing their biggest export markets overnight. The country still depends on extraction—food is the extraction—but nobody planned for the debt load when prices collapsed.

What changed

The wheat sector shows the clearest whiplash. In 2022, drought was distant news and prices spiked to $219 per ton after Ukraine. By early 2026, wheat prices crashed to $5.00 to $5.12 per bushel—a six-year low. Then came the weather blow: production fell 29 percent from 2025 to 2026 (from 1.402 billion to 990 million bushels), driven by the worst Plains drought since records began. Kansas yields collapsed to 37 bushels per acre, down from 51 the year before. But low prices never recovered; farm income collapsed instead, falling from $11.2 billion in 2024 to $9.5 billion by 2026. That is real money leaving the sector. Tariffs added insult. China imposed 44-49 percent tariffs on US sorghum and wheat in April 2025, and sorghum exports to China fell 97 percent by July. A November 2025 deal paused some tariffs, but volumes never came back.

Soybean processing got hit differently—by abundance. The renewable diesel boom created nearly unlimited demand for soybean oil, and the industry responded with a 23 percent capacity expansion over three years, investing over $6 billion in new crush facilities. Four major plants came online in North Dakota, Kansas, and elsewhere in 2024-2025, and ADM announced upgrades to four more crush plants in August 2026 unlocking 700,000 metric tons of additional annual capacity. Crush volumes hit a record 2.54 billion bushels forecast for 2025-26. But flooding the market with soybean meal (the byproduct of oil extraction) destroyed margins. Crush margins fell from $2 to $3 per bushel in 2023 to $1.10 to $1.50 per bushel in 2025 and stayed compressed at $1.10 to $1.50 even in August 2026 despite record volumes. That is the whiplash: investment chasing a mandate, capacity flooding the market, and profits evaporating. Soybean prices themselves swung violently—up to $12.30 per bushel in early 2024, down to $9.55 in mid-August 2024, recovering to $10.30 by end-of-2025, then sitting around $11.60 in August 2026—but low enough that soybean farmers still cannot cover debt.

The debt bomb is the third force. Operating loans jumped 40 percent in Q4 2025 versus Q4 2024, and average loan sizes grew 30 percent. Interest expenses hit $33 billion in 2026, a structural strain. Chapter 12 farm bankruptcies rose 46 percent in 2025. Commodity prices—corn at $4.20-$4.62, soybeans at $10.71-$11.09—are too low to service debt without federal support. The sector has split: well-capitalized operators are racing into autonomous equipment, AI crop monitoring, and precision agriculture (agtech raised $7 billion in 2025), while mid-size family farms cannot amortize the capital cost and are being squeezed toward contract production with large processors. Consolidation is accelerating. Meat processors (Smithfield, JBS) are building new $1 billion facilities with automation. Independent ag dealerships face a 30 percent reduction. The margin compression is structural: the sector grew just 0.9 percent in 2026 forecast versus a 2021 baseline, and average industry margins fell from 3.6 percent to 3.6 percent as farm-gate margins collapsed under input costs.

The evidence

  • Wheat farming: Production crashed 29 percent from 1.402B bushels (2025) to 990M (2026); prices fell to 6-year low of $5.00-$5.12/bu in early 2026 (2026).
  • Soybean processing: Crush margins collapsed from $2-$3/bushel in 2023 to $1.10-$1.50/bushel in 2025 despite record 2.54 billion bushel volumes (2025-26).
  • Farm debt crisis: Chapter 12 bankruptcies rose 46 percent in 2025; operating loans jumped 40 percent Q4 2025 vs Q4 2024 (2025-26).
  • Tariff shock: China imposed 44-49 percent tariffs on sorghum and wheat in April 2025, collapsing sorghum exports to China 97 percent by July 2025 (2025).
  • Technology divide: Agtech raised $7 billion in 2025 and deal volume surged 19 percent, but mid-size farms cannot amortize capital costs (2025).

Who rides it

Large, well-capitalized operators are consolidating control. Meat processors (Smithfield, JBS, Cargill, Tyson) control 70 percent of processing and are building new $1 billion automation-heavy facilities. Mid-size farms that can afford automation and precision agriculture—autonomous equipment, AI crop monitoring, $7 billion in agtech investment in 2025—are pulling ahead. Contract production with these giants offers the stability that commodity markets no longer provide. Technology companies racing into agtech (25 deals in Q1 2025) are capturing the capital flight. Lenders are also winning in the near term: operating loan volumes are up 40 percent and growing, which means more fee income and more collateral, even if borrowers are riskier.

Who's squeezed

Mid-size family farms are crushed. They carry 30 percent larger operating loans than a year ago with interest expenses forecast to hit $33 billion in 2026. They cannot afford the $7 billion worth of agtech that large operators are buying. They cannot absorb a 46 percent jump in Chapter 12 bankruptcies that hit farms like them. Corn at $4.20-$4.62 and soybeans at $10.71-$11.09 do not cover debt service without federal subsidy. Tariff shocks wipe out export markets overnight (sorghum exports to China down 97 percent). Independent ag dealerships (facing 30 percent reduction) and small processors have margin floors that are falling through the floor as renewable diesel mandates flood the market with cheap meal and crush spreads compress from $2-$3 to $1.10-$1.50 per bushel. The sector is structurally splitting: growth is 0.9 percent forecast (2026), and smaller players are being moved to contract production for survival.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The debt trap is the single biggest risk. Farm debt stress is acute and accelerating with operating loans 30 percent larger than a year ago and interest expenses forecast to hit $33 billion in 2026. Commodity prices are too low to cover debt service: corn at $4.20-$4.62 and soybeans at $10.71-$11.09 leave no margin for error. If commodity prices stay flat or fall further—driven by global oversupply (wheat ending stocks at 6-year highs, soybean crush flooding the market with meal) or another tariff shock like the China sorghum collapse (down 97 percent in months)—the sector could see a cascade of bankruptcies that destroys family farms and consolidates the entire value chain into a handful of well-capitalized corporations. Federal subsidies, the traditional safety net, face congressional pressure for reductions, leaving debt-crushed mid-size farmers with no cushion.

In one sentence

The farm price cycle in a nutshell

Farm debt exploded, commodity prices crashed, and tariffs erased export markets overnight, leaving mid-size farmers drowning in loan payments while mega-processors automate and consolidate.
