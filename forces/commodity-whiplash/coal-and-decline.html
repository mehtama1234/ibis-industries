Coal & the Managed Decline

Thermal coal is obsolete, but metallurgical coal's geopolitical whipsaw keeps miners trapped between zombie plants and tariff roulette.

US coal mining is smaller and grayer every year, but it still moves in violent bursts. A geopolitical spike can push metallurgical coal to $240 a ton in May, then crush it back to $128 by July. The industry forgot what it became: not a fuel supplier, but a bracket-game player betting on Asian infrastructure spending and American political will to keep coal plants running past their natural death.

What changed

Coal made electricity cheaper in 1950. By 2026, it is being made scarce in the grid by the same force that made renewables and natural gas cheap: the commodity market's brutal math. Thermal coal—the stuff that actually fuels power plants—has been systematically replaced. It fell to 19.8% of the US electricity mix by 2020 and keeps shrinking. In 2025, only 2.6 GW of coal capacity was retired, the lowest number in 15 years, but that was because the Trump administration forced delays on retirements that had been planned all along. Another 6.4 GW of coal plants are scheduled to close in 2026. The coal industry did not stop this. It cannot stop this. Thermal coal is not making a comeback.\n\nBut something else got valuable: metallurgical coal—the kind used for steelmaking, not electricity. It rides on infrastructure spending in India and Southeast Asia, and when geopolitical shock hits (an Iran conflict spiking tensions in May 2026, for example), coking coal shot up to $240 per ton. That looks like a win until you watch it fall back to $128 by July. That is not volatility; that is whipsaw. That is the difference between a quarter where the largest coal miners post earnings beats and a quarter where smaller operators announce losses. The problem is that margin has gotten razor-thin. The takeaway from producers: narrower than it was, riskier than it was, and entirely hostage to geopolitical shock and tariff moves by China. US coal exports fell 16 million short tons in 2025 after Beijing imposed retaliatory tariffs. That single policy move cut off one of the few remaining escape valves for US coal.\n\nProduction costs are rising across the board—equipment is wearing out, wages are creeping up, and smaller mines that made sense at $150 per ton do not make sense at $120. This squeeze went on for years. From 2023 to 2025, smaller miners were operating at losses or thin margins. They lasted until the mid-2026 price spike, but that relief was temporary. The industry now moves on a pendulum: a few months of high prices keep people employed, then the prices collapse and the marginal operators vanish. It is not a market in equilibrium. It is a market under managed decline.\n\nThe real winner in this setup is the largest producer that can survive multiple price cycles: Arch Resources and CONSOL Energy merged in January 2025, forming Core Natural Resources. Consolidation matters when margins are thin because it lets you close the smallest, least efficient mines during downturns and run only the most competitive ones. Smaller, regional producers have no such lever. They got trapped between a thermal coal market that is shrinking on purpose (utilities know coal plants are closing and are not investing in new ones) and a metallurgical coal market that spikes and crashes on geopolitical noise. The workforce has been hollowed out: 34,900 coal miners as of 2026, down from 38,000 in 2025, and down more than 50% since 2011. Every year, fewer people work in this industry, and fewer still have the skills to stay if prices hold.

The evidence

  • Energy generation: thermal coal fell from dominant fuel to 19.8% of US electricity mix by 2020 and continues shrinking despite 2.6 GW retirements in 2025 (lowest in 15 years).
  • Metallurgical coal exports: US coal exports fell 16 million short tons in 2025 after China imposed retaliatory tariffs, blocking the escape valve for marginal producers.
  • Geopolitical price whip: coking coal spiked to $240/ton in May 2026 after Iran conflict, then collapsed to $128-130/ton by July 2026, creating boom-bust cycles for smaller miners.
  • Workforce decline: coal mining employment fell to 34,900 in 2026 from 38,000 in 2025, continuing a 50%+ decline since 2011.
  • Consolidation: Arch Resources and CONSOL Energy merged in January 2025 to form Core Natural Resources, letting large players survive margin compression that smaller operators cannot weather.

Who rides it

Large, low-cost operators—now consolidated into Core Natural Resources after the Arch-CONSOL merger—can survive price cycles that kill smaller miners. They have the scale to shut down marginal mines during downturns and still report earnings. They can also afford to run at thin margins for quarters at a time, playing the geopolitical lottery for metallurgical coal exports to Asia. They are not growing. They are not investing. They are surviving. That is the win.

Who's squeezed

Regional and smaller coal producers are trapped. Thermal coal demand is declining on purpose (utilities know coal plants are being retired and are not betting on new ones). Metallurgical coal is the only growth story, but it is entirely at the mercy of geopolitical shocks and tariffs. When coking coal collapses from $240 to $128 a ton in two months, marginal operations tip into losses. They cannot consolidate their way out like the large producers can. They cannot retrain their workforce fast enough. They close or get acquired at distress prices. The workforce—34,900 coal miners in 2026, down from 50% more a decade ago—has no industry left to grow back into.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The entire coal industry is now betting on geopolitical shocks staying loud and US policy staying friendly to coal. In May 2026, an Iran conflict spiked coking coal and kept the lights on for marginal producers. But geopolitics is random. The Trump administration delayed coal plant retirements in 2025, but only 2.6 GW (the lowest in 15 years)—not because demand came back, but because the White House could order the delay. That is not a business. That is a subsidy disguised as energy policy. If tariffs change, if geopolitics quiets, if China buys less metallurgical coal, the price collapses again and nobody has prepared for it. The managed decline is managed only as long as the government is willing to manage. The moment it stops, the industry does not have a second act.

In one sentence

Coal & the managed decline in a nutshell

Coal miners now survive on geopolitical price spikes and government delays to retirements, not on the economics of coal itself.
