Fertilizer & the Inputs

Commodity price spikes and geopolitical supply shocks are printing money for fertilizer makers while crushing the farmers and grain traders who depend on them.

Fertilizer and grain markets are moving in violent swings set by factors nobody in Iowa or Illinois can control: China's phosphate export restrictions, Iran's hold on the Strait of Hormuz, Russia's quota games, and Trump's tariff reversals. The result is a bifurcated supply chain where a handful of mega-producers capture outsized profits while the rest of the agricultural economy gets squeezed.

What changed

Fertilizer prices exploded in early 2026 because the world's supply got deliberately constricted. Urea jumped from $389 a ton in early 2025 to $450 a ton by early 2026. Diammonium phosphate—the phosphate-based fertilizer every corn and soybean farmer needs—jumped 36% in eight months to nearly $800 a ton. Anhydrous ammonia, the nitrogen feedstock for half of North America's crops, climbed almost $300 a ton between February and April 2026 alone. These weren't market fluctuations. They were the result of three governments playing supply games. China extended phosphate export restrictions through August 2026, directly choking off one of the world's critical inputs. Iran's conflict disrupted traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, taking 50% of traded sulfur offline and 36% of traded urea—the loss cascaded through nitrogen, ammonia, phosphate, and potash prices. Russia kept quota limits on outbound fertilizer, holding back supply when the world needed volume. Add Trump's April 2025 tariffs on nitrogen and phosphate shipments (10-15%) that lasted seven months before being lifted, and traders never knew what the rules were.

Who made money? Fertilizer manufacturers printed cash. CF Industries pulled in $7.08 billion in revenue in 2025, up 22.8% year-over-year, with net earnings of $1.46 billion—and projected 84% earnings growth in 2026. Nutrien projected 26.5% earnings growth for 2026. The industry's overall market size grew to $42.2 billion in 2026, up 9.1% from the year before, with estimated margins sitting at around 6.9% (and likely higher during the price spike periods). The business count held relatively stable at 818 establishments and 598 enterprises, but consolidation via M&A was ongoing—the big three (CF, Nutrien, Mosaic) controlled the price signal. By March 2026, the DOJ opened a price-fixing investigation into all three, a tacit admission that commodity suppliers were capturing more value than competitive markets would allow.

But farmers and grain traders were not winning. The Corn, Wheat & Soybean Wholesaling market sat at $238.5 billion in 2026—that sounds big, except it was shrinking. The market declined 2.4% in 2025 alone, and the structural growth rate over five years was just 1.3% CAGR (2020-2025). Margins never exceeded 2.0-2.2% of revenue in any recent period, meaning a $238 billion industry was running on razors. Commodity prices in 2026 sat 15-37% below farmer breakeven costs: corn at $4.20 per bushel, soybeans at $10.30, wheat at $5.00. When input costs rose sharply and output prices fell, wholesalers couldn't pass losses upstream or downstream—they absorbed them. The business count dropped 0.4% annually since 2020, and while employment stayed flat around 55,000-58,000 workers (1.2% projected growth), the consolidation was real and not about efficiency—it was about survival.

The broader story is bifurcation. Bunge completed its $8.2 billion acquisition of Viterra in July 2025, creating the world's third-largest grain storage operator with 400 million bushels of capacity. But to get the deal approved, Bunge had to offload grain elevators—five went to small regional players, one went to Cargill in January 2026. That January move was telling: Cargill acquired P&H's GrainsConnect elevators in June 2026. The technology layer was also bifurcating. Bushel, a grain-logistics software platform, now powers 50%+ of US and Canadian grain facilities, and AI adoption is projected to reach 70% by 2026 in the sector—but those gains accrue almost entirely to the mega-operators who can afford the capital spend and integration complexity. Mid-market players lack the scale. China also shifted its buying behavior: when US tariffs hit 125% in April 2025, China pivoted to South American grain supplies, and US soybean exports fell 13% year-over-year in 2025-26. That bifurcation—US bloc versus China-anchored supplies—forced every wholesaler to choose sides or diversify partners in ways that squeezed their already-thin margins further.

The evidence

  • Fertilizer Manufacturing: CF Industries 2025 revenue $7.08 billion (up 22.8% YoY) with $1.46 billion net earnings and projected 84% earnings growth for 2026.
  • Fertilizer Manufacturing: Urea prices climbed from $389/ton (early 2025) to $450/ton (early 2026); diammonium phosphate rose 36% in eight months to nearly $800/ton; anhydrous ammonia jumped ~$300/ton February-April 2026.
  • Grain Wholesaling: Corn, Wheat & Soybean Wholesaling market at $238.5 billion (2026) but declined 2.4% in 2025; margins pinched at 2.0-2.2% EBIT while commodity prices sat 15-37% below farmer breakeven.
  • Grain Wholesaling: US soybean exports fell 13% year-over-year in 2025-26 after China shifted buying to South America in response to April 2025 tariff escalation (125% reciprocal tariffs).
  • Consolidation & Supply Shocks: Bunge completed $8.2 billion Viterra merger (July 2025); Iran conflict took 50% of traded sulfur and 36% of traded urea offline (Feb-April 2026); DOJ opened price-fixing investigation into CF, Nutrien, Mosaic (March 2026).

Who rides it

Fertilizer manufacturers—especially the big three (CF Industries, Nutrien, Mosaic)—are riding commodity price spikes and supply constraints to record profits. CF Industries posted $1.46 billion in earnings in 2025 and expects 84% growth in 2026. Nutrien projects 26.5% earnings growth for 2026. The consolidation also favors mega-grain operators like Bunge and Cargill, who can absorb technology costs (Bushel software, AI adoption at 70%) and negotiate terms across global blocs (US suppliers, South American pivots, China-anchored alternatives). Small regional players who snap up Bunge's divested elevators get volume, but not pricing power.

Who's squeezed

Farmers and grain wholesalers are squeezed in the middle. The Corn, Wheat & Soybean Wholesaling market declined 2.4% in 2025 and runs on 2.0-2.2% margins while commodity prices sit 15-37% below farmer breakeven costs. A farmer selling corn at $4.20 per bushel can't cover seed, fertilizer, fuel, and labor when inputs are spiking. Wholesalers can't pass losses upstream to farmers (they'd go bankrupt) or downstream to processors (who have alternatives). The business count has dropped 0.4% annually since 2020. Mid-market grain dealers lack the scale to afford Bushel software or AI integration, locking them out of the efficiency gains their large competitors enjoy. China's tariff retaliation (125% peak, partial ease to 10% by May 2025) also killed demand: US soybean exports fell 13% year-over-year in 2025-26.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is sustained bifurcation of the agricultural economy into a small number of mega-operators and a shrinking base of mid-market players. Fertilizer makers are under DOJ antitrust scrutiny (price-fixing investigation opened March 2026), which could cap their windfall—but only if enforcement is swift and aggressive. The real worry is that geopolitical constraints on fertilizer supplies (China restrictions, Iran conflict, Russia quotas) become structural, not cyclical. If key nutrients stay expensive and volatile for years, farmers can't plan. Some will exit production, consolidate, or shift to crops that need fewer inputs. The supply bifurcation (US bloc versus China-anchored blocs) also threatens pricing stability: if wholesalers are forced to choose supply partners by tariff regime rather than cost, entire regional grain markets could fragment. A farmer in Illinois selling to a wholesaler locked into South American suppliers faces a different economy than one selling to a US-anchored mega-operator.

In one sentence

Fertilizer & the inputs in a nutshell

Fertilizer makers are printing profits from constrained global supply while farmers and grain traders absorb losses because commodity prices have collapsed below breakeven costs.
