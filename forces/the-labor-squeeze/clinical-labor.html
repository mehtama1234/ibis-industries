Clinical labor everywhere

Care work runs short

Three healthcare subsectors—mental health clinics, obstetrics, and ambulatory surgery—are all facing the same wall: acute clinician shortages that no wage hike alone can solve. The market is consolidating upward fast, with hospitals and private equity vacuuming up independent practices. But even the big players are getting sandwiched: reimbursement growth is crawling (2.4–3.9% annually) while labor costs are sprinting, margins are compressing, and the shortage just keeps getting worse.

What changed

Mental health clinics have become the clearest window into clinical scarcity. The industry hit $42.0 billion in 2026, growing at 4.8% CAGR from 2021, but the workforce numbers tell the real story: 301,246 employees in 2021 are projected to swell to 365,228+ by 2026—a 21% jump in five years. Yet 43 of 44 US states report active behavioral health workforce shortages right now, and demographers project a deficit of 43,660 to 93,940 adult psychiatrists by 2037. The shortage is so acute that M&A activity exploded: 104 behavioral health deals closed in 2025, up from 73 in 2024, with mental health subsector leading at over 80 transactions. Consolidation is the play for scale—Syra Health and Nora Mental Health announced a partnership in April 2026 explicitly to "expand access," which is code for pooling clinician rosters and leveraging shared infrastructure. Insurance reimbursement is adding pressure: telehealth claim denials jumped 84% from 2024 to 2025, and in May 2025 the Biden administration refused to enforce new parity rules that would have required demonstrated mental health benefit parity in practice. Margins in the sector were already thin at 5.8% in 2021, constrained by wage growth outpacing revenue growth, and they've stayed compressed.

Obstetrics and gynecology reveals a different but equally brutal scarcity: geography and law. The OB-GYN market is a $17.2 billion industry in 2026, growing at only 3.9% that year and a measly 0.4% CAGR over 2021–2026, yet the supply side is cratering. Idaho shed 35% of its OB-GYN workforce between mid-2022 and end-2024. Residency applications to abortion-ban states declined 6.7% in 2024 while applications to abortion-legal states rose 0.4%—a shift that signals long-term supply loss in entire regions. Meanwhile, demand paradoxes are playing out: fertility rates hit an all-time low of 1.6 total births per woman in April 2026, with only 3.6 million births in 2025 (down 1% from 2024). Maternity caseloads are shrinking. Yet OB-GYNs are irreplaceable for contraceptive access, preventive gynecology, and emergency obstetric care, so demand for the service doesn't evaporate—it just shifts. The consolidation response is swift: 47% of physicians are now in hospital employment (2024), and private equity has acquired 65% of physician practices from 2019 to 2023. The American Medical Association retired decades-old bundled billing codes (the maternity bundle) effective January 1, 2027, forcing a shift to individual E/M and procedure codes—a billing restructuring that works better for large, integrated operations than solo or small-group practices.

Ambulatory surgery centers are the third example, and here the labor squeeze is colliding with margin compression and coverage expansion. The ASC market hit $46.5 billion in 2026, with 6.3% CAGR growth, but large consolidated platforms (like USPI) are harvesting 38.6% EBITDA margins while smaller independents are compressed into low-to-mid 30s. Reimbursement growth is a paltry 2.4–2.9% annually, a pace that can't outrun wage inflation and clinical staffing costs. Anesthesia locums use jumped 17% since 2024 and is projected to climb another 5% in 2026—a sign that ASCs can't find permanent anesthesiologists and are renting them at premium rates. Nursing shortages are equally acute as hospitals poach staff. The consolation prize is CMS's 2026 expansion of the ASC Covered Procedures List, which added 560 new procedures including cardiac ablations and lumbar fusion—high-acuity work that sounds lucrative until you realize these procedures carry thinner margins at ASCs than at hospital outpatient departments due to site-of-service payment differentials. Consolidation is the antidote: Ascension's $3.9 billion acquisition of AmSurg in 2024 expanded the health system's footprint from 58 to 300+ ASCs across 34 states, the largest health-system ASC transaction on record. That kind of scale buys you staffing leverage and the ability to absorb margin compression.

The evidence

  • Mental health clinics: 43,660–93,940 psychiatrist shortage projected by 2037 (2025 estimate), with 43 of 44 states reporting active workforce gaps; M&A accelerated to 104 deals in 2025
  • Obstetrics: Idaho shed 35% of OB-GYN workforce in two years (mid-2022 to end-2024); fertility rate hit 1.6 births per woman (April 2026); residency applications to abortion-ban states down 6.7% in 2024
  • Ambulatory Surgery Centers: Anesthesia locums use up 17% since 2024; reimbursement growth only 2.4–2.9% annually (2025–2026) against wage inflation; Ascension's $3.9B AmSurg acquisition (2024) creates 300+ center platform
  • Mental health telehealth: Claim denials jumped 84% from 2024 to 2025, adding to reimbursement pressure despite demand (buprenorphine prescriptions at 15.4 million in 2024)
  • Health system consolidation: 47% of physicians in hospital employment (2024); private equity acquired 65% of physician practices from 2019–2023; large ASC platforms harvest 38.6% EBITDA margins vs. low-to-mid 30s for independents

Who rides it

Big health systems and scaled private equity platforms. Ascension swallowed 300+ surgery centers in a single acquisition. Large ASC platforms lock in 38.6% EBITDA margins by absorbing labor cost inflation across a wide roster of sites. Consolidators can cross-post clinicians between facilities, build shared call infrastructure, and leverage scale to negotiate supply contracts and staffing rates. Mental health M&A frontrunners like Syra and Nora can pool clinical talent and integrate digital tools (Spring Health, Lyra Health) to try to do more with fewer psychiatrists. If you're independent or regional, you either get acquired or watch your margins shrink every quarter as labor costs accelerate and you can't negotiate reimbursement power alone.

Who's squeezed

Independent practices, regional operators, and small health systems. Mental health clinics with 5.8% margins can't absorb simultaneous wage growth and 84% telehealth denial spikes. OB-GYNs in low-supply states (especially abortion-ban states) are watching younger doctors leave and can't compete for hires against hospital-backed consolidators. Smaller ASCs with margins compressed into the low-to-mid 30s can't afford the locums anesthesiologists or premium nursing wages that big platforms can. The CPT billing restructuring effective January 1, 2027 (retirement of the maternity bundle) favors integrated billing systems over solo billers. Solo or small-group practices are being squeezed into acquisition or closure.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Consolidation doesn't solve the shortage; it just hoards the talent. When you have 43 of 44 states reporting workforce shortages and a 43,000–94,000-person psychiatrist deficit looming by 2037, buying up clinics doesn't create new clinicians. It just moves them into the bidders' portfolios. The second risk: if reimbursement growth (2.4–2.9% ASC, 3.9% OB-GYN, 4.8% mental health) continues to lag wage inflation (historically 3–4%, likely higher in healthcare), even consolidated platforms will face margin compression across the board. The third: insurance is actively fighting back (84% telehealth denial spike, parity rule refusal). If payers tighten reimbursement faster than consolidators can scale, you get a squeeze where labor stays expensive, reimbursement stays stingy, and consolidators can't arbitrage the gap anymore.

In one sentence

Clinical labor everywhere in a nutshell

Psychiatrists, OB-GYNs, and surgical nurses are vanishing faster than hiring can replace them, and whoever operates at scale wins while independent practices and regional clinics get squeezed.
