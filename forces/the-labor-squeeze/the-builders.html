A · The trades can't hire

The People Who Build Things

Construction trades can't fill job openings fast enough, so tariffs and wage spirals are forcing consolidation and automation.

The trades that frame houses, pour concrete, and lay brick face a problem that money alone won't fix: there simply aren't enough workers. Residential construction is weak, but the shortage isn't cyclical—it's structural. Contractors need people faster than they can hire them, wage growth is outpacing pricing power, and tariffs are eroding margins that were already thin. The result is a race to automate or get swallowed.

What changed

The labor shortage in construction has turned into a margin squeeze that rewards scale and punishes small operators. Masonry, the most specialized of the trades, saw revenue shrink 1.6% in 2025 despite a $40 billion market, pinched between residential weakness (6%+ mortgage rates locked out buyers) and tariff shocks on materials—Mexican ceramic masonry jumped to 25% duty, Italian stone hit 10%, and steel tools climbed 50%. Yet masonry employment is creeping up only 2–3% per decade, leaving the trade 20,700 workers short annually just to replace retirees. Carpenters face the same squeeze at a bigger scale: the $63.5 billion carpentry industry shrank in establishment count (down 0.9% annually since 2021) while 65% of builders still can't fill positions. Carpenter wages hit $60,104 in 2026, up from $59,310 in May 2024, but material costs climbed harder—lumber tariffs (10% as of October 2025) stacked on 35% prior Canadian duties, and the 3–4% profit margins already compressed by labor costs are being erased by tariff cascades. Concrete is the clearest winner of the demand reshuffling, reaching $110 billion in 2026 (up from $73.3 billion in 2022) on the back of infrastructure law spending and data center buildout, but even there, margins have compressed 3–5 percentage points in 18 months as ready-mix prices climbed to $179–$195 per yard and 94% of firms report open positions they can't fill.

The tariff shock is accelerating a structural shift. In masonry, robotic bricklayers (capable of placing hundreds of bricks per hour with precision) and prefabricated panels are being tested by larger contractors, though adoption remains capital-intensive and early. In carpentry, modular prefabrication is moving components off-site; on-site work is shifting to finishing, custom details, and site-specific challenges—reducing the demand for general carpenters and raising the bar for those who remain. Concrete contractors are adopting telematics and automation to cope with 94% reporting open craft positions, and consolidation is running hot: Suncrete acquired ready-mix producers in Louisiana (April 2026, $31M+) and Texas (May 2026); Quikrete inherited ready-mix assets from Summit Materials and announced a deal with Martin Marietta for Texas ready-mix and cement assets. In 2025 alone, masonry subcontractor M&A activity jumped 38.6% year-over-year (366 transactions), driven explicitly by labor shortages—larger firms are buying smaller masonry shops to acquire skilled crews faster than recruiting can build them.

The consolidation and automation wave is reshaping who wins and who exits. Large regional and national operators can absorb tariff costs, finance robotic systems, and pass through wage inflation to customers with long-term contracts. Small masonry shops (22,035 establishments declining 0.6% annually) and independent carpentry crews (195,000 establishments down 0.9% annually) face a choice: invest in prefab and automation capital they may not have cash for, accept margin compression, or sell to a roll-up. Residential builders locked in below-4% mortgages (67% of existing homes) have no incentive to move or build new, so the market is bifurcating: new construction (nonresidential, infrastructure, data centers) is where wages and material costs are rising fastest and where consolidated firms can afford to operate; renovation and maintenance of existing stock is where smaller operators cluster and where pricing power is lowest. Geographic arbitrage is also beginning—contractors are relocating to regions where labor markets are looser or where nonresidential work is heavier, abandoning saturated residential markets.

The evidence

  • Masonry (US, 2026): $40 billion market, 0.5% CAGR 2020–2025, 146,100 workers (2024), declining at 0.6% annually in establishment count; 2025 revenue shrank 1.6% amid residential weakness and 25% Mexico-sourced tariff shocks.
  • Carpentry (US, 2026): $63.5 billion market, 0.3% decline in 2026, 923,000 carpenters employed; establishment count down 0.9% annually since 2021 despite 65% of builders unable to fill positions.
  • Concrete (US, 2026): $110.1 billion market, 4.2% CAGR 2021–2026, 285,000+ workers, 90,964 establishments; margins compressed 3–5 points in 18 months as ready-mix costs climbed $179–$195/yard.
  • Tariffs (2025–2026): Mexican ceramic masonry 25%, Italian stone 10%, steel/aluminum 50%, copper 22% YoY increase, Canadian lumber stacked to 45% effective; cement tariffs from Canada/Mexico took effect 2025.
  • M&A (2025–2026): Masonry subcontractor consolidation at 38.6% YoY (366 deals); Suncrete acquired Louisiana and Texas ready-mix; Quikrete inherited and announced major cement/ready-mix deals.

Who rides it

Large regional and national construction firms with the capital to invest in automation (robotic bricklayers, prefab systems, telematics) and the customer base to absorb tariff passthrough. Concrete contractors tied to infrastructure law spending and data center buildout are growing revenue (up to $110 billion) and consolidating market share. Firms that acquire skilled labor via subcontractor M&A are gaining scale faster than they could recruit it. Material suppliers with pricing power (cement, ready-mix, steel) and access to tariff-advantaged supply chains are capturing margin gains that contractors are losing.

Who's squeezed

Small masonry shops (22,035 establishments declining 0.6% annually) and independent carpentry crews (195,000 establishments down 0.9% annually) facing wage inflation and tariff pressures with no scale to pass costs through. Residential builders and contractors locked into low-margin work (renovations, maintenance, infill) in saturated labor markets. Self-employed tradespeople and small crews competing for labor with consolidators who offer steady work and benefits. New entrants to the trades face longer training timelines and lower perceived job security as automation and prefab reduce the demand for generalist carpenter and masonry positions.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The wage-tariff spiral could outpace productivity gains from automation, keeping trades chronically understaffed and expensive. Residential construction at 1.18 million units (May 2026, down 15.4% month-over-month) could collapse further if interest rates don't fall—leaving only nonresidential and infrastructure work, which concentrates demand in regions and sectors where large firms dominate. Automation adoption is still early and capital-intensive; if smaller operators can't finance robotic systems or prefab infrastructure, the fragmentation between large and small grows into permanent structural inequality, leaving thin-margin work for whoever's left and luxury work for whoever pays up.

In one sentence

The people who build things in a nutshell

Construction trades face a structural labor shortage that tariffs and wage inflation are turning into a capital-intensive automation race, winnowing out small operators and consolidating the industry into fewer, larger firms.
