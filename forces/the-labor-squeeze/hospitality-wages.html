The hospitality wage spiral

Servers and housekeepers got expensive; chains either automate, shrink their crew, or go bankrupt.

Fast food used to run on rock-bottom labor. California's $20 minimum wage for chain restaurants (April 2024) broke that math. Labor costs jumped 25-39% since 2019. Hotels watched housekeeping wages spiral while running skeleton crews with 70-80% annual turnover. The industry is bifurcating: luxury boutiques and extended-stay properties winning; economy chains and casual dining closing or filing Chapter 11.

What changed

For forty years, hospitality's margin came from paying the lowest wage the law allowed. That stopped in 2024. California's Fast Food Accountability and Standards Act mandated $20/hour for chain restaurants with 60+ locations—a number that sent shockwaves across franchised QSR. Other states followed: Washington hit $16.66; Florida is heading to $15 by September 2026. For a fast-food operator, this wasn't a small bump; chains absorbed 25% of operating-cost increases in the first six months, forcing menu prices up 8-12%. The math broke further because traffic didn't hold. Fast-food visits dropped 0.3% in 2025, then accelerated to a 2% decline in Q4 2025 versus Q4 2024. GLP-1 appetite suppressants added a hidden guillotine: households with one member on Ozempic or Wegovy cut restaurant spending by 5.3% on average, and dinner spending at fast food specifically fell 6% among regular users.

Chain restaurants got hit differently but just as hard. Labor costs rose 39% since pre-pandemic 2019. Food inflation (tariffs + avian flu pressuring poultry and beef) sat 34% above pre-pandemic by 2025. When 62% of operators raised menu prices to cover it, 60% reported deteriorated conditions in 2025, and 42% reported unprofitability. The casualty list grew: TGI Fridays and Red Lobster both filed Chapter 11 in 2024 and shut 130+ locations each. FAT Brands (Smokey Bones) filed in January 2026 and closed all remaining locations. On The Border filed in March 2025; Bravo Brio filed again in August 2025 (this was the second bankruptcy for that chain in five years). Full-service casual dining—the $12-$20 entree category—got hollowed out. Interestingly, value-driven casual brands like Chili's and Texas Roadhouse thrive by offering $12 sit-down plates that undercut limited-service chains' $15 entrees, revealing that consumer defection isn't just about price but about *perceived value per dollar spent*.

Hotels faced a labor squeeze with no good exit. Wages rose 25.6% versus 2019 while occupancy and RevPAR barely budged. The industry's answer: skeleton crews with 70-80% annual turnover—which means burned-out staff, empty floors, and guests walking. Luxury boutiques escaped the squeeze: they hit 6% revenue gains in 2025, with average daily rates of $452 and RevPAR of $307 (luxury segment average $263). Extended-stay properties grew 4% in supply in 2025—the biggest increase in four years—because they matched the hybrid-work decade and pulled project-employment demand. Economy properties, conversely, contracted. The industry shifted from building new hotels to conversion projects, which let operators reopen tired properties faster with lower capital spend and, critically, lower staff needs. The shock came from bankruptcies: Revo Hospitality (260 properties, 8,800 employees) filed Chapter 11 in January 2026. Sonder—the AI-powered, Marriott-backed startup that was supposed to be the future—filed for Chapter 7 liquidation in November 2025 after failing to integrate with Marriott's systems post-acquisition (August 2024).

The irony is that automation was supposed to be hospitality's wage escape hatch. Chains invested billions in kiosks, kitchen displays, and reservation systems. Sonder bet that AI housekeeping and checkout would eliminate labor costs entirely. Instead, Sonder's assets got liquidated, and fast-food traffic kept falling. The real winners aren't automating—they're pricing up and filtering down. Luxury hotels raised rates and let price-sensitive guests evaporate. Extended-stay captured hybrid workers who value long-stay discounts over turnover. Value casual dining (Chili's, Texas Roadhouse) won share by offering the perception of better value. The real losers are economy chain restaurants, midscale hotels, and anyone betting that higher prices would stick without losing traffic. In 2026, traffic didn't come back. Wage floors set by law—California's $20, Washington's $16.66, Florida's rising $15—no longer allow for the margin structure that built this industry. Whoever can't automate, can't raise prices, or can't migrate upscale is exiting the game.

The evidence

  • Fast Food (2026): California's $20/hour minimum wage (April 2024) forced menu prices up 8-12% and traffic declined 2% in Q4 2025 versus 2024; GLP-1 adoption cut dinner spending 6% among users.
  • Chain Restaurants (2025): Labor costs rose 39% since 2019; 62% of operators raised prices; 42% unprofitable; TGI Fridays, Red Lobster, On The Border, FAT Brands all filed Chapter 11 in 2024-2026.
  • Hotels (2025): Wages rose 25.6% versus 2019; economy segment contracted while luxury hit 6% revenue gains and extended-stay grew 4% in supply; Sonder filed for liquidation; Revo Hospitality filed Chapter 11 (January 2026).
  • Casual Dining (2025): Value-driven brands (Chili's, Texas Roadhouse) thrive with $12 sit-down entrees; full-service segment hollow; Bravo Brio filed bankruptcy again.
  • Extended-Stay Hotels (2025): Grew 4% in supply—largest increase in four years—by capturing hybrid-work demand at premium occupancy levels.

Who rides it

Luxury hospitality and value positioning won. Boutique luxury hotels achieved 6% revenue growth in 2025 with $452 ADR; extended-stay properties captured hybrid-work patterns and grew 4% in supply. Value casual-dining chains (Chili's, Texas Roadhouse) gained share by offering perceived value—$12 sit-down plates that undercut $15 limited-service entrees. The common thread: they raised prices *and* filtered downward, so only price-insensitive or efficiency-hungry customers stayed. Whoever could reposition upscale or downscale won. Whoever tried to hold the middle lost.

Who's squeezed

Economy chains and midscale properties got squeezed hardest. Full-service casual dining—the $12-$20 entree sweet spot—hollowed out: TGI Fridays, Red Lobster, FAT Brands, On The Border, and Bravo Brio all filed Chapter 11 between 2024 and 2026, closing 130+ locations each. Operators who refused to raise prices or cut service quality just went bankrupt instead. Franchisees in high-wage states—especially California after April 2024—either exited or were forced to cut hours and staff so hard that service collapsed. Economy hotels contracted as extended-stay and luxury grew. Workers in these collapsing formats lost jobs.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that wage floors no longer allow the margin structure that built this industry, and traffic won't return. Fast-food visits fell 2% in Q4 2025; even if wage floors stayed flat, traffic erosion from GLP-1 adoption (5.3% restaurant-spending cuts per household) is structural, not cyclical. Hotels are running skeleton crews with 70-80% turnover—a level that means burned-out staff, guest complaints, and future defection. If more states follow California's $20 model, or if inflation forces further nominal increases, the math breaks for economy and midscale operators all over again. Automation was supposed to save the industry; Sonder's liquidation suggests it doesn't yet. The risk is a long, slow exit of capacity and quality from the budget and middle tiers, leaving only luxury and discount extremes.

In one sentence

The hospitality wage spiral in a nutshell

Low-wage hospitality repriced upward in 2024-2026, forcing restaurants and hotels to choose between eating the cost, cutting staff, automating the counter, or closing locations.
