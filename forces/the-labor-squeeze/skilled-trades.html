A · The trades can't hire

The skilled-trades gap

The aging trades workforce and few new entrants push wages up faster than contractors can pass them on.

Electricians, plumbers, HVAC techs, and roofers can't find enough people to fill the work. The workforce is aging out, trade schools are half-full, and demand keeps climbing. So wages are spiraling—but the contractors taking those jobs can't raise their prices fast enough. The gap between labor costs and what customers will pay is crushing smaller firms.

What changed

The skilled trades faced a historic labor shortage starting in 2025. The US electrician shortage got so acute that Nvidia and Microsoft CEOs called it out as a concrete bottleneck for AI infrastructure expansion. Data center construction became the unexpected growth engine for electricians in 2026, spending reaching $50.7 billion annually (up 28% year-over-year from 2025), but there simply weren't enough hands to do the work. Journeyman electricians started commanding $76K to $110K and higher, with specialized work in solar, EV charging, and data centers pulling premium rates. Yet the 269,000 electrical contractors in the US struggled to pass those wage bumps to customers—most businesses operate on razor-thin 5-6% margins, and even top-quartile shops only hit 10-12%. Meanwhile, tariffs ravaged input costs: copper wiring jumped 18% and electrical panels shot up 22% in 2025-2026, squeezing the very contractors trying to hire.

HVAC contractors hit a different bottleneck. The industry's 590,000 workers faced an EPA mandate (effective January 2026) forcing technicians to retrain on new A2L refrigerants like R-454B and R-32, and installation costs jumped 10% overnight. Wages for qualified techs climbed 8% annually through 2025-2026. Residential HVAC work cratered because mortgage rates stuck near 6%, killing new home construction (single-family starts fell 5% to 909,000 units in 2025), but commercial HVAC exploded due to energy-efficiency mandates and AI-driven building automation. The industry bifurcated—commercial growing 8.27% annually while residential squeezed—but both needed more trained technicians than existed. Private equity saw opportunity and pounced: add-on acquisitions jumped 88% year-over-year through mid-2025, with Apex Service Partners alone rolling up 100+ brands by early 2026. Small independent shops got hollowed out; big platforms with scale could absorb wage inflation, small ones couldn't.

Roofing contractors faced perhaps the bleakest scenario. The $92.5 billion industry (2026) is 80% repair and maintenance work, not new construction. When June 2025 tariffs doubled steel and aluminum duties from 25% to 50%, metal roofing prices shot up 60%, asphalt shingles rose 6-10%, and single-ply membranes climbed 12-18%. General roofing material costs jumped 15-25% in 2026, pushing the national average roof replacement bill over $20,500. Meanwhile, 62% of roofing contractors reported difficulty finding skilled workers, and labor costs jumped 10-30% for most firms in 2025. The construction industry needed 439,000+ new workers in 2026 overall, and roofers were competing for every available hand. Smaller roofing contractors—the bulk of the 109,000-firm market—hit the wall: Ready Roofing filed Chapter 11 in June 2026, Ribbit Roofing in May, Snohomish Roofing in March, and Certified Roofing Solutions in July, all citing margin pressure and tariff impact. Supply chains consolidated hard too: Home Depot acquired SRS Distribution for $18.25 billion in June 2024, and QXO bought Beacon Roofing Supply for ~$11 billion in May 2025, leaving smaller contractors at the mercy of mega-distributors.

The core dynamic is the same across all three trades: wages became scarce (labor supply is fixed and aging; few young people enter the trades), which made experienced workers expensive and valuable (journeymen electricians $76K-$110K+; HVAC techs pulling 8% annual raises). Materials got expensive too (tariffs, supply chain chaos). But the ability to raise prices to customers didn't keep pace. Most contractors operate on 5-7% margins and can't pass through a 15-25% material cost bump or a 10-30% labor cost jump. The customer doesn't pay more; the contractor eats it.

The evidence

  • Electricians: US data center construction spending reached $50.7 billion annually in 2026, up 28% year-over-year, but electrician shortage is acute enough that Nvidia and Microsoft CEOs have publicly cited it as a concrete bottleneck for AI infrastructure expansion (2026).
  • HVAC: Heat pumps outsold central air conditioners and gas furnaces for the first time in 2025, with over 48% of US homes now using electrical heating, driving commercial HVAC growth of 8.27% annually (2025-2026).
  • Roofing: 62% of contractors reported difficulty finding skilled roofers, and the construction industry needs 439,000+ new workers in 2026, with labor costs jumping 10-30% for most roofing firms in 2025 (2025-2026).
  • Tariffs: June 2025 steel and aluminum tariffs doubled from 25% to 50%, causing metal roofing prices to jump 60%, asphalt shingles to rise 6-10%, and single-ply membranes to climb 12-18% (2025-2026).
  • PE Consolidation: Private equity add-on acquisitions in HVAC services jumped 88% year-over-year through mid-2025; Apex Service Partners (Apollo-backed) rolled up 100+ brands by early 2026, while QXO acquired Beacon Roofing Supply for ~$11 billion in May 2025 (2025-2026).

Who rides it

Big platforms with scale—private equity-backed consolidators like Apex Service Partners, QXO, and PE-funded HVAC and roofing roll-ups—win by absorbing wage inflation and supply disruption across hundreds of acquired shops. They can move workers between jobs, negotiate better terms with material suppliers, and spread financial risk. Tech infrastructure companies with deep pockets (Nvidia, Microsoft, AWS) win because they can outbid smaller construction firms for electrician capacity and pay the premium rates. Mega-distributors like Home Depot and the newly consolidated roofing suppliers capture pricing power when contractors have nowhere else to buy. Workers who have specialized skills—particularly in data center, solar, EV charging, and heat pump installation—command premium wages and can pick employers.

Who's squeezed

Small independent contractors get squeezed from both sides. The 269,000 electrical contractors, 120,000 HVAC shops, and 80,000+ roofing firms operating as nonemployers can't raise prices to keep up with wage and material costs. Most operate on 5-6% margins and hit the wall when labor jumps 10-30% and materials jump 15-25%. Residential builders and renovation firms lose because both labor and materials got expensive while customer demand softened (single-family starts down 5% to 909,000 units). Homeowners and small businesses lose because service prices climbed sharply—roof replacement now exceeds $20,500, HVAC retrofits cost 10% more, and electricians command premium rates—while quality dropped as contractors rushed workers and cut corners to survive. Some contractors just gave up: four midsize roofing firms filed Chapter 11 in 2026 alone.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that the labor shortage becomes permanent structural, not cyclical. If fewer young people enter the trades over the next five years, the shortage will only deepen. Wages will keep spiraling, driving more small shops out of business and accelerating PE consolidation. Critical infrastructure—data centers, electrical grids, HVAC systems keeping hospitals and offices running—becomes dependent on a shrinking pool of expensive workers employed by a few mega-platforms. At the same time, tariffs on materials keep rebounding (copper, steel, aluminum stay volatile), and supply chains stay fragmented, so input costs never normalize. Smaller contractors keep going bankrupt, urban and rural areas with thin margins get abandoned by contractors seeking only high-margin work, and the customer pool that can afford the new price floors shrinks. Regulatory mandates (A2L refrigerants, energy efficiency codes, EV charging infrastructure) keep adding retraining costs, but there's no corresponding rise in what customers are willing to pay.

In one sentence

The skilled-trades gap in a nutshell

Aging trades workers and few new entrants made labor scarce and expensive, but contractors can't pass the cost to customers, so smaller firms go bankrupt while big platforms buy up the survivors.
