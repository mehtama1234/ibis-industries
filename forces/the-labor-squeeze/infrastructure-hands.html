A · The trades can't hire

Hands for the Infrastructure Boom

The EPA mandates lead pipe replacement, states spend record billions on transit, and federal infrastructure funds flow—but there are not enough workers to swing the hammers.

The US has $70 billion in water and sewer line work to do. New York just got $68.4 billion for mass transit. Federal infrastructure money is real and ramping. But the workers to build it are not showing up. Labor shortages are now the ceiling on how fast any of this actually happens.

What changed

The EPA's Lead and Copper Rule Improvements (October 2024, effective December 2024) mandates complete replacement of all lead service lines within 10 years—by 2034. That means $423 billion in pipe replacement work nationally, with utilities already facing execution risk. The water and sewer construction sector alone is a $70 billion industry (2026) handling this work, but it is constrained by one thing: there are not enough people to do it. The construction industry is short 306,000 workers as of July 2025. Eighty-eight percent of firms report hiring gaps. Forty-five percent of contractors are already reporting project delays. Wages for skilled trades are rising 8-12% annually just to try to pull workers in, and it is not working.

Federal spending has opened the tap. IIJA disbursements ramped from $1 billion per year in 2022 to a $11 billion peak projection for 2027. New York committed a record $68.4 billion to mass transit in 2025. California announced $2.5 billion in transit and infrastructure spending in July 2026. The Federal BUILD program has awarded over $18 billion across 18 rounds. Heavy engineering construction jumped to $42.5 billion (2026), up from $26 billion in 2021. And yet the sector is shrinking in real terms. Home builders can barely move the needle—$166.2 billion in 2026, essentially flat from 2022, despite $10,900 in tariff-driven cost increases per home that they are absorbing just to stay alive. The money is there. The work is there. The workers are not.

Tariffs are making it worse. Steel tariffs of 50% and aluminum tariffs of 25-30% add direct cost to every project. Water and sewer contractors face tariffs on imported pipe equipment pushing material costs up 5-10% on top of base prices. Home builders report tariff costs of $10,900 per unit, with 60% of builders reporting tariff-driven cost increases (2026). Contractors respond by cutting margins—heavy engineering firms are down to 5-7.5% net margins, flat or falling from 2025, because they cannot pass costs to cash-strapped municipalities and states. Water and sewer construction margins sit at 3.6% (2022 data; 2025-2026 figures not publicly disclosed, suggesting worse outcomes). When you cannot hire and you cannot raise prices, you squeeze margin. And when margins evaporate, smaller players consolidate or exit. M&A among home builders doubled between August 2025 and June 2026—18% of builders now report acquisition approaches, up from 9%. Taylor Morrison, Tri Pointe, United Homes Group, and Holiday Builders all changed hands. Flatiron and Dragados merged to create a $18.5 billion backlog powerhouse. The sector is consolidating into fewer, bigger firms that have capital to swallow tariff shocks and wages, leaving smaller contractors margin-crushed.

Regulatory whiplash is piling on. The EPA finalized lead line replacement mandates in October 2024, but in May 2026 it rolled back Biden-era PFAS 'forever chemicals' regulations—rescinding limits on PFHxS, PFNA, and HFPO-DA—and pushed PFOA/PFOS compliance from 2029 to 2031. That creates short-term cost relief but long-term unpredictability. More immediately, IIJA authorization expires September 30, 2026. The water program faces elimination, and the FY 2026 budget proposal cuts EPA funding by 23% and Drinking Water/Clean Water State Revolving Funds by 31.5%. Award volumes in water infrastructure already fell 53% in the first half of 2025 due to executive orders and regulatory uncertainty. Contractors cannot recruit workers for a boom they are no longer sure is coming.

The evidence

  • Water and sewer construction: $70 billion market (2026) facing 306,000 unfilled jobs and 3.6% margins—federal mandates create demand but labor shortages cap execution.
  • Heavy engineering construction: $42.5 billion (2026), up from $26 billion (2021), but 92% of firms report hiring difficulties and margins squeezed to 5-7.5% by tariffs and wage growth.
  • Home builders: $166.2 billion (2026), flat since 2022, absorbing $10,900 tariff costs per unit while M&A doubles and small firms consolidate; 60% report tariff-driven cost increases.
  • Federal infrastructure spending: $68.4 billion New York MTA capital program (2025), $18+ billion BUILD awards through 2025, but water program authorization expires September 2026 with 23% EPA budget cuts proposed.
  • Tariff impact: Construction goods tariffs at 25-30% effective rate (2026), 50% on steel, with material costs up 5-10% on water/sewer projects and labor wages up 8-12% annually across trades.

Who rides it

Large, well-capitalized construction firms (Flatiron-Dragados with $18.5 billion backlog, MasTec acquiring McKee Utility Contractors for $276 million in March 2026) win because they can absorb tariff shocks, float higher wage bills, and hold shrinking margins until federal spending climaxes. Engineering consultants and automation vendors benefit as firms invest in BIM, AI, and electrified equipment to fill labor gaps—capex-hungry but necessary. Utilities and states that front-load projects before IIJA expires in September 2026 get first access to labor and federal funds.

Who's squeezed

Smaller contractors and regional water/sewer builders get margin-crushed and consolidated. Home builders outside the top tier face the worst: tariff costs of $10,900 per unit that they cannot pass on, 4% annual wage growth for skilled trades they cannot find, and margins collapsing from 6.1% (2022) to 6-8% (2026) while M&A activity doubles. Municipal utilities and states without federal backing face a 10-year mandate (lead line replacement by 2034) but shrinking federal support (31.5% cut to Revolving Funds proposed in FY 2026 budget) and rising contractor costs.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that federal infrastructure authorization expires September 30, 2026—the water program sunsets, and the FY 2026 budget proposal cuts EPA by 23% and Revolving Funds by 31.5%. Contractors and utilities built staffing and pricing assumptions on IIJA lasting longer. If spending collapses, a year or two of hiring will reverse into layoffs, and the mandated lead line replacement work (completing by 2034) will stall. Award volumes already fell 53% in H1 2025. A full cliff in Q4 2026 would strand workers, idle firms, and leave municipalities unable to meet their 10-year compliance window without raising taxes or borrowing at higher yields.

In one sentence

Hands for the infrastructure boom in a nutshell

Federal infrastructure money is real but Federal authorization expires September 2026, leaving a $423 billion lead pipe replacement mandate and a $70 billion water sector with 306,000 unfilled jobs and margins too thin to raise wages further.
