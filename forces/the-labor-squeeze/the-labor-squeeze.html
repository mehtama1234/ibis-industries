E · The big picture

The labor squeeze

Workers got scarce — wages spiral, the trades can't fill the gap, and whoever can automate or pay up wins while everyone else eats the margin hit.

Labor just became the economy's binding constraint. Across electricians, home care, trucking, and fast food—industries that employ millions and touch every home—worker scarcity is forcing a painful choice: automate, consolidate, or die. The numbers are unambiguous: electricians now command $76K–$110K+ for specialized work; home care agencies report 75% annual turnover and can fill only 75% of job openings; trucking's average driver age is 46 with a retirement wave looming; and fast food traffic is collapsing even as chains raise wages to $20/hour in California and still can't find workers. For decades these were low-margin, fragmented industries. Now margin compression, wage pressure, and labor shortages are forcing a reshuffling that will concentrate wealth among scale players who can absorb the hit.

What changed

The electricians trade is being pulled in two directions and it is creating winners and losers fast. Residential construction has cratered because of high interest rates—the market that built the electrician industry—but data centers have become the unexpected growth engine. In April 2026, data center construction spending hit $50.7 billion annually, up 28% year-over-year. Nvidia and Microsoft CEOs have now publicly cited electrician shortages as a bottleneck for AI infrastructure. This demand for specialized labor—data centers, solar, EV charging—has driven wages and created a two-tier market. Journeymen earning $76K–$110K+ are commanding premium rates, especially for specialized work. But tariffs are crushing smaller contractors: copper wiring up 18%, electrical panels up 22%. Small independents cannot absorb that cost and cannot scale fast enough to chase the data center work. Consolidation is accelerating into 2026. The big platforms with capital and geographic reach can service the high-margin data center jobs while smaller shops fight over the shrinking residential market.

Home care is experiencing an even sharper squeeze: Medicare is cutting payments while demand explodes from an aging population. CMS finalized a 1.3% aggregate payment reduction for 2026, capping a four-year streak of cuts. At the same time, 95% of agencies report moderate-to-severe staffing shortages. Caregiver wages rose from $13.07 per hour in 2014 to $16.77 in 2024, but turnover stays at 75% annually because pay is still low relative to other healthcare roles and the work is relentless. The outcome is brutal: agencies are turning away up to 25% of patient requests because they cannot staff them. The market is now reshaping around private equity consolidation. General Atlantic acquired TEAM Services Group for approximately $3 billion in Q2 2026—the largest home care deal on record—and Kinderhook took Enhabit private for $1.1 billion in Q1 2026. These platforms have the capital to invest in technology (cloud EMRs, remote patient monitoring), absorb payment cuts across a broader cost base, and weather the wage pressure. Smaller independent agencies in rural areas are closing as they cannot compete on either dimension: they pay similar wages but cannot achieve the margins or efficiency gains that consolidation brings.

Trucking shows a parallel pattern of margin compression meeting driver scarcity. Operating costs per mile hit an all-time high of $2.34 in 2025. The US State Department paused new employment visas for truck drivers in August 2025, tightening labor supply on top of an aging driver workforce—average age 46, with a retirement wave expected over the coming decade. Small owner-operators and fleets under 1,000 trucks are being squeezed from both sides: rates are too soft to cover costs, and they cannot compete with large carriers on scale or financing. The only profitable segment is LTL (less-than-truckload) and mega-carriers with 1,000+ trucks. Spot van rates hit $2.41 per mile in late February 2026, up 20% year-over-year, signaling selective recovery, but this recovery is concentrated among large carriers that can optimize their network and wait out soft contract rates. A 25% tariff on imported heavy trucks starting October 2025 raised equipment costs, and cross-border freight dropped 5% in mid-2025 (Canada-bound traffic down 14.5%). The bankruptcies are reaching all-time high rates as small carriers exit.

Fast food shows what happens when a fragmented, low-margin industry meets labor scarcity with no room to automate. The market grew just 0.7% in 2026, and traffic is now declining faster. It fell 0.3% in 2025 and accelerated to negative 2% in Q4 2025 versus Q4 2024. The causes are structural: the GLP-1 appetite suppression effect is real (households with a GLP-1 user cut restaurant spending by an average 5.3%), and consumer spending is retracting under higher rates and reduced wage growth. California's $20 per hour minimum wage for fast food workers, effective April 2024, lifted operating costs roughly 25% and forced chains to raise menu prices 8–12%. But raising prices only works until customers stop coming. Tariffs have added another layer: avian flu has pressured poultry supplies, and 15% of US food imports are now tariffed, pushing commodity costs up steadily. Large chains with scale can absorb these hits through automation (kiosks, kitchen robots, delivery optimization) and by raising prices selectively at franchises with captive customer bases. Smaller chains and franchisees are not so lucky. BurgerFi filed Chapter 11 in September 2024, TGI Fridays in November 2024, and Uncle Julio's assets sold in foreclosure auction in December 2024. Each cited the same pressures: labor inflation, traffic loss, and an inability to pass full costs to price-sensitive customers. The survivors will be large platforms that can automate, consolidate, and tolerate lower margins while crushing independents.

The evidence

  • Electricians: Data center construction spending reached $50.7 billion annualized in April 2026, up 28% YoY, pulling specialized labor away from residential work and forcing wage pressure of $76K–$110K+ for journeymen.
  • Home care: CMS implemented a 1.3% aggregate payment cut in 2026 while 95% of agencies report moderate-to-severe staffing shortages; General Atlantic's $3 billion TEAM Services acquisition in Q2 2026 accelerates consolidation.
  • Trucking: Operating costs hit an all-time high of $2.34 per mile in 2025; the US State Department paused employment visas for truck drivers in August 2025, tightening labor supply as the average driver age is 46.
  • Fast food: Traffic declined 2% in Q4 2025 versus Q4 2024; California's $20/hour minimum wage (April 2024) raised operating costs 25%, forcing menu price increases of 8–12% while GLP-1 users cut spending 5.3%.
  • Cross-industry pattern: Tariff-driven material cost inflation (copper +18%, electrical panels +22%, tariffed food imports +15%) is crushing small, fragmented operators who cannot automate, consolidate, or absorb margin compression.

Who rides it

Scale players and consolidators dominate. Large electrical contractors and PE-backed platforms in home care can absorb tariff costs, invest in technology, serve high-margin customers (data centers, enterprise health systems), and tolerate lower margins on commodity work. Mega-carriers in trucking (1,000+ trucks) with optimized networks survive rate compression. Fast food chains with scale can automate (kiosks, kitchen robots) and raise prices selectively. Private equity is the big structural winner—TEAM Services, Enhabit, Kindred Healthcare all consolidating fragmented markets into platforms that can weather wage pressure, payment cuts, and tariff headwinds.

Who's squeezed

Fragmented, small operators get crushed. Independent electricians cannot scale to data center work or absorb tariff inflation. Small home care agencies in rural areas close as they cannot match platform efficiency or wage competitiveness. Owner-operators and fleets under 1,000 trucks are exiting trucking at record bankruptcy rates. Franchisees and smaller fast food chains cannot automate or absorb labor cost inflation—BurgerFi, TGI Fridays, Uncle Julio's all filed bankruptcy or foreclosure in 2024–2025 under these exact pressures.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest tension is whether wage growth and labor scarcity become a wage-price spiral that pushes inflation back up and forces the Fed to re-tighten, or whether automation and margin compression accelerate fast enough to prevent broad-based wage acceleration. Home care is the crux: if caregiver wages must rise further to fill the 25% of unfilled patient requests and retain staff below 75% turnover, that cascades into Medicare payment pressure and insurance costs. Fast food and trucking show the dark side: when wages and automation race, automation wins and workers lose. The risk is that labor scarcity becomes a structural feature—not a cycle—because demographic trends (retirements, low birth rates, immigration restrictions) mean the labor pool is actually shrinking in absolute terms. If that is true, then wage-price dynamics will stay inflated and consolidation will accelerate as the only lever to restore profitability.

The labor squeeze in a nutshell
