The driver shortage

Trucking's driver crisis meets freight cycles—automation doesn't move the needle yet, so wages spiral and carriers bleed out.

Trucking has a problem that no amount of technology has solved: there aren't enough drivers, and there never will be again. The average long-haul driver is 46 years old. The Department of State paused new employment visas for truck drivers in August 2025. Simultaneously, the industry is caught between tariffs that raised truck equipment costs by $10,000 to $35,000 per vehicle and freight demand that barely keeps pace. Carriers are going bankrupt. Rates are finally rising. But the margin squeeze is real.

What changed

The freight trucking market has entered a structural bottleneck, not because there isn't work, but because people won't do it at the wages the industry wants to pay. In 2022, the general freight truckload sector was a $212.2 billion market. By 2026, it had grown to $300.1 billion—a 41% expansion—but the number of carriers actually shrank. Ninety-three thousand trucking companies have exited the market since 2022. Bankruptcies hit a record high in 2025. Yellow Corporation, one of the largest LTL carriers, liquidated in 2024, and regional operators like Daniel Trucking and Davis Express filed Chapter 11 or ceased operations in 2025. Meanwhile, operating costs per mile hit an all-time high of $2.34 in 2025, with fuel alone eating up roughly 21% of total costs, driver wages climbing steadily, and tariffs adding new friction—25% duties on imported heavy trucks started in October 2025.

What got scarce was driver availability, not cargo. The problem isn't a recession in demand; it's an exodus of people willing to drive. Long-distance refrigerated trucking, which serves pharma and food cold chains, has roughly 22,000 to 24,000 drivers with an average age of 57—meaning retirements aren't coming in five years, they're coming now. The US trucking workforce is approximately 1.3 million people, but small carriers can't compete for talent when Walmart, Amazon, and other mega-employers are hiring at $20-plus per hour for warehouse and local delivery jobs with no six-week training requirement and no three weeks away from home. A March 2026 regulation barred asylum seekers and DACA recipients from obtaining commercial driver licenses, cutting off a pipeline that represented one in six truckers. The State Department paused hiring visas the month before, tightening the international supply. The industry's response was predictable: wages began climbing, but only fast enough to stop the bleeding, not fast enough to attract new entrants.

Spot rates climbed because spot rates *had* to climb. By late February 2026, van rates hit $2.41 per mile, up 20% year-over-year. In the reefer market—long-distance refrigerated—rates reached $3.35 per mile in May 2026, up a full dollar from $2.35 a year earlier. These look like victories for carriers, except they aren't. The reason rates rose is that small carriers were forced to exit. The industry didn't add capacity to meet demand; it shed unprofitable capacity and raised prices on the shipping customers who were left. Large carriers—Prime Inc., C.R. England, Schneider National—consolidated. The local trucking segment saw spot linehaul rates jump 23% year-over-year through February 2026, but contract rates only climbed 5%, meaning one-off loads got expensive while regular customers got protected. Tariffs accelerated this consolidation: a 25% duty on Class 8 trucks from Mexico and Canada (raised to 50% on Canadian goods in August 2026) added $10,000 to $35,000 per truck. Forty-seven percent of carriers delayed fleet replacement entirely. Small owner-operators, who make up 88.9% of the 660,000 to 795,000 truckload establishments in the US, couldn't afford new equipment and couldn't afford used equipment either.

What became newly valuable was scale, data, and automation—but slowly. Large brokers reported 30% productivity gains in quoting and load management through AI adoption by 2026, letting them move more shipments with the same headcount. E-commerce parcels hit 22.37 billion in 2024, and every parcel meant local last-mile trucking; that kept smaller carriers alive in their niches, but pricing power remained fragile. The regulatory squeeze on training providers—the FMCSA removed nearly 3,000 training schools from the registry in late 2025 and sent removal notices to 550+ additional CDL schools following 1,400+ onsite investigations in early 2026—meant that the supply of new drivers stopped just as demand for experienced drivers rose. Pharmaceutical cold-chain logistics hit $22.75 billion in 2025, growing at 9.12% annually, and only large carriers with compliance infrastructure and real-time monitoring could capture that margin. The industry needed workers and wasn't getting them. It needed investment and couldn't justify it. It needed automation and got quoting software instead.

The evidence

  • General Freight Trucking: $300.1 billion market in 2026 (up from $212.2 billion in 2021), but 93,000 carriers have exited since 2022, with operating costs at $2.34/mile and spot van rates at $2.41/mile in February 2026 (up 20% YoY).
  • Local Freight Trucking: $92.6 billion in 2026 (0.1% growth, compressed by tariffs and wages), with spot linehaul rates up 23% YoY to $2.01/mile in February 2026; 3,000 CDL training providers removed in late 2025.
  • Long-Distance Refrigerated Trucking: $13.6 billion (2025), growing 5.9% in 2026, but with 22,000–24,000 drivers averaging age 57; reefer rates hit $3.35/mile in May 2026, up $1.00 YoY, and bankruptcies continue (Daniel Trucking July 2025, Davis Express April 2025).
  • Tariff Impact: 25% duty on Class 8 trucks effective October 2025 (50% on Canadian goods August 2026) adds $10,000–$35,000 per vehicle, forcing 47% of carriers to postpone fleet replacement and deepening the margin squeeze.
  • Labor Scarcity: US State Department paused employment visas for truck drivers (August 2025); March 2026 CDL rule barred asylum seekers and DACA recipients, eliminating one in six truckers; average driver age is 46 (long-haul) to 57 (reefer), signaling retirement wave.

Who rides it

Large carriers with scale, compliance infrastructure, and digital logistics platforms win. Prime Inc., C.R. England, Schneider National—the carriers with 1,000-plus trucks—consolidated market share as smaller competitors exited. They can absorb tariff costs, navigate regulation, attract drivers with benefits packages and home-time policies that small fleets can't match, and run AI-powered load management systems that generate 30% productivity gains. Brokers with real-time monitoring and cold-chain expertise capture the high-margin pharma and vaccine lanes (the $22.75 billion segment growing at 9.12% annually). The mega-carriers also benefit from rate firming: spot van rates up 20%, reefer rates up 40% year-over-year. They have the balance sheet to survive a freight recession and the operational heft to thrive in the recovery.

Who's squeezed

Small carriers and owner-operators get crushed. Eighty-eight point nine percent of truckload establishments are nonemployers or micro-fleets, and they operate on less than 1% margins. When tariffs add $10,000 to $35,000 per truck, they can't replace aging equipment. When wages climb and the driver pool shrinks, they can't compete with large carriers for talent. Regional carriers—the mid-tier players who had some profitability—filed bankruptcy at record rates in 2025. Even as spot rates rise, contract rates (which feed the small carriers' bread and butter) only climbed 5% while spot rates jumped 23%, meaning the upside goes to the big players running spot markets, not to the local truckers. A driver shortage doesn't hurt the consolidated carrier; it hurts the small carrier who can't find a driver and can't raise rates enough to pay one.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The industry has no answer for the driver shortage yet. Automation looks good on a PowerPoint (AI in load matching, real-time monitoring, autonomous concepts years away), but it doesn't move the needle on the fundamental problem: there is no pipeline of new drivers entering the market at scale. Ninety-three thousand carriers exited since 2022, but new ones aren't being born to replace them. The FMCSA crackdown on CDL training providers (3,000 removed in late 2025, 550+ additional notices in early 2026) cut off the supply of newly licensed drivers. A March 2026 regulation eliminated asylum seekers and DACA recipients from CDL eligibility, slashing one in six truckers from the legal labor pool. The State Department paused hiring visas a month prior. Meanwhile, the average driver age is 46 (long-haul) to 57 (reefer), and retirement waves are coming this decade. If wages don't rise enough to attract new people, and regulation keeps cutting off labor sources, and automation takes five more years to mature, the industry faces a multi-year capacity crunch where rates keep rising but shipment velocity slows. Customers shift to alternatives (nearshoring, inventory buffers, local warehousing) to avoid the cost and unreliability. The rate spike becomes a demand destroyer.

In one sentence

The driver shortage in a nutshell

Trucking's driver shortage made workers scarce and wages expensive, consolidating market power to large carriers while small fleets bleed out and automation remains theoretical.
