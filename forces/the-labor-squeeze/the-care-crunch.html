B · Care work runs short

The care-worker crunch

Nurses and home aides are the scarce input in a booming, demographically guaranteed sector—and margins collapse for anyone who can't pay up.

Care work—nursing, home health aides, residential support—is one of the few sectors with ironclad demand. Millions of aging Americans need care, and they'll keep needing it. But the workers who deliver that care have become the binding constraint. Wages have spiked, turnover is catastrophic, and providers face a brutal choice: raise wages and kill margins, or ration care and lose patients.

What changed

The demographic squeeze is forcing care to scale whether the industry is ready or not. US hospitals employ 5.8 million people and served a market worth 1.5 trillion dollars in 2025, but margins have collapsed from 7.6% in 2022 to just 1-2% today because Medicare reimburses at only 82-87 cents per dollar of care. Labor costs alone exploded 42% since 2020, hitting hospitals from both sides: wage bills spiral upward while reimbursement stays flat. The result is a consolidation frenzy—22 M&A deals worth 14.5 billion in Q1 2026 alone, the highest Q1 in five years, as larger systems cannibalize smaller ones for negotiating leverage. Larger chains can spread fixed costs and negotiate drug prices; regional hospitals and independent operators cannot. Bankruptcy filings reached 20 in 2025, up from 15 in 2024. The Steward Health Care bankruptcy in 2025 became the largest hospital-sector collapse in 30 years. Smaller, geographically isolated hospitals die. Large systems survive but with razor margins and constant labor pressure—Kaiser Permanente's 30,000+ worker strike in early 2026 over wages and staffing remains ongoing as of August.

Home care is even more vulnerable because the fragmentation is extreme. The market grew to 191.6 billion dollars in 2026 at a 3.9% growth rate, but 437,000 separate agencies compete in a race to the bottom on reimbursement. Medicare payment cuts have hit home care for four straight years, with a 1.3% aggregate cut in 2026 (220 million dollars) after a proposed 6.4% slash was partly averted. Agencies are closing in rural areas because they can't absorb the hits. But the real killer is staffing. Ninety-five percent of home care agencies report moderate-to-severe staffing shortages, turning away up to 25% of patient requests. Caregiver wages rose from 13.07 dollars per hour in 2014 to 16.77 dollars in 2024, a 28% bump, but turnover stays stuck at 75% annually because the work is brutal and low-pay relative to other healthcare roles. Home health aide positions are projected to grow 17% over the decade 2024-2034, but the supply of workers who'll take the jobs isn't keeping pace. Private equity sees a demographic safe bet and is sweeping up fragmentation: 55 home health and hospice deals in just the first half of 2026, with 41% PE-backed. General Atlantic bought TEAM Services Group for 3 billion dollars in Q2 2026—the largest home care deal on record. PE consolidation creates scale, but even scaled platforms can't escape the wage-turnover-margin squeeze because they're still filling the same shortage of bodies.

Senior residential care is riding the demographic wave but hitting the same labor wall. The market is 110 billion dollars in the non-medical in-home franchise segment alone, and occupancy reached 88.7% in Q3 2025, the 17th consecutive quarterly increase. Growth is accelerating as baby boomers enter their 80s (2027 onwards). But registered nurses, certified nursing assistants, and dining staff report 40% turnover, and over half of home care agencies report ongoing shortages. Wages have climbed 31% cumulatively since 2019, and operators face a binary choice: raise pay and shrink margins, or leave beds empty or understaffed. The industry is responding with historic consolidation: 2025 saw 871 M&A deals, 20.8% above 2024's prior record of 721. Assisted living accounted for 44% of those deals. On an annualized basis, 2026 is surpassing both years. The demand is locked in—73 million baby boomers, aging in place, needing assistance. But the supply of workers is tight, and whoever can consolidate fastest and achieve scale wins negotiating power over suppliers and access to capital for wage investments. The gap between occupancy demand and staffing capacity means winners take the market while losers shut down.

Across all three segments—hospitals, home care, residential senior care—technology is being pressed into service as a partial hedge. Seventy-five percent of health systems deployed at least one AI tool by 2025, mostly for note-taking and image analysis, to reduce administrative burden on nursing staff and squeeze more throughput from the same number of bodies. But IT infrastructure remains a bottleneck, and AI can't replace a human aide turning a patient in bed or monitoring someone's breathing at night. The labor shortage is real and structural. Whoever can pay up and retain staff wins. Whoever can't consolidates or fails.

The evidence

  • Hospitals: Medicare reimbursement at 82-87 cents per dollar, forcing consolidation—22 deals worth 14.5B in Q1 2026 (highest Q1 in 5 years).
  • Home care: 95% of 437,000 agencies report severe staffing shortages; caregiver wages rose to $16.77/hour in 2024, but 75% turnover persists.
  • Home care deals: General Atlantic acquired TEAM Services Group for $3B (Q2 2026), largest home care deal on record; 55 deals in H1 2026 alone, 41% PE-backed.
  • Senior care: 871 M&A deals in 2025 (20.8% above 2024), with assisted living at 44%; occupancy hit 88.7% in Q3 2025 (17th consecutive quarterly rise).
  • Kaiser strike: 30,000+ workers (nurses, service staff, clerical) struck in early 2026 over wages and staffing, ongoing as of August 2026—largest healthcare labor action in years.

Who rides it

Large health systems and private equity consolidators win by achieving scale. Kaiser, Ascension, Humana, and regional roll-ups can negotiate supplier contracts, spread fixed IT costs, and absorb wage pressures while still turning a profit on volume. PE-backed platforms acquire fragmented agencies, integrate technology, and sell scale and operational efficiency. BrightSpring Health Services, General Atlantic's latest deal (TEAM Services for 3 billion), and Kinderhook's 1.1 billion Enhabit acquisition show PE betting on demographic tailwinds as a structural safe play. Consolidated operators can afford to raise wages to retain staff, which smaller competitors cannot. They also get better access to capital for technology investments and real-estate collateral. Hospitals and senior care franchises that can absorb turnover costs and invest in retention programs survive and grow margins as smaller players exit.

Who's squeezed

Independent and regional hospitals, small home care agencies, and mid-market senior care operators get squeezed. A 2025 bankruptcy count of 20 hospitals, up from 15, and closures of rural home care agencies show the carnage. Providers that can't consolidate and can't command premium reimbursement face a brutal margin squeeze: Medicare cuts are mandatory (1.3% in home care, reductions in hospital reimbursement), wages are rising (16.77 per hour in home care in 2024, 31% cumulative growth in senior care since 2019), and they have no leverage to negotiate. Patients in rural areas lose access to care as agencies close. Workers at failing providers get laid off. Patients get rationed—95% of home care agencies are turning away 25% of requests because staffing is impossible. The middle market disappears; you're either big and consolidated or you're bankrupt.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that wage spirals and consolidation outpace demand growth, leaving the system unable to serve the booming number of aging Americans at any price point. Seventy-three million baby boomers are entering retirement, and 711,700 new home care jobs are projected 2022-2032, but the projected supply of workers in healthcare roles is not keeping pace with that demand. If consolidation doesn't produce enough efficiency or technology adoption doesn't reduce labor intensity enough, and if wages continue to spiral, the cost of care could exceed what Medicare, Medicaid, and middle-class families can afford. The government could then impose hard reimbursement caps, crushing providers entirely and causing wholesale care rationing. Alternatively, the shortage could force a cascade of wage theft, undocumented workers, and quality collapse in rural and poor neighborhoods. The demographic demand is guaranteed, but the delivery system's solvency is not.

In one sentence

The care-worker crunch in a nutshell

The care sector is growing fast because 73 million baby boomers need care, but the workers who provide it are scarce, costly, and leaving in droves, forcing providers to choose between losing money and losing staff.
