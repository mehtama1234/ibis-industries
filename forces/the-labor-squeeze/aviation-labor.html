Aviation's people problem

Pilots got expensive, caterers got squeezed, and airports can't find the staff to handle record crowds.

Aviation is running full. Passenger load factors hit 84% in 2026—planes so packed there's almost no slack. But the people who move those planes and feed those passengers are scarce, expensive, and in control. Pilots negotiated 30-50% pay raises between 2023 and 2025. Catering workers have been in contract disputes for seven years. And the FAA is 3,000 controllers short of requirement, triggering thousands of flight delays every month. The labor squeeze has hit aviation hard, reshaping margins and consolidation patterns across the whole industry.

What changed

Labor became the single largest operating cost for US airlines. In 2026, labor represents 36.8% of airline operating expenses—a threshold reached after pilot pay surges of 30-50% negotiated between 2023 and 2025. That's not gradual inflation; that's a structural shift. The pilot shortage is real and measurable: a 24,000-pilot shortfall is projected to peak in 2026 from mandatory retirements. Airlines can't fill the gaps with inexperienced pilots—Federal rules require 1,500 flight hours minimum. So the shortage cascades into either canceled flights or higher wages, and higher wages then get passed to fares and absorbed by passengers. Jet fuel spiked 74% year-over-year in May 2026 (hitting $4.51 per gallon), but airlines have learned that they can raise fares when labor costs climb too, because the alternative—cutting flights—destroys revenue faster. The domestic airline market grew 9.7% CAGR from 2021 to 2026 (from $135.2 billion to $268.2 billion), yet net margins are expected to be only 3.9% in 2026. That's the squeeze in a single number: revenue doubled but profit is thin because wages ate the spread.

Ground crews and support staff face similar dynamics at airports. The US airport operations market is $17.9 billion in 2026, recovering to 104% of pre-pandemic passenger volumes by 2024. But airports employ only about 155,000 people to handle that traffic—a number that looks adequate until you ask why it takes five people to check a bag when one person could do it twelve years ago. The answer is the same: wages for skilled ground crews rose, so airports stretched remaining staff thinner or hired lower-wage workers and burned through turnover. The FAA air traffic controller crisis is the most visible scar: only 14,264 controllers on payroll in 2024 when the system needs 17,264. Government shutdowns in late 2025 made it worse—peak delays reached 8,000 flights on a single day. This isn't a temporary glitch; it's a structural bottleneck. Controllers are overworked, retiring fast, and training a new one takes three to four years. No airline can solve that. It's a tax on the whole industry.

Airline catering is the clearest picture of labor and margin collapse. The catering market is $3.9 billion in 2025 with razor-thin margins: 4-5% EBIT, about a quarter of what a healthy industrial company expects. Seven years of labor disputes at major catering firms—LSG Sky Chefs, Gate Gourmet, Flying Food Group—have run wages and tension higher without resolution. In July 2026, hundreds of Flying Food Group workers blocked airport entry at LAX over wage and safety claims. The industry's leverage is flipped upside down: airlines own the customer, so they dictate meal budgets. When fuel prices spike 30.9% in a single month (as happened in March 2026), airlines cut economy meals entirely rather than absorb the cost. Hawaiian Airlines eliminated free meals on all domestic flights except New York in July 2026, offering paid options at $10.99 to $16.99 instead. Caterers have to lay off workers or negotiate down—they can't push back. The only innovation breaking through is AI: Airbus deployed Smart Catering AI on Virgin Atlantic flights in 2025-2026 to track meal waste and optimize menus, which helps caterers defend margins against shrinking meal budgets.

Consolidation is speeding up because only big airlines can survive thin margins and wage pressure. Spirit Airlines, the last true low-cost carrier that could undercut the Big 4, ceased operations in May 2026, eliminating 17,000 jobs. Allegiant Air acquired Sun Country Airlines that same month. JetBlue has explored mergers with Southwest, United, or Alaska to get bigger and handle higher labor costs. The industry is absorbing thousands of employees into fewer, larger carriers. Small and mid-size airlines die. Workers lose individual negotiating power when they're absorbed. But the Big 4 get pricing power and scale to absorb wage inflation—at least for a while, until fuel prices spike again or recession hits and load factors drop below 80%.

The evidence

  • US domestic airlines: $268.2 billion market (2026), 3.9% net margin, labor at 36.8% of OPEX; pilot pay +30-50% (2023-2025) driven by 24,000-pilot shortage
  • US airports: $17.9 billion market (2026), 104% recovery to pre-pandemic passenger volumes, but FAA short 3,000 controllers, triggering 8,000 peak flight delays (late 2025)
  • Airline catering: $3.9 billion market (2025), 4-5% EBIT margin, 7-year labor disputes at major firms; July 2026 worker blockade at LAX over wages and safety
  • Jet fuel shock: 74% YoY cost increase (May 2026, $4.51/gal); airlines cut economy meals, shifting burden to catering workers; Hawaiian Airlines eliminated free meals except NY route (July 2026)
  • Consolidation wave: Spirit Airlines failed May 2026 (17,000 jobs lost); Allegiant acquired Sun Country same month; JetBlue exploring mergers with Southwest/United/Alaska to absorb wage pressure at scale

Who rides it

The big four airlines—United, American, Southwest, Delta—win by consolidating competitors and raising fares when labor costs climb. Load factors at 84% mean they can pass wage inflation to passengers without losing volume. They're exploring mergers to get even bigger, which gives them leverage to negotiate with unions while hedging fuel cost risk across a larger network. Airports in major hubs also win by charging airlines and passengers higher fees for scarce capacity and gates—traffic is booming and slot scarcity is real. Technology vendors like Airbus and gategroup win by selling AI waste-reduction tools to caterers struggling on 4% margins. And anyone already holding pilot licenses wins by having sellers compete for their time: new hires start at $60,000+ and scale to $200,000+ within ten years.

Who's squeezed

Regional and mid-size carriers lose because they can't absorb 30-50% wage increases at scale. Spirit went under; Allegiant swallowed Sun Country. Small airports lose because they can't compete for the hub traffic where margins are fatter. Catering workers lose in a strange way: wages have stalled in disputes for seven years, while the work is harder as meal budgets shrink and efficiency mandates rise (AI tracking waste means less slack). Ground crews and TSA screeners lose because they're often government or contractor workers locked into rigid pay bands while cost-of-living rises. Passengers lose initially because fares climb faster than wages for most workers; over time, fewer people fly and business shrinks. And the FAA loses because Controller pay hasn't kept up with private-sector alternatives—burnout, retirements, and three-to-four-year training cycles mean the shortage is self-reinforcing.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that labor shortages collide with recession before supply catches up. If a recession hits in 2027-2028, airlines cut capacity and lay off thousands of staff just when they finally trained enough pilots to fill the 24,000-pilot gap. Load factors drop, fares fall, and the margin squeeze reverses into a margin collapse—except wages don't fall as fast as revenue. Layoffs follow. The industry entered this squeeze with a 3.9% net margin and no cushion; a 15% recession-driven traffic drop would mean losses across the board. The consolidation into four carriers also means less redundancy in the network: if one of them hits distress (fuel shock, cyberattack on bookings, labor strike), it cascades. And the FAA controller shortage has no quick fix—training capacity is fixed, so even if Congress funded more jobs and raised pay, controllers won't show up in volume until 2029-2030. Delays, operational risk, and safety culture strain will simmer for years.

In one sentence

Aviation's people problem in a nutshell

Scarce pilots and controllers made labor the biggest cost in aviation, forcing consolidation among carriers and squeezing catering workers while load factors hit 84% and margins shrink to 3.9%.
