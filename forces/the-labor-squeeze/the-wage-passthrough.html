D · The responses

Passing the wages on

Service businesses try to pass wage inflation to customers — until price resistance bites and margins collapse.

When labor gets scarce and wages spike, the instinct is simple: charge customers more. Coffee shops, bars, breweries—the whole consumer-service stack tries it. But most don't have the pricing power. Customers walk. Margins vanish. And the places caught in the middle—independent operators, fast-growth concepts, regional players—fold.

What changed

In coffee, the wage squeeze meets commodity inflation head-on and the customer refuses to pay. Arabica prices surged 21% year-over-year in August 2025 and stayed 18.5% higher than a year prior as of April 2026. Tariffs whipped margins twice—10-50% rates hit in spring 2025, got pulled in November, then 10% snapped back on in February 2026. Starbucks, the industry giant, absorbed the hit: North America operating income dropped 41%, from $5.4 billion in fiscal 2024 to $3.2 billion in fiscal 2025. To cover labor costs, they spent $500 million on extra shifts in Q2 2025 just to handle mobile order volumes. The market itself is barely moving—$75.5 billion in 2026, flat at -0.2% growth that year. Three independents couldn't take it: Compass Coffee closed 10 of 30 stores after Chapter 11 in January 2026, then got bought for $2.9 million by Nerò. Red Bay Coffee and The Blend both folded. Meanwhile, the big players got bigger: Keurig Dr Pepper finished an $18 billion acquisition of JDE Peet's in April 2026, creating a $16 billion pure-play coffee company with 50+ brands. The customer shifted too—59% bought via drive-thru in fall 2025, 38% via app. Convenience and automation became the margin escape hatch.\n\nBars faced a different version of the same squeeze. Margins fell from 6.8% of revenue in 2022 to 5.4% in 2026—nearly 20% compression. The industry employed 392,915 workers in 2022 and was projected to grow to 424,379 by 2027, but that's across 70,000 venues with no chain holding more than 5% of the market. A typical nightclub's staff costs $70,416 per month, and bartender turnover runs 50-70% annually, which means constant hiring friction and wage pressure. Imported spirits hit with 15-20% price increases by mid-2026 after tariffs, and domestic spirits felt grain inflation and barrel constraints. Consumers, especially Gen Z, got selective. Gen Z's consumption rate actually rose to 73% in 2025 from 66% in March 2023, but 65% of Gen Zers said they intended to drink less. They drink fewer times per occasion—3.9 drinks instead of 4.4 in 2024—but spend more per drink when they do show up. Cheap volume is dead. The industry bifurcated: upscale experiential venues thrived, private equity started consolidating (Sun Holdings bought Bar Louie in 2025), and everyone else felt the margin squeeze. Themed venues surged 32.9% in 2025, and experiential dining was up 46% year-over-year with 34% more pop-ups and chef experiences. The customer was willing to pay, but only for something memorable.\n\nBreweries got hit worst of all. Production fell 5.1% in 2025 and another 4% in the first half of 2026. The industry had 9,344 breweries by June 2026, down 1.8% year-over-year, with 481 closures in 2025. The 191,000 craft brewery workers in 2025 had already declined 3% from the year prior. Labor costs topped $25 per hour for many, and aluminum cans jumped 15% due to tariff pass-through. Only 54% of surveyed breweries reported growth; 43% reported declines. Three went under: Alamo Beer Company filed Chapter 11 in February 2025, Duck-Rabbit Craft Brewery filed Chapter 7 in April 2025, and Nebraska Brewing Company filed Chapter 11. But the bifurcation was stark: consumers who call themselves craft beer drinkers are drinking more often (85% monthly consumption, up 10 points), and brewery visits rose to 5.5 per year. Market share by volume for craft held at 13.4%. Large producers with automation survived and gained share—Molson Coors' premium light beers added 1.8 percentage points of market share by August 2024, and the Above Premium segment was now 28% of net sales, up from 23% three years earlier. Non-alcoholic beer, the one segment exempt from volume collapse, jumped 111% in volume from 2021 to 2025. The small, independent brewer—caught between wage floors they couldn't automate away and customers who wouldn't pay for commodity beer—had nowhere to hide.

The evidence

  • Coffee & Snack Shops: Starbucks' North America operating income collapsed 41% (FY2025 vs FY2024) amid $500M labor investment to absorb wage costs; three independent chains folded (Compass, Red Bay, The Blend) while Keurig Dr Pepper closed $18B JDE Peet's acquisition in April 2026.
  • Bars & Nightclubs: Margins fell from 6.8% (2022) to 5.4% (2026); Gen Z drinkers moderated volume (-0.5 drinks per occasion) but premiumized spend; tariff-driven spirit prices rose 15-20% by mid-2026; experiential venues +32.9% (2025) while traditional venues struggled.
  • Breweries: Production down 5.1% (2025) and 4% H1-2026; 9,344 breweries operating by June 2026 (down 1.8% YoY); three filings (Alamo, Duck-Rabbit, Nebraska) in 2025; only 54% reported growth versus 43% reporting declines; labor costs exceed $25/hour with 15% tariff-driven packaging cost spikes.

Who rides it

The winners are the ones with scale and automation, or the ones who can charge premium prices to customers with real money. Starbucks survived its margin collapse by automating and investing in labor—mobile ordering captured 38% of sales by fall 2025, and drive-thru took 59%. Keurig Dr Pepper bought JDE Peet's for $18 billion and now controls 50+ brands with $16 billion in sales—consolidation lets them negotiate input costs and absorb labor inflation across a wider revenue base. In bars, upscale experiential venues thrived: themed bars up 32.9% in 2025, experiential dining up 46% year-over-year. Private equity started rolling up: Sun Holdings bought Bar Louie in 2025. In breweries, large producers with automation, like Molson Coors, gained market share—premium light beers added 1.8 percentage points and the Above Premium segment is now 28% of net sales. Non-alcoholic beer, the only segment with true volume growth, jumped 111% from 2021 to 2025 as consumers found an escape route. Whoever could automate away the labor or charge a premium escaped the squeeze.

Who's squeezed

The independents and regionals get squeezed the hardest. Compass Coffee, a fast-growth concept, filed bankruptcy and sold for $2.9 million. Red Bay Coffee and The Blend folded entirely. Alamo Beer Company, Duck-Rabbit Craft Brewery, and Nebraska Brewing Company all filed for bankruptcy in 2025. The craft beer sector lost 481 breweries in 2025 alone, with 191,000 workers, down 3% year-over-year. For smaller operators, the math is brutal: they have to pay $25+ per hour for labor, they can't pass tariff-driven aluminum costs (up 15%) to price-sensitive customers, and they can't afford automation. Margins vanish. Only 54% of surveyed breweries reported growth versus 43% reporting declines. In bars, the fragmentation is so extreme (70,000 venues, no chain over 5% market share) that independent owners face $70,416 per month in staff costs with turnover running 50-70% annually, and tariff-driven spirit prices up 15-20%, with no way to pass it through to younger customers moderating their intake. The customer in every category—coffee, bars, beer—is willing to pay, but only for something automated, scaled, or memorable. Commodity service at old prices is extinct.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is a wage floor that becomes unmovable while automation is still incomplete and consumers refuse to pay. Across all three industries, labor costs have no downward path—bartender turnover at 50-70% annually means turnover costs and wage pressure are persistent, and brewery labor exceeds $25 per hour with no alternative. If tariffs stay elevated (10% reimposed in February 2026 after removal in November 2025), commodity costs don't fall either. The customer will keep bifurcating: premium experiential and automated wins, while mid-market traditional loses. For a mid-sized regional brewery or an independent nightclub or a second-wave coffee shop, there's a long squeeze until either automation scales faster or the labor market loosens. The risk is sustained margin erosion and a wave of bankruptcies among anyone who can't afford to automate or doesn't have access to capital to acquire or rebrand.

In one sentence

Passing the wages on in a nutshell

Service businesses can't pass wage inflation to price-sensitive customers, so independents and regionals fold while only scale, automation, and premium experience survive the margin collapse.
