A · The core banks consolidate

The Wall Street Machine

Capital markets consolidate into a few hands—and the rails, data, and custody layer replace the traditional middleman.

Wall Street isn't shrinking. In 2026, US investment banking alone is a $219.3 billion industry that's growing 7.6% a year. But it's consolidating: fewer people are doing more deals, boutique firms are eating the lunch of giants, and the real value—custody, settlement, and the plumbing—is moving from the corner office to the tech stack. The machines and the data are winning.

What changed

In 2025, the M&A machine delivered $4.9 trillion in deals—the second-highest volume on record, up 41% from 2024. Investment banking fees hit $100 billion for the year, and Morgan Stanley's IB revenue jumped 47% while JPMorgan's advisory fees were up 12%. On paper, Wall Street is booming. But behind the deal bonanzas, the industry is automating away its people and outsourcing the high-touch work. Employment fell 1.1% in 2026 even as deal flow recovered, and margins compressed from 35% in 2022 to 30-35% today. Why? Competition on execution fees (especially in equities), regulatory costs, and AI infrastructure investments all bite into profits. Meanwhile, the boutiques—Houlihan Lokey, Moelis, and smaller advisory shops—grew 18% to 28% in 2025, partly because they don't have to carry the capital-intensive trading and compliance burden of the Big Five.\n\nThe real action, though, is in custody and settlement. The custody, asset, and securities services industry is only $46.1 billion and growing at just 1.9% in 2026, but this layer—where assets are actually held and moved—is where the margin and the control live. Four dominant banks (BNY Mellon, State Street, JPMorgan, and Fidelity) now dominate this space. Global custody fees are under intense pressure, but the firms that own the rails are forcing clients to accept lower fees on the settlement side in exchange for higher fees on new services: blockchain custody, compliance AI, risk analytics. Digital assets are the wedge. Crypto custody is projected at $834 billion globally in 2026, and the OCC permitting national banks to offer crypto custody in March 2025 and approving five new digital-asset bank charters in December 2025 means the plumbing for digital wealth is becoming native to the traditional system. BNY Mellon and State Street both launched crypto custody platforms in May 2025. In July 2025, State Street became the first third-party custodian to settle tokenized debt on JPMorgan's blockchain, turning custody into a programmable layer. The SEC's Custody Rule Modernization, planned for April 2026, will require explicit provisions for crypto assets and updated identity verification for digital instruments—a compliance overhaul that costs money to implement but cements the advantage of scale.\n\nWhat's scarce now is not capital—there's plenty of capital. What's scarce is the data about who owns what, the real-time ledgers that tell you what's moving, and the rails that move it. Those are expensive to build and impossible to replicate. Bank of America cut junior investment banking staff in March 2025, and Bridgewater Associates cut 90 employees (7% of its workforce) in January 2025, both signaling that the firms no longer need human intermediaries for the routine work of moving capital around. The machine is faster and cheaper. What remains valuable is either the brand and the client relationships (for the boutiques) or the data and the plumbing (for the custodians and settlement layer). The rest gets automated, offshored, or squeezed into margin compression.

The evidence

  • Investment Banking & Securities Dealing: $219.3 billion market in 2026, with $4.9 trillion M&A volume in 2025 and boutique advisory firms (Houlihan Lokey, Moelis) growing 18-28% while Big Five rationalize headcount despite deal recovery
  • Custody & Asset Services: $46.1 billion market expanding to crypto, with State Street settling tokenized debt on blockchain in July 2025 and four dominant banks (BNY Mellon, State Street, JPMorgan, Fidelity) controlling the plumbing
  • Crypto Custody: $834 billion globally in 2026, with OCC approving five new digital-asset bank charters in December 2025 and traditional custodians (BNY, State Street) launching digital platforms in May 2025
  • Employment Rationalization: 1.1% job cuts in 2026 despite strong deal pipelines, with Bank of America cutting junior IB staff in March 2025 and Bridgewater Associates cutting 90 employees (7% of workforce) in January 2025
  • Margin Compression: Investment banking margins fell from 35% in 2022 to 30-35% in 2026, driven by competition on execution fees, regulatory costs, and AI infrastructure investment

Who rides it

The winners are the custodians, settlement layer operators, and boutique advisory firms. BNY Mellon, State Street, JPMorgan, and Fidelity now control the rails that move trillions. They can charge lower fees on routine custody but higher fees on new services (blockchain, compliance AI, risk analytics). Boutique firms like Houlihan Lokey and Moelis win because they can operate without capital-intensive trading operations, meaning higher returns on capital and no need to rationalize staff. They're lean, focused, and expensive for clients—which is exactly what works when deals are complex and margins are tight elsewhere.

Who's squeezed

Junior investment bankers and traditional regional custodians get squeezed. Banks like Bank of America cut junior staff in March 2025, and employment fell 1.1% in 2026 despite robust deal flow—the message is clear: machines do the work humans used to do. Regional custodians and smaller settlement firms can't compete on cost or scale with the Big Four, so they either get bought or starve. Bridgewater Associates cutting 7% of its workforce signaled that even top-tier private wealth firms can't support the old staffing model. The middlemen—brokers, back-office processors, compliance officers working on routine tasks—are being systematically replaced.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is concentration of power. Four banks now dominate custody and settlement—BNY Mellon, State Street, JPMorgan, and Fidelity. They control the rails that trillions of dollars flow through every day. If one of them has a technology failure, a regulatory problem, or a security breach, it cascades across the entire system. The SEC's Custody Rule Modernization planned for April 2026 will put more compliance burden on these firms, but it also cements their advantage over smaller players because they have the scale to absorb the cost. What looks like progress (blockchain custody, automated settlement) is actually consolidation of power.

In one sentence

The Wall Street machine in a nutshell

Wall Street isn't disappearing—it's consolidating: fewer people, fewer firms, and total control of the plumbing that moves capital.
