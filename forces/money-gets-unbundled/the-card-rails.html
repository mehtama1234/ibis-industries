The rails that tax every swipe

The payment networks have become an unavoidable tax on the entire consumer economy.

Every card swipe costs the merchant something—a percentage that goes to the card network, not to the bank. That infrastructure has become a chokepoint. In 2025 and 2026, the payment rails are stronger than ever, but the game is changing: regulatory cracks are opening, alternatives are scaling, and the players who built the rails are starting to fracture.

What changed

The credit card issuing business faces contradictions that reveal the true stress on the payment system. Card issuers in the US controlled a $178.9 billion market in 2026, barely growing at 0.3% annually, and their profitability has collapsed—return on assets fell from 5.9% in 2022 to just 2.8% in 2024, even as they charged consumers 21.5% APRs on average by February 2026. They're extracting more interest from borrowers, but they're also forced to hold massive loss provisions as serious delinquency rates hit 13.1% in Q1 2026, the highest in 15 years. Total revolving credit reached $1.25 trillion, but default risk is rising faster than revenue. The top five issuers—Chase, Amex, Citi, Capital One, and Bank of America—now control over 50% of the market. Capital One's $35 billion acquisition of Discover, which closed in May 2025, reshaped the competitive landscape and accelerated consolidation. What this means: issuing is becoming safer for giants but riskier for everyone else.

But issuing is just one layer. The processing rails themselves—Visa, Mastercard, and a handful of processors handling the transaction flow—sit deeper in the money flow. The credit card processing and money transferring market reached $87.4 billion to $208.57 billion in 2026 (depending on how you count the global scope), growing at 11 to 20% annually, with margins holding at 21-23% in 2025. That is the real chokepoint. Merchants paid a record $198.25 billion in fees in 2025, which is a 3-4% tax on every card sale in the economy. Visa and Mastercard are still fortified, but they face genuine threats: antitrust pressure on interchange fees, the August 2025 court vacatur of the Federal Reserve's debit interchange cap (Regulation II, now stayed pending appeal in early 2026), fragmented state-level regulation, and merchant fee revolt. Meanwhile, PayPal and Stripe are consolidating. In July 2026, Stripe and Advent International jointly bid approximately $53 billion for PayPal, with Polymarket pricing an 82% acquisition probability. If that closes, it creates a mega-player that rivals Visa and Mastercard in scale and threatens the rail-owner's pricing power.

The rails also face cannibalization from the bottom. Buy now, pay later platforms captured $70 billion in transactions in 2025, stealing share from credit cards, especially from Gen Z. FICO launched FICO Score 10 BNPL and Score 10 T in 2025, incorporating BNPL payment history into credit scores for the first time, signaling that the payment rails no longer own the definition of creditworthiness. The BNPL market reached $560.1 billion in 2025, and regulation is expected in 2026 that will require BNPL providers to conduct affordability assessments and seek full authorization—making them more like credit cards but with lower margins. At the same time, fintech funding surged 23% year-over-year to $28.6 billion globally in H1 2026, with AI-enabled fintechs capturing 23% of funding in Q3 2025, the highest share since Q4 2023. Major rounds like Plaid's $575 million in April 2025 (valued at $8 billion by February 2026) show that the payment stack is being disaggregated. What made the rails valuable is the same thing that's becoming risky: they sit on the flow of money, they know everything, and they take a cut. That cut is becoming a political and regulatory target, and real-time payments are finally becoming real.

The consolidation and regulatory pressure have made one thing scarce: the margins that used to buffer payment processors. Five thousand to five thousand nine hundred establishments provide credit card processing and money transfer services as of 2025, and they employ 183,000 to 190,000 people in the US, many at $17.99 per hour entry-level. That workforce is being automated away. Labor costs, fraud losses (synthetic identity fraud alone costs $3.1 billion annually as of 2026 and is the fastest-growing financial crime, fueled by AI deepfakes), and regulatory compliance burdens are compressing margins from both ends. The businesses that survive are consolidating into regional mega-processors or becoming specialists in vertical niches. What is new is the speed of the reshaping: the Discover-Capital One deal, the PayPal-Stripe bid, the Regulation II court vacatur, the BNPL rule-making, all happened within 13 months. The rails that used to feel permanent are becoming tactical.

The evidence

  • Credit card issuing (Finance): $178.9 billion market in 2026 with margins declining to 2.8% ROA (2024), despite 21.5% average APRs, as Capital One's May 2025 Discover acquisition consolidates the top five issuers to 50%+ market control.
  • Payment processing (Finance): $87.4 billion to $208.57 billion market in 2025-2026 growing at 11-20% annually, with merchants paying record $198.25 billion in fees (2025), but Visa-Mastercard facing antitrust pressure and the Stripe-PayPal $53 billion bid (82% probability, July 2026) reshaping the competitive hierarchy.
  • BNPL platforms (Consumer Finance): Captured $70 billion in 2025 transactions, stealing from credit cards; FICO Score 10 BNPL (2025) now incorporates BNPL history into credit scores; market reached $560.1 billion in 2025.
  • Fraud costs (Finance & Technology): Synthetic identity fraud hit $3.1 billion annually by 2026, the fastest-growing financial crime fueled by AI deepfakes, forcing issuers to tighten underwriting and set aside loss provisions.
  • Regulatory fragmentation (Finance & Government): August 2025 court vacatur of Federal Reserve's debit interchange cap (Regulation II) stayed pending Eighth Circuit appeal; BNPL regulation expected 2026; states like California and New York enforcing their own rules—creating three-tier competitive chaos.

Who rides it

Visa, Mastercard, and mega-consolidators like Stripe-PayPal (if the deal closes) win. They own the rails and the data, and even as margins compress, their scale lets them absorb fraud losses and regulatory costs that smaller processors cannot. Fintech funding surged 23% to $28.6 billion globally in H1 2026, with AI-enabled fintechs capturing 23% of funding in Q3 2025—but only those building real-time payment alternatives, embedded finance, and vertical stacks with their own data moat. Issuers who can absorb delinquency (Capital One, Chase) also benefit because they can cross-sell other products. What they all have in common: they control the chokepoint, they know the customers, and they can turn that knowledge into adjacent revenue.

Who's squeezed

Regional banks, small processors, and traditional credit card middlemen get squeezed. The five thousand to five thousand nine hundred establishments providing payment processing are being automated and consolidated. Merchants are the biggest losers—they pay 3-4% in fees on every transaction (a record $198.25 billion in 2025) and have few alternatives as long as credit cards remain the dominant payment method. BNPL platforms lose too, as regulations roll in requiring affordability checks and full authorization, turning them into credit card competitors with lower margins and higher friction. Card issuers outside the top five face collapsing ROA and delinquency risks as consumers carry $1.25 trillion in revolving debt and default rates hit 15-year highs—the math doesn't work unless you're already big enough to absorb losses or ruthless enough to exit the space.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Synthetic identity fraud is accelerating (costs $3.1 billion annually as of 2026 and growing faster than any other crime) and is now fueled by AI deepfakes. Issuers and processors are locked in an underwriting arms race—they can't stop it at the scale of the payment system, only push costs downstream onto consumers (higher rates, stricter approval) or force merchants to absorb fraud-prevention burdens. If AI-generated identity documents become indistinguishable, the entire credit card issuing model—which relies on verifying identity once, then trusting the risk—breaks down. The payment rails have no way to solve this alone; it requires government identity infrastructure and coordination across banking and law enforcement that doesn't exist yet.</worry> </invoke>
