B · Credit leaves the bank

Lending without a bank

Credit routes around traditional banks through brokers, fintechs, and nonbank servicers—and the middlemen are getting crushed.

Lending has left the building. Online platforms, embedded-finance companies, and nonbank lenders now move credit directly to borrowers, bypassing the loan broker entirely. The traditional bank is no longer the gatekeep—it's just one option among many, and a shrinking one at that.

What changed

For decades, a mortgage broker was the middleman between borrower and lender. You wanted a loan, you paid them a fee, they found you a bank. That model is dead. Online originations reached 45-47% market share by 2025-2026, and direct-to-consumer platforms like Rocket Mortgage have made the broker economically optional. Fewer people need intermediation when they can get a rate quote in five minutes on their phone. The loan broker industry fell 17.1% from 2021 to 2026 (from $11.5 billion to $9.1 billion), and employment in the sector has crashed 39% off its peak. Yes, margins ticked up in 2024-2025—independent mortgage banks averaged 21 basis points per loan in 2025 (up from 10 bp in 2024) and made $727 per loan in Q1 2026—but that's a survival rebound, not a growth story. The real action is happening at the extremes: either you're large enough to compete with direct lenders (United Wholesale Mortgage originated 422,120 loans worth $164.3 billion in 2025), or you're being rolled up into someone else's platform. Guild Mortgage sold to Bayview Asset Management in November 2025. Cenlar was acquired by Pennymac. There were 62 M&A deals in 2025, up from 37 the year before, mostly small independents realizing they have no succession plan and no moat.\n\nInvoice factoring tells a parallel story, with a twist. Factoring is also shrinking overall—the US market is $3 billion in 2026, down from $3.9 billion in 2022 (declining 1.9% in 2025 alone). But within that shrinking pie, two distinct bifurcations are happening. First, transportation factoring survives because trucking has structural cash-flow problems. The 2023-2024 freight recession drove spot rates below operating costs, forcing weak carriers out and driving survivors into factoring. Yet total invoice volumes still fell 5.7% year-over-year by December 2024. Interest rates were the swing factor: when rates were elevated through 2024, factoring became competitive against bank loans. But as the Federal Reserve cut rates after May 2024, that advantage evaporates. Factoring rates are now squeezing into a 1.95-4.0% range for trucking. Second, fintech and embedded-finance platforms are picking off the high-growth segments. AI-powered credit scoring and fraud detection are table-stakes now, and platforms like Lendflow bundle factoring with loans and merchant services to reach small businesses that never would have called a traditional factor. Global embedded finance is projected to hit $155.96 billion in 2026, with factoring as a core component. The industry forecast expects global invoice factoring to grow 10.5% CAGR from 2024-2034, reaching $7.75 billion, contradicting the US-only decline—meaning growth is happening everywhere but the US, and it's being captured by fintech, not traditional factors.\n\nBut the biggest story is loan servicing, where nonbanks have become the actual backbone of the mortgage market. Nonbanks now control 66% of all mortgage servicing as of early 2024, and 84% of Ginnie Mae-backed loans. This is not a niche. When Rocket Companies closed its $14.2 billion acquisition of Mr. Cooper Group in October 2025, it created a 2.1 trillion combined origination-and-servicing powerhouse with 10 million customers. The deal was explicitly about vertical integration: fusing the nation's largest lender with the nation's largest servicer to capture $500 million in annual synergies. PennyMac, another nonbank, added 740 billion in subservicing relationships when it acquired Cenlar in March 2025. H1 2025 mortgage originations hit 2.3 trillion (later revised to 2.1 trillion), driving servicing portfolio growth of 33-57% year-over-year for major nonbanks. The technology shift is accelerating the consolidation: 38% of mortgage lenders used AI/ML in 2024, jumping to 83% planning GenAI budget increases in 2026. AI now automates borrower monitoring, default prediction, payment processing, and compliance—the very jobs that once justified having dozens of regional servicers. The regulatory environment is shifting too. The CFPB finalized a streamlined servicing rule in September 2025 that eliminated the complex "complete application" framework, reducing compliance burden but raising expectations for borrower assistance. Meanwhile, regulators are recommending higher capital and liquidity standards for nonbanks, which favors the scaled players (Rocket, Mr. Cooper, Lakeview with $738 billion UPB as of July 2025) over small independent servicers.\n\nWhat's made scarce here is the independent intermediary—the small loan broker, the regional factor, the single-state servicer. What's become cheap is origination (online channels and direct banks have commoditized the rate), underwriting (AI delivers non-QM decisions in under five minutes, removing human expertise), and servicing (automation and scale). What's newly risky is being a nonbank without enough scale to absorb regulatory compliance and interest-rate swings—FDIC consent orders against BaaS sponsor banks in 2024-2025 have raised compliance costs for embedded-finance platforms. What's valuable is data (the 10 million customers Mr. Cooper gets access to via Rocket), technology (AI-powered credit scoring and compliance), and the rails themselves (if you own the platform, you own the borrowers).

The evidence

  • Loan Brokers (Finance & Insurance): Market fell 17.1% CAGR from 2021-2026 (from $11.5B to $9.1B); employment down 39% from peak; online originations captured 45-47% market share by 2025-2026, displacing intermediaries; United Wholesale Mortgage originated 422,120 loans ($164.3B) in 2025 but faced direct competition from Rocket Mortgage.
  • Invoice Factoring (Finance & Insurance): US market contracted from $3.9B (2022) to $3.0B (2026), down 1.9% in 2025 alone; transportation segment remains resilient despite freight recession (spot rates fell below operating costs in 2023-2024); invoice volumes fell 5.7% YoY by Dec 2024; embedded-finance platforms bundling factoring with loans projected global embedded finance to reach $155.96B by 2026.
  • Mortgage Servicing (Finance & Insurance): Nonbanks now control 66% of all servicing (early 2024) and 84% of Ginnie Mae-backed loans (mid-2024); Rocket-Mr. Cooper merger ($14.2B, October 2025) created 2.1 trillion combined origination-servicing platform with $500M annual synergies; H1 2025 originations hit 2.3 trillion, driving 33-57% YoY servicing portfolio growth for major nonbanks.
  • AI Adoption in Lending (2024-2026): 38% of mortgage lenders used AI/ML in 2024; 83% planned GenAI budget increases in 2026; AI now automates underwriting (non-QM decisions in under 5 minutes), borrower monitoring, default prediction, and compliance—eliminating the human expertise that once justified broker networks.
  • Regulatory Environment (2024-2025): CFPB September 2025 streamlined loss-mitigation rule eliminated complex 'complete application' framework; FDIC consent orders against BaaS sponsor banks (2024-2025) raised compliance costs for embedded-finance platforms; regulators recommending higher capital and liquidity standards for nonbanks, favoring scaled players.

Who rides it

The winners are platform companies with scale and data: Rocket Companies (merged with Mr. Cooper to get 10 million customers), PennyMac (now controlling 659 billion UPB after acquiring Cenlar), Lakeview Loan Servicing (738 billion UPB as of July 2025), and embedded-finance startups like Lendflow that bundle multiple products (loans, factoring, merchant services) into one API. They win because they own the customer relationship, control the technology stack (AI automation, real-time credit scoring), and can absorb regulatory and interest-rate swings. Online lenders win. Direct-to-consumer channels win. Anyone with a platform and 100+ million dollars in scale wins.

Who's squeezed

Regional brokers and independent servicers get squeezed into irrelevance or sold off. The 46,000-52,000 broker employees (down 39%) are consolidating into larger firms or leaving the industry. Independent factors survive only in transportation, where structural cash-flow problems keep borrowers coming, but general commercial factoring is eviscerated by cheaper bank lending and fintech platforms offering the same service embedded in a broader product. Small regional servicers can't compete on technology, compliance, or customer acquisition—they're being rolled up (Guild Mortgage to Bayview, Cenlar to PennyMac) or shuttered.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that nonbanks have become too central to mortgage origination and servicing (66% of servicing, 84% of Ginnie Mae loans) without the same regulatory backstop or capital buffers as banks. When interest rates swing or markets freeze, nonbanks don't have access to the Fed's discount window or stable deposit funding. The CFPB and regulators are tightening capital and liquidity standards, and FDIC consent orders have already raised compliance costs. If servicing liquidity crises recur—especially during forbearance periods—the scaled nonbanks could face systemic pressure that ripples through the entire mortgage market, and there's less redundancy now that the small regional players are gone.

In one sentence

Lending without a bank in a nutshell

Credit has left the bank and gone to platforms, fintechs, and scale players, leaving behind a graveyard of regional brokers, independent servicers, and traditional factors.
