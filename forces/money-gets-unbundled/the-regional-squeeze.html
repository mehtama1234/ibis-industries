D · The tensions

The regional-bank squeeze

When deposits get expensive and tech costs soar, the math turns against smaller banks — and forces them out.

Regional and community banks face a three-part squeeze in 2025-2026. The Fed's 1.75 percentage-point rate cuts from September 2024 through Q4 2025 have compressed the margins they rely on, uninsured deposits surged $214.7 billion in Q4 2025 alone as depositors shop for rates, and a $1.26 trillion commercial real estate maturity wall looms by 2027. Meanwhile, the technology and compliance costs required to compete with big banks and fintechs have become fixed overhead only scale can spread across.

What changed

The commercial banking industry grew 9.2% CAGR from 2021 to 2026, reaching $1.5 trillion in revenue (2026), but that aggregate masks a brutal bifurcation. Net interest margins recovered to 3.39% by Q4 2025, the highest since 2019, but that peak is reversing. The Fed's rate cuts have compressed deposit costs for big banks with scale and network effects. Smaller banks cannot follow suit without hemorrhaging uninsured depositors. In Q4 2025 alone, uninsured deposits surged $214.7 billion, a flight toward safety and better rates. For regional banks with less than $50 billion in assets, deposit shopping is existential because they lack the scale to replace them.

The commercial real estate crisis is the second pressure. Banks are holding $2.7 trillion in CRE exposure, and $1.26 trillion in CRE loans will mature by 2027. Those loans, many originated at 4.3% when rates were stable, are refinancing at 6.2%—a 190 basis-point jump that bleeds borrower equity. Delinquencies hover near decade highs, forcing regional banks into loss reserves and capital draws. Big banks can absorb these hits because CRE is one line item in a diversified portfolio. For a $10 billion regional bank with 30% of loans in CRE, the math is catastrophic.

The third squeeze is technology and regulatory spend. Credit unions hit record net income of $18.8 billion in 2025, but that tailwind is ending. Large credit unions with $500 million-plus in assets are consolidating aggressively and pouring capital into AI, cybersecurity, and digital banking. Loan delinquency hit a 12-year high of 1.02% in Q4 2025 across credit unions, constraining profitability growth. Smaller credit unions face 539 cyber incidents in the past 12 months and must invest in fraud detection or lose members to big banks and fintechs like Robinhood and Rocket Mortgage that offer seamless digital experiences and faster approvals. The cost to remain competitive has become a fixed burden that only scales across a large member base or deposit book.

The result: consolidation by force. Credit union federally insured count fell from 4,455 to 4,287 in one year (Q4 2024–Q4 2025). Navy Federal, the world's largest credit union, crossed $203.6 billion in assets and added 826,000 members in one year (6% YoY growth), while smaller players merge into larger ones. First Technology ($17B) merged into Digital Credit Union ($12.7B); Wings Financial ($9.5B) merged into Ent Credit Union ($10.3B); CommunityAmerica ($5.3B) merged into Unify Financial ($3.5B). What was once a network of local credit unions is becoming a pyramid with a few giants at the top.

The evidence

  • Commercial banking: $1.5 trillion market size (2026), 9.2% CAGR (2021-2026), but compressed margins as Fed rate cuts ease and CRE refinancing crisis looms
  • Credit unions: $149.3 billion revenue (2026), but net income growth ending as rate cuts compress margins from 3.44% peak (Q1 2026) and delinquencies hit 12-year high of 1.02% (Q4 2025)
  • Consolidation: Credit union federally insured count fell from 4,455 to 4,287 (one year), with Navy Federal adding 826,000 members at 6% growth and major mergers of equals announced (First Tech+Digital, Wings+Ent, CommunityAmerica+Unify)
  • Deposit flight: Uninsured deposits surged $214.7 billion in Q4 2025 alone, showing depositors shopping rates and fleeing to larger, safer institutions
  • CRE crisis: $1.26 trillion in commercial real estate loans mature by 2027 at 6.2% versus original 4.3%, while $2.7 trillion total CRE exposure sits on bank balance sheets and delinquencies hover near decade highs

Who rides it

The big four—JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citigroup—grow stronger in relative terms. They can absorb CRE losses because CRE is one line among diversified portfolios. They keep deposits because size and the implicit safety premium matter in a volatile environment. Navy Federal and other mega-credit-unions ($200B+ in assets) capture the consolidation windfall, adding scale, branch networks, and member bases that let them invest in AI and digital without breaking their margin math. Fintechs and embedded finance platforms capture the dissatisfied edge cases—speed and transparency matter more to small businesses and depositors tired of regional banks' slow digital tools and opaque pricing.

Who's squeezed

Regional banks ($10–50 billion in assets) face the squeeze. They can't outprice big banks for uninsured deposits. They can't diversify CRE risk fast enough. They can't invest in the tech stack required to retain younger, mobile customers. Community banks (sub-$1 billion) are disappearing into mergers or acquiescence. Smaller credit unions ($50–500 million in assets) face the same trap: tech costs and compliance burdens are fixed, scale is not. The 2023 banking failures (SVB, Signature, First Republic) proved mid-size banks could die in real time, killing confidence in the $10–50B tier. Depositors remember. The regional bank model—local lending, personal service, embedded community ties—cannot compete on either technology or deposit cost when centralized platforms offer both at scale.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is a cascading commercial real estate default wave if property values fall or interest rates spike unexpectedly. Banks are holding $2.7 trillion in CRE, and $1.26 trillion matures by 2027 at rates 190 basis points higher than original. If defaults rise sharply, regional banks will face capital crises simultaneously (deposit flight + loss reserves), triggering forced sales or FDIC seizures. Unlike the 2023 failures (which were deposit-flight events), a CRE wave would be asset-side, affecting every bank on the same clock. Large banks can absorb it; mid-size banks cannot. The second worry is that consolidation accelerates into a true credit vacuum—if regional banks fail too fast, small businesses and underserved communities lose access to lending, and credit becomes moated behind big-bank or fintech gatekeepers with their own credit scoring and underwriting biases.

In one sentence

The regional-bank squeeze in a nutshell

Rate cuts and deposit competition are crushing net interest margins for regional banks while tech investment demands and commercial real estate risk accelerate consolidation toward the giants.
