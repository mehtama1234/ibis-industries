Moving and Holding the Money

Brokerage, custody, and float are being reshaped by automation, scale, and the shift to digital assets—killing commissions and squeezing regional middlemen.

For decades, moving money and holding it safely were lucrative, quiet businesses. Brokers took a cut. Custodians charged fees. The float—the time your cash sat in transit—belonged to the bank. That model is breaking. AI now executes most trades. Cryptocurrency custody is forcing every major bank to build new infrastructure. Fee pressure is relentless. The businesses that win are those with scale, data, and direct access to the rails. Everyone else gets compressed.

What changed

The commodity dealing and brokerage industry in the US—the middlemen who execute trades and move money—has seen margins collapse from 14.3% in 2022 to 6.6% in 2025. That's not a temporary dip. It's structural. Pandemic-era chaos in 2020-2022 created wild volatility: crude oil crashed, agricultural futures spiked, currencies swung violently. Dealers made 31.5% more revenue in 2021 alone. But once economies stabilized in 2024-2025, that volatility evaporated. Spreads tightened. Commissions fell. Today, the US commodity industry generates $21.6 billion in annual revenue (2025), down from $24.3 billion in 2022, with only 2.2% annual growth and a negative 1.6% actual decline in 2025 itself. The human broker—the person who knew the market, could execute a big order without moving prices too much—is becoming obsolete. AI now handles 89% of world trading volume as of 2025. Machine learning algorithms execute thousands of trades per second. Hedge funds report that AI agents in commodities have cut slippage by 35%. When volatility returns, volume surges: commodity derivatives trading jumped to 12.49 billion contracts in 2025, up 17.5% from 2024, with options alone up 48%. CME Group averaged 28.1 million daily contracts across all products in 2025, up 6% year-over-year. But the money made per trade shrinks with every upgrade to the plumbing. Brent crude jumping from $72 to $118 per barrel in early Q1 2026 sustained hedging demand and trading volumes in energy derivatives, but not the explosive arbitrage spreads of 2020-2022. Geopolitical shocks—Middle East tensions, US tariffs on aluminum and steel (25% to 50% in 2025), Chinese trade tensions—keep markets unpredictable. This creates hedging opportunity. But it's opportunity split among thousands of automated traders, not concentrated in the hands of a few dozen elite brokers with exclusive market access.\n\nCustody and asset services tell a parallel story with a digital twist. This industry is larger and growing faster: $46.1 billion in 2026, up at a 3.7% CAGR since 2021. But the growth masks a concentration crisis and a forced reinvention. Four dominant banks—BNY Mellon, State Street, JPMorgan, Fidelity—control most institutional custody. Margins are still healthy at 26.9% in 2026, but fee pressure is relentless. Custody, by definition, is boring: hold the assets, keep them safe, settle transactions, file the paperwork. The money comes from volume and the float. But volume is being automated—blockchain-based settlement is getting faster. JPMorgan's blockchain-based Digital Debt Service (launched July 2025) enables institutional clients to settle tokenized debt securities with automated clearance, cutting the float to near-zero. The float, that quiet profit center, is disappearing. The industry is forced into a race to capture digital asset custody. Cryptocurrency custody was projected to reach $834 billion globally in 2026. Until March 2025, national banks couldn't legally hold crypto for clients. Then the OCC issued Interpretive Letter 1183, opening the door. By December 2025, the OCC had conditionally approved five new national trust bank charters for digital asset firms: Circle, Ripple, Paxos Trust Company, BitGo Bank & Trust, and Fidelity Digital Assets. This is a rare moment of regulatory permission—a green light to build. BNY Mellon and State Street both launched comprehensive digital asset custody platforms in May 2025. They're not waiting for the next crisis to prove digital assets matter. They're racing to embed themselves in the post-traditional custody stack.\n\nWhat changed is the definition of winning in these businesses. Scale, automation, and regulatory access to new rails matter more than relationship skill or discretionary trading talent. Approximately 8,000 commodity dealing and brokerage establishments exist in the US, employing 25,500 people (2025). That's down from higher numbers a decade ago. Consolidation is underway but not dramatic—yet. Regional commodity brokers, insurance-backed trading operations, and independent FCMs (futures commission merchants) are under pressure. They can't match the margin compression. They don't have the AI infrastructure. They can't afford the compliance build for crypto or blockchain. The custody industry has roughly 30,975 establishments (2026), which sounds diverse, but 76,500+ employees are concentrated in a handful of global giants. The SEC's Custody Rule Modernization, planned for April 2026, will mandate explicit provisions for crypto assets, new onboarding procedures, identity verification, and recordkeeping systems for digital instruments. Building these systems costs tens of millions. Smaller custodians can't afford the build alone. The result is a quiet reshuffling: smaller regional players, brokerage arms at community banks, and independent asset servicers get absorbed or marginalized. The scale players—JPMorgan, BNY Mellon, State Street, Fidelity—can amortize infrastructure investment across trillions in assets under administration. They become the only rational choice for institutional clients who need to hold crypto, tokenized securities, and traditional assets in one regulated place.

The evidence

  • Commodity dealing and brokerage: margins dropped from 14.3% (2022) to 6.6% (2025) as AI now executes 89% of global trading volume, cutting broker value and flattening per-trade economics.
  • Crypto custody: projected $834 billion globally in 2026; OCC approved five new digital asset bank charters in December 2025 and cleared national banks to offer crypto custody via Interpretive Letter 1183 in March 2025.
  • CME trading volumes: 28.1 million average daily contracts in 2025 (up 6% YoY), with commodity derivatives hitting 12.49 billion contracts (up 17.5% from 2024), but spreads tightening as AI automates execution.
  • Custody industry concentration: 30,975 establishments (2026) employ 76,500+ people but are dominated by four global giants (JPMorgan, BNY Mellon, State Street, Fidelity) preparing for blockchain-based settlement and digital asset mandates.
  • Geopolitical re-volatilization: Brent crude spiked from $72 to $118/barrel in Q1 2026; US tariffs on aluminum and steel (25-50% in 2025) created new arbitrage windows but not enough to restore 2020-2022 margin levels.

Who rides it

Banks with scale, global compliance infrastructure, and regulatory approvals for digital assets (JPMorgan, BNY Mellon, State Street, Fidelity). They can absorb the cost of crypto custody platforms, blockchain settlement systems, and SEC compliance overhauls. They become the only choice for institutions managing assets across traditional and digital rails. AI-native trading platforms and electronic exchanges like CME Group that have automated execution and built the infrastructure for high-frequency, low-margin volume trading. The rails themselves—CME Group, Nasdaq, Bloomberg—win because they own the plumbing and the data flow.

Who's squeezed

Regional commodity brokers and independent FCMs face margin compression (now 6.6%) and can't match AI capabilities, making them acquisition targets or marginalized players. Community bank asset servicers and smaller independent custodians get squeezed by regulatory complexity (SEC Custody Rule Modernization looming April 2026 mandates for crypto and blockchain) and can't afford the tens of millions required to build compliant digital asset infrastructure. Mid-market trading operations and relationships-based brokers who built careers on execution skill and market knowledge find that skill priced to near-zero by 10,000 competing algorithms.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is regulatory overshoot on digital asset custody. The OCC has approved five new digital asset bank charters (December 2025) and cleared national banks to offer crypto custody (March 2025), but the SEC's Custody Rule Modernization (planned April 2026) could impose compliance costs so severe that only the largest institutions can afford to serve this market. If the rules demand real-time blockchain auditing, continuous identity verification, or custodian-level regulatory capital buffers for digital assets, mid-market players are locked out entirely. This could starve the entire digital asset ecosystem of custody providers and push institutions to unregulated or offshore solutions, defeating the stated policy goal of bringing crypto into the regulated banking system.

Moving and holding the money in a nutshell
