Money Gets Unbundled

Finance splinters out of banks into rails, apps, and platforms; scale and data win, middlemen lose.

Banking is leaving the banks. What used to be a service only a bank could offer—moving money, lending, managing portfolios—is now embedded in payment apps, card networks, lending platforms, and robo-advisors. The same force that unbundled retail (Amazon), media (YouTube), and taxis (Uber) is now unbundling finance itself. The winners are platforms with scale, data, and control over the rails. The losers are regional banks, loan brokers, and traditional middlemen.

What changed

The US financial system is fragmenting into three layers. At the top, giants who control the rails or the data are consolidating power: Visa and Mastercard process $198.25 billion in merchant fees annually (2025), fortified against antitrust pressure by pivoting to data services and embedded finance. Stripe and PayPal are on the verge of merging at $53 billion (2026), a deal that signals the winner-take-most dynamic at play—only mega-players can afford the infrastructure, regulatory complexity, and talent to compete. BlackRock is buying its way into private markets dominance through acquisitions like HPS for $12 billion (2024) and Preqin for £2.5 billion (2025), because that's where the higher margins are as retail money flees high-fee active management. At the bottom, traditional banks and brokers are being squeezed from three directions at once. Commercial banks face a $1.26 trillion commercial real estate maturity wall by 2027, with loans originally at 4.3% now refinancing at 6.2%, while deposit costs stay sticky (uninsured deposits surged $214.7 billion in Q4 2025 alone). Margins are compressing despite the Fed's rate cuts of 1.75 percentage points through Q4 2025—the industry's net interest margin bottomed and is only now recovering to 3.39%, the highest since 2019, but the rebound is fragile. Regional banks that can't invest $50+ million in technology and compliance simply can't survive: the 2023 bank failures (SVB, Signature, First Republic) killed the mid-size consolidation wave temporarily, but the big four are now stronger relative to community banks than ever.\n\nLoan brokers are the clearest losers. The industry collapsed 17.1% from 2021 to 2026 (from $11.5 billion to $9.1 billion), with headcount down 39% from peak. Why? Because technology made them obsolete overnight. Online lenders and direct bank channels now handle 45-47% of mortgage originations (2025-2026), and the human broker's edge—understanding the loan process, navigating complexity—is being replaced by AI underwriting systems that deliver decisions in under five minutes. United Wholesale Mortgage originated 422,120 loans ($164.3 billion) in 2025, but it competes directly against Rocket Mortgage's slick consumer app, which brokers can't match. The few brokers that are profitable (earning $727 per loan in Q1 2026) are surviving only because they've become data aggregators or volume plays for lenders, not true intermediaries. Consolidation is the only exit: 62 M&A deals in 2025 (up from 37 in 2024), with Bayview acquiring Guild Mortgage in November 2025, are firms with no succession plan selling out.\n\nThe credit card and payment processing industry ($87.4 billion in 2026) is in the middle of a radical realignment. Merchants paid $198.25 billion in fees in 2025—a 3-4% tax on every card sale—so they are actively exploring alternatives: digital wallets, stablecoins, peer-to-peer rails. Visa and Mastercard face antitrust pressure on interchange fees and debit-routing regulations (the Federal Reserve's debit cap was vacated in August 2025, now pending appeal in 2026), yet they're adapting by offering data services and real-time rails that smaller processors can't match. Fintech funding surged 23% YoY to $28.6 billion globally in H1 2026, with AI-enabled fintechs capturing 23% of Q3 2025 fintech funding, but most of these startups are either niche players (handling BNPL or embedded payroll) or acquisition targets. BNPL alone reached $560.1 billion in 2025 and is now subject to affordability assessment rules (2026), pulling retail credit away from traditional card issuers but generating its own middlemen squeeze as regulations tighten. Finally, portfolio management ($340 billion revenue in 2025) is being hollowed out by passive index funds and ETFs, which captured 55% of mutual fund and ETF assets by 2025 (up from 34% a decade ago), while active mutual funds faced $1 trillion+ in net outflows in 2025. Only 38% of active funds beat their peers after fees (2025), so the survivors are either being acquired or specializing in alternatives and private markets—where margins are higher but scale is everything. Robo-advisors and hybrid advisory now manage $1 trillion in US assets (2026), with Vanguard Personal Advisor alone managing $300 billion, pulling retail clients away from traditional advisors.

The evidence

  • Commercial banking: net interest margins compressed by Fed rate cuts (1.75 pp through Q4 2025) while $1.26 trillion in CRE matures by 2027 at refinancing spreads of 380+ basis points wider (2025-2026)
  • Payment processing: merchants paid record $198.25 billion in card fees (2025), driving fee revolt and search for alternatives like stablecoins and peer-to-peer rails
  • Portfolio management: passive index funds and ETFs captured 55% of mutual fund and ETF assets (2025), up from 34% a decade ago, forcing active managers to consolidate or specialize in higher-margin private markets
  • Loan brokerage: industry declined 17.1% CAGR (2021-2026) as online originations reached 45-47% market share (2025-2026) and AI underwriting displaced human intermediation
  • Consolidation signals: $53 billion Stripe-PayPal merger bid (July 2026), BlackRock's $12 billion HPS acquisition (2024) and £2.5 billion Preqin deal (2025), 62 mortgage broker M&A deals (2025 vs. 37 in 2024)

Who rides it

Platforms with scale and control of the rails—Visa, Mastercard, the eventual Stripe-PayPal colossus, and passive index fund managers—are winning because they own the infrastructure that everyone else must use. BlackRock's consolidation into private markets wins because it owns both the data and the distribution. Regional banks and fintech platforms that can invest heavily in compliance, AI, and consumer experience also win, but only if they have capital and customers. Everyone else is a renter paying rent to the platform owner.

Who's squeezed

Regional banks are squeezed by deposit competition, the CRE maturity wall, and inability to invest in technology. Loan brokers are the clearest victims: 39% headcount reduction since peak, 17.1% revenue decline (2021-2026), and automation displacing their core value proposition. Traditional active managers are hemorrhaging assets to passive funds and robo-advisors. Traditional card processors and smaller payment networks can't compete against Visa/Mastercard's data and rail advantages and can't afford the consolidation price. Independent mortgage originators are disappearing into larger shops or going direct-to-consumer, eliminating the broker middleman entirely.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that as finance unbundles, it rebundles around giants who control the data and the rails, creating oligopolistic chokepoints and systemic fragility. Visa and Mastercard face antitrust pressure but survive because regulators can't easily break them up without breaking commerce itself. Stripe-PayPal at $53 billion (2026) creates a player nearly as powerful as a legacy network. When the next crisis hits—a spike in loan defaults as rates stay high, a commercial real estate collapse as the CRE maturity wall hits, or a run on uninsured deposits (which surged $214.7 billion in Q4 2025)—the concentration of risk in fewer, larger players could amplify rather than dampen contagion. Regional banks are weak and interconnected. Retail investors are chasing passive funds that own everything, amplifying drawdowns when markets fall. The rails are controlled by a handful of players who can deny access or raise fees whenever regulation weakens.

In one sentence

Money gets unbundled in a nutshell

Banking is fragmenting into data-driven platforms, with winners controlling the rails and middlemen—regional banks, brokers, and small processors—squeezed out by scale, automation, and consolidation.
