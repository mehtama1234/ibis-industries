Living and Dying by the Rate

When the Fed moves rates, entire lending middlemen either thrive or vanish.

A one-percent move in interest rates doesn't just shift what borrowers can afford. It rewires the entire lending supply chain — which middlemen survive, which ones get cut out, and which industries become suddenly valuable or instantly obsolete. For brokers and factors, the rate is not just a pricing input. It's whether they exist.

What changed

Mortgage brokers built their business on information advantage and access. They knew lenders that direct borrowers didn't. They got paid commissions for closing the gap. But in 2025-2026, as rates held near 6.5% and the volume pie shrank by an estimated 400,000 home sales nationally, that information advantage vaporized. Online originations now capture 45-47% of the market (2025-2026), and platforms like Rocket Mortgage took the broker out of the loop entirely. At the same time, AI underwriting — non-QM systems now issuing decisions in under five minutes — removed the human expertise brokers once charged for. The math is brutal. Independents mortgage banks earned $443 per loan in net production profit in 2025, then $727 per loan in Q1 2026, a 64% jump. But that recovery masks a collapsing transaction count. The industry shrank from $13.2 billion in 2021 to $9.1 billion in 2026, a 17.1% decline over five years. Since 2021, employment fell 39%, from roughly 76,000 people to 46,000-52,000. The fix? Consolidation. M&A deals exploded to 62 in 2025, up 68% from 37 in 2024. Bayview Asset Management bought Guild Mortgage in November 2025. Pennymac bought Cenlar's servicing platform. Small independents realized they had no succession plan and no way to compete at scale. They sold or closed.

Invoice factors face a different rate squeeze but just as unforgiving. The 2023-2024 freight recession crushed transportation carriers, eroding working capital and driving demand for factoring. But when rates spiked in 2024, factoring became competitive against bank loans — factors charged 1%-4%, banks wanted 5%-6%. Then the Fed began cutting. As rates normalized toward 2025-2026, bank loans became cheaper again. Factors lost their rate-insulated advantage. Meanwhile, the market size was already broken. US invoice factoring sits at $3.0 billion in 2026, down from $3.9 billion in 2022. Growth was negative 1.9% in 2025 and negative 4.4% compounded annually from 2020-2025. The factoring company count fell to 247 (2025), and employment dropped from 5,254 (2022) to roughly 5,000-5,200 today. Yet there's a counterintuitive twist: global embedded finance is projected to hit $155.96 billion in 2026, and factoring is embedded as a core component. Platforms like Lendflow bundle invoice factoring directly into their APIs, selling it to SMBs who never touched a factoring house. The technology is becoming table-stakes — AI/ML for credit scoring, fraud detection, and workflow automation. But the old standalone factoring firms are margin-squeezed and oversupplied. Transportation factoring survives because freight delays and cash burn are structural; it's the general commercial factoring that bleeds to platforms. And regulatory headwinds make it worse. FDIC consent orders against BaaS sponsor banks in 2024-2025 raised compliance costs, slowing fintech partnerships and raising the cost floor for embedded-finance plays.

What changed across both industries is the same: the rate environment turned a margin business into a volume trap. When rates were high, brokers and factors could charge more and still be competitive. Volume was less crucial. But as rates normalized, the only way to scale margin was to reach new customers, automate processes, and shed overhead. Big platforms can do that. Solo brokers and regional factors cannot. The spoils go to whoever can embed lending into their existing product — a fintech with a customer base, a lending marketplace with data, a carrier with an open API. The margins don't disappear; they migrate to players with scale, data, and technology. The old gatekeepers lose the gate.

The evidence

  • Mortgage brokerage: Loan broker market contracted 17.1% from 2021–2026 (2026 size $9.1 billion), with online originations capturing 45-47% by 2025-2026 and employment falling 39% to 46,000-52,000 people.
  • Mortgage consolidation: M&A activity in mortgage banking accelerated to 62 deals in 2025 vs. 37 in 2024, with major acquisitions like Bayview's purchase of Guild Mortgage (November 2025) reflecting a squeeze on independents.
  • Invoice factoring: US invoice factoring declined 4.4% CAGR from 2020-2025, ending at $3.0 billion in 2026, with the factoring company count at 247 and employment roughly 5,000-5,200 (down from 5,254 in 2022).
  • Freight recession impact: Invoice volumes fell 5.7% year-over-year by December 2024 as the 2023-2024 freight crisis eroded carrier working capital and pushed spot rates below operating costs.
  • Embedded finance shift: Global embedded finance market projects $155.96 billion in 2026, with platforms like Lendflow bundling factoring into APIs and reaching SMBs who bypassed traditional factors.

Who rides it

Big platforms with embedded lending win. Fintech APIs bundling factoring, loan matching, and underwriting reach scale that solo brokers and regional factors cannot. Online lenders like Rocket Mortgage win. They own the customer, own the data, own the rate. And the banks win — when rates normalize, direct bank lending becomes the cheapest option, and brokers have no reason to exist. Technology and data become the scarce resource; middleman relationships become the cheap commodity.

Who's squeezed

Regional loan brokers and independent factors get crushed. They lose volume to platforms and banks. Their margin shrinks as rate volatility subsides. They can't automate fast enough to stay competitive. Consolidation is their only exit — sell to a bigger firm or close. Employment evaporates. Mortgage brokerage shed 30,000 jobs in five years. Factoring companies are shrinking their payrolls quietly. The small, independent, human-driven lending business has no economic moat left.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that rate volatility actually disappears for a long period. When rates are stable and predictable, there is no reason for a broker or factor to exist at all. A borrower with perfect information (which the internet provides) and a bank with the best rate wins every time. If the Fed manages to anchor rates in a boring, low-volatility band for the next three years, the remaining factoring and brokerage capacity becomes purely redundant. That is when the final wave of consolidation and job loss hits — not a gradual squeeze, but a sudden collapse once survival mode ends and optimization begins.

In one sentence

Living and dying by the rate in a nutshell

Brokers and factors only survive when rate volatility gives them a margin cushion; when the Fed stabilizes rates, the middleman economics flip from viable to obsolete almost overnight.
