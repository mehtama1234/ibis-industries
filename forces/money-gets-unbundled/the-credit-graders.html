B · Credit leaves the bank

Who grades your credit

The credit-scoring layer is getting thinner, more AI-driven, and more concentrated.

Your creditworthiness used to be a black box. Now it's becoming a commodity—but whoever controls the score controls the gate. The credit bureaus and rating agencies that decide if you can borrow are caught between a crumbling regulatory system and AI-powered alternatives that measure you in new ways.

What changed

The US credit bureaus and rating agencies industry is worth $17.6 billion as of 2026, growing at just 2.7 percent annually since 2020, with 2025 itself seeing only 0.6 percent growth—a company walking on a treadmill. That sluggish top line hides a deeper fracture. Moody's, one of the Big Three, reported $7.7 billion in revenue in 2025 and grew operating income 17 percent year-over-year, but that performance depends on investment-banking work and derivatives pricing; the pure credit-rating business is under siege. The real disruption is coming from two directions at once. First, AI-powered alternative scoring is dismantling the traditional moat. Zest AI research showed its machine-learning model could approve 25 percent more loans while holding risk constant—a jaw-dropping efficiency gain that forces the incumbents to scramble. FICO, the gold-standard scoring firm, launched Score 11 in early 2026 explicitly to widen coverage by incorporating rent payments and cash-flow data. That move alone could bring 9 million previously credit-invisible consumers into the scoring system, diluting the scarcity value of a FICO number. Second, security is now a cost center, not a differentiator. The TransUnion breach in August 2025 exposed 4.4 million Americans' names, Social Security numbers, and birthdates. The 700Credit breach, discovered in late 2025, exposed 5.8 million more. Both incidents forced the Big Three to pour resources into cybersecurity, eroding the ~29 percent margins the industry has held stable for years. Compliance is tightening too: the SEC's proposed overhaul of rating-agency rules targets governance, analyst independence, and methodologies, and regulatory experts predict it will force hiring of compliance and technology staff that existing firms will struggle to absorb at current fee levels.

Meanwhile, the credit repair industry—the middleman layer that sits between consumer and bureau—is getting demolished. Lexington Law and CreditRepair.com, which once dominated via aggressive telemarketing, are zombies. Their parent company PGX Holdings emerged from Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2024-2025 with roughly 80 percent of operations shuttered. In August 2023, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau secured a $2.7 billion settlement against them for charging illegal upfront fees before delivering any results—a Telemarketing Sales Rule violation. That settlement came with $1.8 billion in consumer redress distributed to 4.3 million harmed consumers starting in December 2024. The old model is dead. What's replacing it is automation: modern platforms use machine learning to analyze credit reports, detect errors instantly, generate dispute letters in seconds, and predict which disputes will succeed—gutting the need for call centers and sales floors. The industry still has revenue—$5.29 to $6.6 billion in 2025, up from $4.4 billion in 2022—but employment plummeted 4.7 percent year-over-year as of 2026. About 29.6 percent of US consumers still carry subprime credit as of September 2025, so the market for repair services persists, but the jobs, the commissions, and the gatekeeping power of the repair firms are evaporating. In June 2026, the CFPB announced new rules to crack down on credit repair firms abusing complaint portals to remove legitimate negative information, closing off another escape hatch for the old business model.

The third piece—appraisers, credit counselors, and surveyors—reveals a parallel squeeze on human expertise. The US credit counseling, appraisal, and surveying industry hit $96.9 billion in 2025, up from $76.8 billion in 2022, but growth slowed to 1 percent in 2025 alone despite skyrocketing demand signals. Household debt reached $17.5 trillion by mid-2025; credit card debt alone hit $1.233 trillion. Bankruptcy filings surged 14 percent in 2025. Student loan delinquencies spiked to 7.74 percent in Q1 2025 (from under 1 percent in Q4 2024) as the pandemic pause on loan payments ended and reality resumed. Nonprofits reported onboarding 15,000 new debt-management clients in the first half of 2026—the highest half-year total since 2017. All of this should be a growth engine for appraisers and counselors, but it's not. Automated valuation models (AVMs)—software that estimates property value without a human appraiser—are eating volume. Starting October 2025, the OCC imposed federal quality-control standards on AVMs, which sounds like regulation but actually legitimized the technology by giving it a compliance framework. The mortgage market itself remains soft because interest rates stay elevated, capping appraisal volume. Appraisal utilization did begin to tighten in June-July 2026 (appraisal volume grew faster than active appraiser count for the first time since 2022), suggesting capacity constraints may eventually push pricing up—but for now, the humans are watching their workload flatten while the algorithms accelerate.

The evidence

  • Credit bureaus: Moody's (one of Big Three) reported $7.7 billion revenue in 2025 with 9% growth in analytics and 17% growth in operating income year-over-year, driven by investment-banking and derivatives work rather than pure credit rating.
  • Fintech scoring: Zest AI proved its ML model approves 25% more loans at constant risk; FICO launched Score 11 in early 2026 to expand coverage using rent and cash-flow data, targeting 9 million previously invisible consumers.
  • Credit repair: Market hit $5.29–6.6 billion in 2025 (up from $4.4B in 2022) but employment fell 4.7% YoY; Lexington Law and CreditRepair.com (80% of operations shuttered) are zombies after $2.7B CFPB settlement in August 2023.
  • Appraisals and counseling: $96.9 billion industry in 2025 (up from $76.8B in 2022) but only 1% growth in 2025 despite 14% surge in bankruptcies, 7.74% student loan delinquencies (Q1 2025), and 15,000 new debt-management clients in H1 2026 (highest since 2017).
  • Cybersecurity cost: TransUnion breach (August 2025, 4.4M exposed) and 700Credit breach (5.8M, July–October 2025) forced all three major bureaus to invest heavily in security, eroding stable ~29% margins and raising regulatory compliance burden (SEC overhaul of rating-agency rules underway).

Who rides it

The winners are scale and data. Moody's and the Big Three stay profitable by diversifying—investment banking, analytics, software platforms. TransUnion is pivoting: it acquired Monevo in April 2025 to plug instant pre-qualification into its OneTru cloud platform, capturing lead referrals and building a tech moat instead of a pure-bureau one. FICO, by launching Score 11 to incorporate rent and cash-flow data, is betting it can stay relevant by expanding its reach into populations traditional credit couldn't see. AI-powered alternative scoring platforms like Zest are winning by proving they can do better risk assessment with different data—a direct threat to incumbents but a huge win for lenders willing to adopt. On the counseling side, nonprofits are scaling to meet demand (15,000 new clients in H1 2026), and those leveraging automation for dispute letters and error detection are outpacing the old call-center model.

Who's squeezed

The squeezed are the middlemen and humans. Credit repair firms are collapsing: Lexington Law and CreditRepair.com went zombie after the CFPB settlement, and the June 2026 CFPB rules to close complaint-portal abuse shut off another escape hatch. The old telemarketing playbook is dead—80 percent of operations axed, thousands of jobs gone, employment down 4.7 percent even though total market revenue grew in dollars. Appraisers and human counselors face displacement: automated valuation models are now federally legitimized (October 2025 OCC standards), mortgage volume remains soft, and utilization is only now beginning to tighten after years of slack. Regional and independent credit bureaus—of which there were 1,280 establishments as of 2026—lack the scale or data to compete with Moody's, Equifax, and TransUnion, and they're getting squeezed out of M&A consolidation and data-sharing partnerships. Consumers also lose: every data breach (4.4M + 5.8M in 2025 alone) means their sensitive financial history is at risk, and the shift to alternative scoring data (rent, cash flow, digital payments) means no credit history is truly private anymore.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that credit scoring—the gate between you and capital—is concentrating into a smaller set of black boxes run by machine learning. Moody's, FICO, and TransUnion control the data and the models, and while regulation is coming (SEC overhaul of rating agencies, CFPB crackdowns on repair firms), it's reactive and slow. AI models are opaque: if Zest AI's model approves 25 percent more loans at constant risk, it's hard to know if that's a better model or if it's gaming incentives we haven't noticed yet. Breaches like the TransUnion (4.4M) and 700Credit (5.8M) incidents in 2025 show the bureaus can't reliably protect the data they hoard—yet lenders have no choice but to use their scores because everyone else does. The shift to alternative data (rent, cash flow) means the traditional notion of "credit invisible" people getting access is real, but it also means every digital footprint you leave is now fair game for scoring. And if AI-driven models start making lending decisions at scale, and those models are trained on historical data that encodes past discrimination, you've got a systemic amplification problem that neither regulation nor patch fixes can solve fast.

In one sentence

Who grades your credit in a nutshell

Credit scoring was a moat; now it's a commodity line managed by three big firms and disrupted by AI, while security breaches and regulatory pressure make it more expensive to run and everyone else gets squeezed.
