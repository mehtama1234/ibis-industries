The scale game in banking

Deposits, tech, and compliance costs push banking toward a few giants.

Banking used to be local. Your neighborhood bank held deposits, made loans, kept the spread. That's over. Today, the cost of staying in the game—compliance software, fraud detection, digital platforms, loan origination systems—is so high that only the giants and well-funded fintechs can afford to play. The result: money is flowing to scale, and everyone in the middle is getting squeezed.

What changed

The deposit game changed first. When the Federal Reserve cut rates 1.75 percentage points from September 2024 through the end of 2025, every bank suddenly faced the same math: deposit costs were sticky but shrinking, loan yields were falling, and the net interest margin—the spread banks live on—was compressed. For the big guys, that margin recovered to 3.39% by Q4 2025, the highest since 2019, because they could absorb the pressure with sheer volume. But in Q4 2025 alone, uninsured deposits surged $214.7 billion as customers shopped for better rates. Big banks could afford to pay up and keep that money. Smaller banks and credit unions couldn't. Meanwhile, that middle-of-the-road crowd was stuck with $1.26 trillion in commercial real estate exposure maturing by 2027, with many loans originally at 4.3% now having to refinance at 6.2%—a brutal math problem that weakened their credit quality and ate into capital reserves. Credit unions hit 1.02% loan delinquency in Q4 2025, a 12-year high, signaling real stress in those portfolios.

Technology became the moat. The U.S. commercial banking industry grew 9.2% annually from 2021 to 2026, reaching $1.5 trillion in size by 2026, but the margin story tells the real tale: 19.7% margins in 2025 for those who could grab business. Building the digital infrastructure to compete costs tens of millions. Loan origination systems, anti-fraud AI, cybersecurity stacks, mobile platforms—you can't cheap out on any of it. Navy Federal, the world's largest credit union, crossed $203.6 billion in assets by Q1 2026 and added 826,000 members in one year by investing aggressively in digital. Meanwhile, the number of federally insured credit unions fell to 4,287 from 4,455—consolidation accelerating because the smaller players couldn't build the tech. Mergers-of-equals are the survival strategy: First Technology ($17 billion) merged into Digital Credit Union; Wings Financial ($9.5 billion) merged into Ent Credit Union; CommunityAmerica ($5.3 billion) merged into Unify Financial. Each merger is a small bank throwing in the towel because the cost of staying independent and competitive was too high.

The charter game flipped, and that opened new competition at a different scale. Industrial banks—niche lenders that used to be dominated by Sallie Mae and Comenity—faced a restructuring when the Fed cut rates 75 basis points in 2025, compressing margins even further. Interest income is 75% of ILC revenue, so every rate cut is a direct hit. But in July 2025, the FDIC formally invited new applicants for industrial bank charters, a reversal after years of de facto closure. By January 2026, Ford Motor, GM, and Stellantis had conditional approval to start their own FDIC-insured banks. Each has one year to operationalize. That's not the future—it's happening now. These automakers can embed finance directly into dealer platforms and lending apps, cutting out the middleman bank. Fintech lenders are doing the same: Q3 2025 saw unsecured personal loan originations hit a record 7.2 million, with fintech capturing 42% of that market. The old ILCs—Sallie Mae, Comenity—are watching their deposit bases shrink and their margins compress with nowhere to hide. The industry is splitting: large, capital-rich parent-owned banks win; unprofitable smaller ones are being shut down or acquired.

Compliance and cybersecurity are now non-discretionary. The NCUA listed cybersecurity and fraud as top supervisory priorities in early 2026, after credit unions suffered 539 cyber incidents in the previous 12 months. Building the people and systems to defend against that—incident response teams, compliance specialists, regulatory reporting infrastructure—is a fixed cost that scales inversely with size. A community bank with $500 million in assets and 50 employees is paying the same regulatory burden per dollar of assets as a $50 billion bank with 5,000 employees. The math is brutal. Meanwhile, credit union net income jumped 31.5% in 2025 to $18.8 billion when rates were high, but that windfall masked the real problem: most of that gain went to large players who could invest in the defenses and digital tools needed to hold onto deposits and comply with an ever-thickening rulebook. Small and mid-size credit unions took those gains as profit and deferred modernization. Now, as the Fed cuts rates and deposit costs fall, they're left exposed.

The evidence

  • Commercial Banking (U.S., 2026): $1.5 trillion market size, 9.2% annual growth, 19.7% margins—but gains concentrate at the top four banks as smaller competitors lose deposit races and technology wars.
  • Credit Unions (U.S., 2025–2026): Navy Federal broke $203.6 billion in assets by Q1 2026 and added 826,000 members in one year through digital investment, while the number of federally insured credit unions fell to 4,287 from 4,455—signaling that only scale-capable players survive.
  • Industrial Banks (U.S., 2025–2026): Ford, GM, and Stellantis won FDIC conditional approval for their own banks in January 2026; fintech lenders captured 42% of record 7.2 million unsecured personal loan originations in Q3 2025—the old charter holders (Sallie Mae, Comenity) are being displaced.
  • Rate Compression (2024–2025): Fed cut rates 1.75 percentage points from September 2024 through December 2025, compressing deposit costs and squeezing smaller lenders; uninsured deposits surged $214.7 billion in Q4 2025 as customers fled to big banks offering better rates.
  • CRE Maturity Risk (2025–2027): $1.26 trillion in commercial real estate loans mature by 2027, many refinancing from 4.3% to 6.2%, straining regional and community bank capital—while big four banks have scale to absorb the stress.

Who rides it

The big four commercial banks (JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citigroup) win outright. They have the size to absorb deposit flight, the capital to invest in technology and compliance, and the margin floor to withstand rate cuts. Navy Federal and other scale-focused credit unions win too, because they aggressively invested in digital and could attract members with better rates and faster service. And fintech lenders win because they have no legacy baggage—they built on modern stacks from the start and can undercut traditional banks on speed and friction. Ford, GM, and the new industrial banks win because they can embed finance into their own products without paying a middleman.

Who's squeezed

Regional and mid-size banks get squeezed from both sides. They can't compete with the big four on deposits (rate-setting power) or with fintechs on speed and user experience. Smaller credit unions are consolidating or failing because they can't afford the technology stack—digital platforms, AI-driven underwriting, cybersecurity, regulatory reporting—needed to keep up. The old industrial bank model (Sallie Mae, Comenity) is in structural decline as automakers and fintechs grab their charters and customers. Community banks face a maturity wall in commercial real estate exposure that bigger banks can weather but they cannot. The 2023 bank failures (SVB, Signature, First Republic) killed the mid-size consolidation wave temporarily, but they also proved that the middle is fragile. The survivors are those with enough capital and deposit stickiness to outlast rate swings and big customers' shopping sprees.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that the commercial real estate maturity wall ($1.26 trillion by 2027, with many loans underwater or nearly so) triggers a wave of regional bank failures that cascades into the credit union system. Credit unions are already at a 12-year-high delinquency rate (1.02% in Q4 2025), and most of them have significant CRE exposure. If even a handful of large regional banks fail because they can't refinance their CRE books, it could spook depositors and trigger deposit flight from smaller institutions that can't absorb the shock. The second risk is concentration—if the big four keep getting bigger by absorbing deposits and smaller players keep disappearing, you end up with too-big-to-fail banks that carry systemic risk and have even less incentive to serve smaller businesses or lower-income borrowers. Neither outcome is in the public interest.

In one sentence

The scale game in banking in a nutshell

Smaller banks and credit unions can't afford the technology and regulatory infrastructure to compete, so deposits flow to the big four and fintech, leaving the middle to shrink.
