IP & Catalogs as Assets

When rights and back catalogs become yield-bearing financial instruments, scale wins and independents must choose: go big or go direct.

Capital is treating intellectual property—music copyrights, broadcast networks, sports licenses—as alternative assets with durable cash flows. When a song generates royalties for decades and a cable network's archive holds evergreen value, the financialization game changes: ownership becomes passive income, and whoever controls the catalog wins without lifting a finger. This shift reshapes who owns what and who keeps what they built.

What changed

Music publishing exploded into a 7 billion dollar sector in 2024 and is projected to reach 12.37 billion by 2026—a 5-7 percent annual clip—as streaming locked in 58 percent of publishing revenue by 2025. But the money concentrates at the top. BMG and Concord merged in early 2026 to form the fourth major player, ending the big-three monopoly and proving that scale is the only defensive move. Meanwhile, mechanical royalty rates hit an all-time high of 15.35 percent of platform revenue in 2026 (up from 15.2 percent in 2024), rewarding big publishers who can absorb the hit and squeeze out smaller outfits. Spotify pays 0.003 to 0.005 per stream; Apple Music, which shifted to a per-subscriber model in 2025, pays only for the tracks that subscriber actually listened to—meaning catalog depth matters more than ever. The indie workaround exists: in 2025, Spotify and the NMPA launched a direct-licensing portal so independent publishers could retain audiovisual sync rights and bypass blanket agreements, but adoption remains niche. The real consolidation play is AI. Warner and Suno struck a licensing deal; UMG and Udio settled in late 2025, establishing that AI training now requires rights clearance and that whoever owns the historical catalog owns the training data.

Cable TV is a mirror image of music, but inverted. The industry moved 127.5 billion in 2025, but that revenue is collapsing fast: cable subscriptions fell from 96.3 million in 2020 to 68.7 million by 2024, and Comcast alone lost 1.15 million TV subscribers in 2025 alone—roughly 3,500 per day. Advertising revenue crashed from 22.5 billion in 2024 to 20.2 billion in 2025, a 10 percent drop in one year, with further declines of 6-8 percent annually through 2027 expected. But here is the pivot: the cable networks themselves—the IP catalogs—are suddenly precious. Fox launched Fox One (20 per month) in August 2025 bundling live sports; ESPN launched ESPN Unlimited (30 per month) in the same month. Comcast announced in June 2026 that it would spin off NBCUniversal into a separate company called Versant Media, keeping the cable networks (USA, MSNBC, CNBC, E!, SYFY, Golf Channel) separate from the mom-and-pop cable distribution business. The cable operator—Charter and Cox merged in May 2025 into a 34.5 billion combined entity—is no longer a content company; it is a dumb pipe. The catalogs, the live sports rights, the evergreen franchises like Law & Order: this is the asset worth money now. Operating margins remain solid at 22 percent EBITDA, but absolute profit is evaporating because the revenue base is shrinking so fast. Only half of Americans watched cable in 2025, down from near-universal adoption a decade ago. Streaming now accounts for 47.5 percent of TV viewing vs. 20.2 percent for cable.

The pattern is identical in both industries: whoever owns the durable IP—the song, the network, the franchise—can monetize it across multiple platforms and time horizons. A 2026 song generates Spotify royalties, Apple Music payments (now subscription-based), YouTube sync fees, TikTok licensing fees, gaming sync fees, and potential AI training revenue. A cable network's archive can be licensed to streamers, sold to foreign markets, licensed for syndication, or repurposed as a bundle within a larger ecosystem. Scale lets you absorb the cost of rights clearance, negotiate with platforms from strength, and sustain your catalog across lean times. Independents cannot. A solo music publisher with a 10-song catalog cannot negotiate with Spotify or Apple on terms; they take the platform's standard rate or go nowhere. A regional cable network operator cannot launch its own DTC service and keep up with ESPN, Fox, and Paramount. The financialization of IP thus becomes a moat. Capital can buy up catalogs, hold them, and extract a slow, steady yield for decades. A founder who built a music catalog over 30 years can now sell it to a major publisher or a PE fund, take a single payout, and exit. The buyers—the big fours (now four major players in music after the BMG-Concord merge, down from six a decade ago) or the cable giants (now consolidating into mega-operators like Charter-Cox)—can service that catalog, clear rights for new uses, negotiate with platforms, and amortize the acquisition cost across a thousand revenue streams.

The evidence

  • Music Publishing (Media & Entertainment): Market reached 7 billion dollars in 2024 and is forecast at 12.37 billion by 2026, with streaming locked in at 58 percent of revenue by 2025 and mechanical rates hitting 15.35 percent of platform revenue in 2026.
  • Cable Networks (Media & Entertainment): US cable revenue stood at 127.5 billion in 2025, but cable advertising collapsed from 22.5 billion in 2024 to 20.2 billion in 2025 (10 percent decline annually), and subscriptions fell from 96.3 million to 68.7 million by 2024.
  • Music Publishing Consolidation: BMG and Concord merged in early 2026 to create the industry's fourth major player with a mid-term 1.2 billion dollar EBITDA target, ending decades of big-three dominance.
  • AI Licensing: UMG and Udio settled in late 2025 after 2024 lawsuits, establishing that AI companies must obtain licensing agreements for training on copyrighted material.
  • Cable Spinoffs: Comcast announced in June 2026 a spinoff of NBCUniversal (including Peacock, NBC broadcast, cable networks USA, MSNBC, CNBC, E!, SYFY, Golf Channel, and Sky International) into a separate public company called Versant Media.

Who rides it

The big four music publishers (now including the merged BMG-Concord entity) and the giant cable operators (Charter-Cox merged in May 2025 for 34.5 billion) win by owning catalogs that generate steady royalties and licensing fees forever. They can absorb rising mechanical rates, negotiate with Spotify and Apple from strength, launch DTC services (ESPN Unlimited at 30 per month, Fox One at 20 per month, both in August 2025), and refinance their IP holdings at lower rates than independents can access. PE funds win by buying catalogs cheap, holding them, and extracting yield over decades without operating the business.

Who's squeezed

Independent music publishers lose access to fair rates when 86 percent of available tracks on Spotify fall below the 1,000-stream threshold (imposed in 2024) and earn zero royalties; solo publishers cannot negotiate directly with Spotify or Apple and must accept platform standard rates or go to niche direct-licensing portals like the NMPA portal launched in 2025. Regional cable operators and mid-sized networks lose the ability to bundle and sell content independently; Comcast's spinoff of NBCUniversal in June 2026 signals that the cable operator business itself is no longer worth owning—only the content IP matters. Owner-operators who built catalogs over decades must choose: accept a one-time payout from a major player or struggle alone against platform consolidation.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that AI training data and copyright ownership remain legally unresolved even after the 2025 licensing deals between UMG-Udio and Warner-Suno. If courts rule that AI companies can use historical catalogs without licensing agreements, or if they rule that AI-generated output does not require rights clearance, the value of owning a historical catalog could evaporate overnight. Catalog owners would lose their primary financialization play. Alternatively, if mechanical royalty rates (now 15.35 percent in 2026) keep climbing due to songwriter advocacy, streaming platforms could respond by shifting payouts to algorithmic distribution or capping total songwriter payouts—collapsing the value of mid-tier catalogs while protecting only the blockbuster hits of the major players.

In one sentence

IP & catalogs as assets in a nutshell

PE and streaming platforms are bundling media IP into high-margin, long-lived revenue streams that smaller publishers and networks cannot service alone.
