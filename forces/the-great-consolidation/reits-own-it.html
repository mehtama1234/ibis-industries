D · Capital owns the assets

REITs own the ground

Institutional capital now owns the real estate, and owner-operators pay rent to finance their own displacement.

Capital is rolling up real estate the way it rolled up retail. Multifamily REITs are swallowing smaller landlords. Industrial warehouses cluster under a few mega-operators. Even land—the oldest, simplest asset—is consolidating into mega-communities run by professional managers. The pattern is everywhere: distress drives bankruptcies, bankruptcies feed acquisitions, and every downturn transfers ownership from operators to institutional capital.

What changed

The apartment market is the clearest case. For years, thousands of small operators ran 550,000 buildings worth $193.9 billion (2022). Rents climbed. Vacancy stayed tight. It was fragmented and profitable for anyone with three buildings and a willingness to work 70-hour weeks. Then supply caught up. Developers chased the boom in 2023–2025, and by Q2 2025 vacancy in luxury apartments hit 11.4%—forcing concessions instead of rent hikes. At the same time, financing got expensive. Multifamily loan delinquencies hit 1.37% in Q3 2025, the highest rate since 2008. That's when the weak operators broke. Pinnacle filed Chapter 11 in May 2025 with $500 million in mortgage debt. Sonder liquidated after Marriott cut its licensing deal. Summit Properties bought Pinnacle's 5,100 rent-stabilized NYC apartments in the bankruptcy auction. Meanwhile, two giant REITs—AvalonBay Communities and Equity Residential—announced a $69 billion merger closing in 2026 to create a 180,000-unit behemoth with $175 million in cost cuts promised in the first 18 months. That's not new efficiency. That's firing staff and renegotiating vendor contracts at scale. The small operator cannot do that with 50 units.

Industrial real estate moved even faster. The US commercial real estate sector is worth $1.7 trillion (2025) and growing 3.0–3.3% annually, but the growth is lopsided. Industrial delivered 940 million square feet of leasing in 2025—the second-best year on record—because e-commerce tenants need 50–70 million sq ft of warehouse space for every 1% increase in online sales. Those leases go to big, professionally managed regional and national portfolios. A mom-and-pop warehouse landlord in Ohio cannot compete with a REIT that owns 5 million sq ft and can offer $1 per sq ft cheaper because of its cost of capital. Office is cratering instead. National office vacancy peaked at 19.6% in Q1 2025 and only slowly improved to 17.7% by mid-2026 after 127 million sq ft was absorbed—but the damage was already done. Class B and C buildings are economically obsolete. Class A trophy assets remain desirable, but only in prime CBDs. The 90,300 office-to-apartment conversions that hit the market in 2026 (up 28% year-over-year) prove the point: individual building owners cannot afford the tens of millions to gut and convert. Big REITs and conversion specialists can. Small owners are walking away or selling into a buyer's market.

Then there's the financing crisis baked in. Refinancing is the hidden consolidator. $957 billion in commercial real estate loans mature in 2025 alone. Another $2.8 trillion mature through 2028. CMBS delinquencies reached 7.29% in 2025—a distress signal. Interest rates have not fallen enough to make refinancing painless. A small operator who locked in a $5 million loan at 3% in 2019 now faces a 2025–2026 refinance at 6.5–7%, doubling the debt service. A REIT with a $500 million portfolio and investment-grade credit can refinance at 5.5%—or raise capital at a lower cost on the public market. The small operator has no such option. They sell. The REIT buys.

Land leasing is the steady consolidator. The US land leasing market is $16 billion (2026), growing slowly at 0.6% CAGR from 2022–2026, but it is now accelerating. Occupancy rates sit near 95%. Rent growth was 7% year-over-year in 2025. That pricing power comes from a simple fact: conventional housing is unaffordable. A family cannot buy a home, so they rent. They cannot afford a rental apartment in a major metro, so they move to a 55+ community or an RV park or a manufactured home lot. Demand is real and sticky. But new community development is "extremely limited" due to zoning and regulatory friction. That means scarcity is built in. Incumbent operators—the large REITs like ELS (452 communities, 173,201 sites) and Sun Communities (5,000+ communities)—benefit from the moat. Smaller independent lot owners? They consolidate into regional chains or get acquired by the big REITs. M&A activity among smaller operators accelerated sharply in the first half of 2026. Washington state capped annual lot rent increases at 5% starting May 2025, and Florida and Virginia are forcing zoning reform—which creates uncertainty but does not change the consolidation pattern. It just means capital flows to operators large enough to absorb regulatory risk.

The evidence

  • Multifamily: AvalonBay-Equity Residential $69B merger (2025-2026) creates 180,000+ unit REIT; Pinnacle bankruptcy auction (May 2025, 5,100 NYC units); delinquencies 1.37% Q3 2025, highest since 2008.
  • Industrial warehousing: 940M sq ft leasing in 2025 (second-best year on record); e-commerce drives 50-70M sq ft demand per 1% online sales growth; consolidates under regional/national portfolios.
  • Office: Vacancy peaked 19.6% Q1 2025, improved to 17.7% mid-2026; 90,300 conversions in 2026, up 28% YoY; Class B/C buildings economically obsolete.
  • Commercial RE refinancing: $957B loans mature 2025; $2.8T through 2028; CMBS delinquencies 7.29% in 2025; small operators forced to sell into buyer's market.
  • Land leasing: $16B market (2026); 7% rent growth 2025; occupancy ~95%; M&A accelerating H1 2026; major REITs ELS (173,201 sites) and Sun Communities (5,000+ communities) consolidating independents.

Who rides it

REITs, regional consolidators, and well-capitalized conversion specialists. They have low cost of capital, can refinance at better rates, can absorb regulatory risk, and can achieve cost synergies across hundreds or thousands of properties. In multifamily, the $69 billion AvalonBay-Equity merger creates a behemoth that can undercut smaller operators by $1 per sq ft just on efficiency and scale. In industrial, the same dynamic holds: large regional portfolios win over fragmented mom-and-pop owners. In land leasing, REITs like ELS and Sun Communities consolidate the independents. The pattern repeats: institutional capital wins, professional management wins, scale wins.

Who's squeezed

Owner-operators with 50–500 units, especially in rent-controlled markets or distressed office markets. They face a three-part squeeze: rising costs (labor, insurance, maintenance), frozen or capped rents (NYC at 2.75–5.25% per Rent Guidelines Board; California at 5% + CPI), and expensive refinancing (multifamily delinquencies at 1.37% Q3 2025 show how many are already underwater). In office, small owners of Class B/C buildings watch their properties crater in value. Sonder's liquidation after Marriott cut its deal shows even growth-stage operators cannot compete. Pinnacle's bankruptcy (May 2025, $500M in debt) shows that even mid-sized players fail when rents flatten and costs rise. Small operators exit via bankruptcy auction, consolidation into larger chains, or just walking away from a leveraged deal.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The refinancing crisis through 2028 will force a second wave of consolidation. $2.8 trillion in CRE loans mature through 2028, and if rates stay elevated, many more small operators will not be able to refinance. The gap between what they owe and what the property is now worth (especially in office, where vacancy is still high and fundamentals are weak) will force sales. Institutional capital is ready to buy the distressed portfolios at 20–30% discounts. The REIT already earns 36.8% margins in multifamily (2022 baseline); scale and cost cuts will expand that further. What you get is a real estate landscape owned and managed by a shrinking circle of mega-operators, leaving no path for anyone without $50 million to own property at all.

In one sentence

REITs own the ground in a nutshell

REITs and institutional capital are absorbing fragmented real estate owners as financing dries up, supply catches up, and scale becomes the only competitive advantage.
