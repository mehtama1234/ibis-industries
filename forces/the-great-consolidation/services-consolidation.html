Consolidating Boring Services

Environmental, engineering, and architecture firms get rolled into regional platforms and PE-backed consolidators as independence becomes untenable.

Capital is hunting in the most unglamorous corner of the economy: engineers, architects, environmental consultants. These services are fragmented—thousands of small practices scattered across the country—and suddenly, they're not profitable enough to stay independent. Scale wins. Tariffs, labor shortages, and AI tooling all favor the big firms that can absorb these costs.

What changed

Environmental consulting was a $27.4 billion US market in 2025, growing just 1.6% that year but powering forward on EPA mandates. In April 2024, the EPA set drinking-water PFAS standards for six compounds; three months later in July 2024, PFOA and PFOS became CERCLA hazardous substances. Those two regulatory lines alone created a multi-year remediation pipeline flowing through 2026 budgets. At the same time, tariffs on steel and aluminum doubled to 50% in 2025, forcing project costs up and squeezing margins to the 8-9% EBITDA range typical for mid-sized firms. This is the kind of environment where only large, diversified consultancies survive: WSP paid $1.78 billion for Power Engineers in February 2025, hunting for specialist depth and geographic reach. The 65,000+ environmental consulting establishments in the US remain highly fragmented, but the consolidation wave is accelerating among the top tier.

Engineering services sits at $386.7 billion in 2026, growing at just 1.5% that year despite a decade of infrastructure money flowing from federal legislation. The infrastructure bill, data-center build-outs for AI, and renewable energy transition should be fueling explosive growth—and they are, in pockets—but over 382,000 engineering positions sat open in 2025 against an aging workforce (average age 46 by 2030) and accelerating automation. Tariffs hit hard: steel and aluminum duties up to 50% in 2025 directly compressed profit margins on projects already starved for labor. Firms cannot pass all these costs to clients without losing contracts. So they're consolidating instead. Over 55% of leading engineering firms deployed AI-assisted design, digital twins, and BIM systems by 2026, but you need scale to invest in those tools and absorb the labor shortage. In 2024 alone, 69 acquisitions happened among MEP giants; 2025 and 2026 are on track to exceed that. PRIME AE acquired Faller, Davis & Associates in December 2025; Salas O'Brien merged with Rock Brook in March 2026. The engineers with $5 million in annual billings cannot compete on technology or pricing.

Architecture is actually contracting. The market was $63.5 billion in 2026, growing at 0.1%—essentially flat—and down from $51.8 billion in 2022. The Architecture Billings Index hit 47.6 in October 2025 (anything below 50 means revenue is declining); 38 percent of firms reported falling revenue, and small practices under $250k annually expected a 7 percent revenue cut. Commercial office construction, which drives architecture demand, cratered because office vacancy rates hit 20.7% nationally in 2025, with San Francisco and Austin exceeding 26%. Nobody wants to build more offices. Smaller independent practices are getting squeezed hardest because they lack the geographic reach and service breadth to weather a single client segment's collapse. Meanwhile, AI software now automates site planning, code-violation detection, BIM workflows, and specification writing—shifting work toward firms that integrate these tools while threatening traditional junior-level drafting roles. The consolidation message is blunt: Stantec acquired UK firm Hydrock in May 2024, adding 950 professionals and increasing its UK presence by over 30%. The pattern repeats across all three sectors: independents cannot absorb tariff costs, cannot hire engineers at any wage, cannot invest in AI tools fast enough, and cannot weather cyclical collapses like the office-vacancy crisis.

The evidence

  • Environmental consulting: $27.4 billion US market (2025), 1.6% growth, tariffs doubled steel/aluminum to 50%, WSP acquired Power Engineers for $1.78B (February 2025)
  • Engineering services: $386.7 billion US market (2026), 1.5% growth, 382,000 open positions vs. aging workforce, 69 MEP acquisitions in 2024 alone with 2025-2026 exceeding that pace
  • Architecture: $63.5 billion US market (2026), 0.1% growth, office vacancy 20.7% nationally (San Francisco/Austin >26%), Billings Index at 47.6 in October 2025
  • Labor crisis across all three sectors: GIS specialists command premiums, licensed environmental engineers 'tightest in a generation' (ACEC 2025), 55% of leading engineering firms deployed AI tools to offset scarcity
  • Small practice collapse: Architecture firms under $250k annually expecting 7% revenue cut; 38% of architecture firms report falling revenue; independents cannot absorb tariff costs or invest in AI-driven workflows fast enough

Who rides it

Large, diversified engineering and environmental consultancies like WSP, AECOM, and Stantec are buying up regional specialists and niche practices at a fraction of what they'd cost to build organically. They absorb tariff costs across a diversified client base, they can invest in AI tools, and they can hire at scale. PE-backed consolidators (looking at architecture and engineering firms) can justify the upfront tech spend and weather cyclical downturns in single sectors because they own a portfolio. The winners are the firms with national footprints, data-center and renewable-energy client concentration, and the capital to integrate AI.

Who's squeezed

Independent practitioners and small regional firms are being squeezed from three directions at once: tariff inflation on materials and labor, inability to hire (average engineering workforce age 46 by 2030; licensed environmental engineers in acute shortage), and AI automation eating into traditional junior roles and drafting work. Architecture took a specific hit from the office-vacancy crisis (20.7% nationally, over 26% in major metros), making it a client-segment catastrophe. Small practices under $250k in billings expect 7% revenue cuts; they cannot compete on price with consolidators, cannot absorb tariff costs, and cannot afford the tech stack that's rapidly becoming table stakes. The exit ramp is narrowing: merge up or close.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that tariff escalation stalls infrastructure and commercial projects outright, which would crater the revenue pipeline that's supposed to be absorbing all these environmental remediation, engineering, and architectural services. If steel stays at 50% and aluminum follows, project economics break even for large firms. A recession would compound the damage: architecture is already down to 0.1% growth with collapsing office demand; engineering demand would evaporate in a credit crunch. The labor shortage is structural—you cannot automate yourself out of needing licensed engineers and environmental specialists—so firms cannot actually cut costs enough to offset tariffs and wage inflation. Consolidation becomes not a choice but the only path to survival, and the independents have no bargaining power.

In one sentence

Consolidating boring services in a nutshell

Capital is rolling up the fragmented world of engineers, architects, and environmental consultants—tariff costs, labor shortages, and AI tooling all favor scale, while small practices get squeezed into mergers or closure.
