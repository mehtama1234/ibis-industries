Scale in Food & Drink

Capital consolidates brewing, distributing, and slaughtering—independent operators face tariffs, automation, and a shrinking middle.

Consolidation is squeezing food and drink from both ends. Large producers with scale are automating, capturing retail space, and pivoting to premium categories—while thousands of independent craft brewers, small meat processors, and regional distributors face simultaneous margin collapse from tariffs, labor scarcity, and shifting consumer preferences away from their core products.

What changed

The US beer industry contracted 5.7% by volume in 2025, and craft brewery count fell 1.8% to 9,344 establishments by June 2026—but the real story is margin destruction. Aluminum tariffs hit 15% year-over-year in April 2025, canning costs doubled, and breweries paid labor rates exceeding $25 an hour. These cost shocks landed on a business model that was already fragile: only 54% of surveyed craft breweries reported growth in 2025, while 43% reported declines. Small regional players like Duck-Rabbit Craft Brewery (liquidated via Chapter 7 in April 2025) and Nebraska Brewing Company (Chapter 11 restructuring) couldn't absorb the hit. Meanwhile, large producers doubled down on automation and premium positioning. Molson Coors reported premium light beers adding 1.8 percentage points of market share by August 2024, with the Above Premium segment growing to 28% of net sales from 23% three years prior. Spirits-based ready-to-drink cocktails jumped 16.4% to $3.8 billion in 2025—a category entirely outside the traditional three-tier beer distribution system that small wholesalers depend on. This means craft breweries lost retail shelf space to RTDs, hard seltzers, and non-alcoholic beer (which exploded 111% from 2021 to 2025), all owned by larger players with consolidated supply chains.\n\nBeer wholesaling reveals the consolidation trap. The $95.4 billion beer distribution market grew just 1.0% from 2025 to 2026, stagnating despite overall beverage growth. The middle layer—1,393 beer distributors employing 115,900 workers—faced simultaneous compression: beer volumes fell 5.7% in 2025, tariffs fragmenting import segments (bottled and draft escaped 25% aluminum tariffs, but canned beer got hammered), and alternative categories bypassing the system entirely. Wages spiked 6.4% to $28.89 an hour even as employment ticked down 0.6% year-over-year—classic consolidation-era labor dynamics. Reyes, a mega-distributor, acquired 11 RNDC markets including a California exit in 2026, concentrating distribution power further. By April 2025, Trump administration tariffs on canned imported beer created a two-tier market: bottled and draft beer (61.5% of trade) remained tariff-free, but canned beer importers faced 25% duties. Mexico, which accounts for $6.3 billion of $7.5 billion in beer imports, bore the brunt. Smaller wholesalers who built their books around high-margin import cans suddenly faced inventory rot and margin collapse.\n\nMeat processing is the clearest consolidation win. The $387.7 billion beef and poultry sector is dominated by four firms—JBS, Tyson, Cargill, National Beef—that hold near-monopoly power. But that power is being weaponized toward automation and scale escape. Beef cattle prices in 2026 averaged $240 per cwt, up 7% from 2025, while immigration enforcement tightened the labor supply beyond what wage increases alone could fix. Processors invested heavily in cobots and AI vision systems to replace de-hoofing, evisceration, and packaging roles—automating away the chronic labor shortages that have plagued the industry since 2020. Meanwhile, China locked out US beef in 2025, wiping out 96% of September exports (556 mt), and only restored partial access via a May 2026 trade deal. That export shock hit smaller regional processors and independent cattle ranchers hardest; the Big Four have global diversification and can absorb it. The DOJ opened antitrust investigations in 2025–2026, and price-fixing settlements ran into hundreds of millions—but enforcement hasn't disrupted the consolidation machine. Plant-based meat, hyped as an existential threat to the Big Four in 2023–2024, collapsed to 2–3% retail share as consumers rejected the taste and authenticity claims. The real threat to independents isn't innovation; it's scale. Tariff chaos created a 17% effective rate on food manufacturing in early 2025, but the Big Four leverage consolidated supply chains to absorb or pass through these shocks—while single-plant operators and regional cooperatives get caught flat-footed.

The evidence

  • Breweries: US beer production declined 5.7% by volume in 2025; 9,344 operating breweries as of June 2026 (down 1.8% YoY), with 481 closures in 2025.
  • Beer wholesaling: $95.4 billion market growing just 1.0% (2025–2026), while spirits-based RTD cocktails surged 16.4% to $3.8 billion (2025)—bypassing traditional distributors entirely.
  • Meat processing: $387.7 billion sector (2026) controlled by Big Four; beef cattle prices averaged $240/cwt (2026), up 7% YoY, driving automation investment while China lockout wiped out 96% of US beef exports in Sept 2025.
  • Tariffs: Aluminum tariffs hit 15% YoY (April 2025); canned beer importers faced 25% tariffs while bottled beer (61.5% of trade) escaped unscathed, fragmenting the import market.
  • Labor: Craft brewery labor costs exceeded $25/hour; beer wholesaling wages spiked 6.4% to $28.89/hour (2026), yet employment ticked down 0.6% YoY amid consolidation.

Who rides it

Large producers with automation and scale—Molson Coors, AB InBev, the Big Four meat processors (JBS, Tyson, Cargill, National Beef)—consolidate market share and shift to premium and alternative categories (non-alcoholic beer up 111% from 2021 to 2025, premium segments now 28% of sales). Mega-distributors like Reyes acquire smaller wholesalers outright (11 RNDC markets including California in 2026), concentrating gatekeeping power. Private equity and franchised production models can absorb tariff shocks and labor inflation through scale and capital access; automation investments ($4.5B global robotics market projected by 2033 at 9.2% CAGR) are only viable for the well-capitalized.

Who's squeezed

Independent craft breweries, regional breweries, and small meat processors face simultaneous margin collapse. Tariffs landed directly on their operating costs (15% aluminum, double-digit can inflation) at a moment when retail shelf space shifted to RTDs, spirits-based cocktails, and non-alcoholic alternatives. Duck-Rabbit Craft Brewery liquidated in April 2025; Nebraska Brewing restructured. Mid-sized regional breweries saw retail placement evaporate while competing on quality and story against automated premium beers from the Big Three. Independent meat processors and small ranches were blindsided by China's export lockout (96% volume wipeout in Sept 2025) and face automation-driven labor market tightening without the capital or efficiency to invest in cobots and AI vision systems.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Tariff fragmentation and category shift are destroying the business models that funded independents. The 25% tariff on canned beer created a two-tier market (bottled imports protected at 61.5% of trade), meaning small import-focused wholesalers saw inventory rot while large distributors pivoted to protected formats. More critically, the growth categories—spirits RTDs, hard seltzers, non-alcoholic beer—are all owned by large players with consolidated supply chains and retail muscle. Independents can't enter these categories without capital, and they can't compete in their core category without scale-driven automation. The middle layer (wholesalers, regional breweries) is being systematically squeezed out, not by innovation but by the combination of tariff shocks, labor inflation that only scale can absorb, and category migration controlled by giants.

In one sentence

Scale in food & drink in a nutshell

Tariff shocks, labor scarcity, and the shift to premium and alternative beverages owned by giants are consolidating US food and drink into the hands of a few automated, scaled producers while independents close or get bought out.
