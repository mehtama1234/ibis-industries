A · Rolling up healthcare

Private Equity in Medicine

Capital corners fragmented healthcare—dentistry, mental health, and dialysis.

Healthcare was built on independent practitioners and small regional operators. Dentists, therapists, psychiatrists, and clinic owners ran their own shops. Now PE and platforms are rolling them up fast. New graduate debt, thin margins, workforce shortages, and regulatory chaos have made independence—especially solo practice—too risky. The roll-up is happening at speed: DSO affiliation nearly doubled in two years; behavioral health M&A hit 104 deals in 2025; and dialysis giants are squeezing smaller players out of existence.

What changed

What this force made scarce is independence itself. The US dental market, worth $196.1 billion in 2026, is being reshaped by Dental Service Organizations (DSOs). In just two years, DSO affiliation nearly doubled—jumping from 16.1% of dentists in 2024 to 39% of practices by 2026. This didn't happen by choice. New dentists graduate with $297,800 in average student debt as of 2025, and Grad PLUS loans are being phased out for students starting in July 2026, forcing people to consider DSOs just to manage the debt. Meanwhile, reimbursement is stagnant: 60% of dentists report rates have flat-lined since 2021, and 25% say they've fallen. Operating costs are rising faster than revenue. In H1 2026 alone, 175+ dental practice locations sold to DSOs and other buyers. Staffing became the bottleneck—62% of dentists cite hiring as their top concern, dental hygienist shortages persist, and McKinsey projects 36,000 missing professionals by 2031. Solo practitioners can't compete on hiring or technology. Smile Doctors, a PE-backed platform, acquired myOrthos and its 70+ orthodontic practices across 13 states in 2025, signaling that specialty consolidation is the move.

The mental health sector is being crushed by a different squeeze: demand is exploding, but the system is broken. The residential mental health and substance abuse market grew to $33.2 billion in 2026, a 5.1% CAGR since 2021, but therapists and psychiatrists can't be hired fast enough. HRSA projects a shortfall of 327,000 behavioral health professionals by 2038. Mood and anxiety disorders alone drive 34.4% of the sector, and eating disorders among young adults are surging. Opioid overdose deaths have fallen from 110,000 in 2023 to 75,000 in 2025, but fentanyl still drives 75% of those deaths, keeping treatment demand relentless. The problem: Medicaid covers 45.6% of revenue but reimburses below cost in most cases. January 2026 gutted the enhanced Medicaid match (the 90% federal reimbursement that funded expansion), threatening state budgets. Then in May 2025, the administration stopped enforcing Biden-era mental health parity rules requiring equal coverage for mental versus physical health—leaving reimbursement in limbo. Small operators can't survive this: they lack scale to absorb low Medicaid rates, can't offer competitive wages, and have no safety net when policy shifts. PE saw opportunity: 104 behavioral health M&A deals closed in 2025, up from 73 in 2024. Mental health transactions alone exceeded 80, up from ~70 in 2024. Buyers are consolidating to create large platforms that can absorb regulatory chaos and negotiate with Medicaid.

Dialysis centers tell a starker story: two giants dominate and smaller players are nearly extinct. DaVita controls 38% of the market, Fresenius 27%, and the market is worth $36 billion in 2025, growing 3.5% CAGR. The two public giants are locked in a tightly controlled race: both guide for modest revenue growth (2–3% annually) but expand margins through relentless cost control and mix shifts. Fresenius deployed its premium 5008X CAREsystem (high-volume hemodiafiltration) across 227 US clinics by Q2 2026, performing 600,000+ treatments, signaling a shift toward higher-acuity, higher-margin dialysis. DaVita is countering with supply contracts for expanded high-efficiency dialyzers launching around 2028. Medicare rates are a cruel trap: the 2026 rate increase was only 2.2%; 2027 is projected at a mere 1.1% (with CMS setting the ESRD PPS base rate at $299.55 per treatment). These micro-increases lag wage inflation year after year, compressing operating margins to 11.7% on average—low for a capital-intensive business. Independent dialysis centers and small regional operators have been squeezed out. The industry treated 516,837 dialysis patients in 2025, but that volume is concentrated in two companies that can absorb low Medicare reimbursement through scale, supply chain leverage, and mix optimization. Home dialysis grew from 10% of the patient population in 2016 to 17.4% in 2022, and CMS is pushing this shift hard to cut costs. Small operators can't make the economics work on home dialysis without massive scale.

The evidence

  • Dentistry: DSO affiliation in US dental practices jumped to 39% by 2026 (from 16.1% in 2024), driven by new graduate debt averaging $297,800 (2025) and reimbursement stagnation that has frozen rates for 60% of practitioners since 2021
  • Mental Health: Behavioral health M&A accelerated to 104 deals in 2025 (vs. 73 in 2024) as fragmented regional operators faced a 327,000-person clinical workforce shortfall projected by 2038 and the January 2026 sunset of enhanced Medicaid FMAP (90% federal match)
  • Dialysis Centers: DaVita and Fresenius collectively hold 65% of the $36 billion market (2025); CMS rate increases (2.2% for 2026, 1.1% for 2027) lag wage inflation, compressing operating margins to 11.7% and squeezing independent operators

Who rides it

DSOs and PE-backed platforms in dentistry are winning. They absorb new graduates escaping debt, hire at scale, deploy technology (AI imaging, teledentistry is now 30% of consultations, up from near-zero in 2020), and negotiate with insurers as large networks. In mental health, consolidators can absorb Medicaid's below-cost reimbursement and hedge regulatory risk; small operators collapse under the volatility. In dialysis, DaVita and Fresenius win—they have the scale to manage CMS's micro-rate increases, the supply chain leverage to control costs, and the capital to invest in premium services like the Fresenius 5008X (deployed in 227 clinics by Q2 2026) that generate higher margins. Scale is the only winning strategy in all three. Technology adoption, workforce management, and payer negotiation all favor large platforms.

Who's squeezed

Solo dentists, small group practices, and independent therapists and psychiatrists are exiting. New dentists can't afford to go solo—$297,800 debt means DSO or bust. Solo practitioners can't compete on hiring (62% of dentists cite staffing as top concern; 36,000 dental professionals missing by 2031), can't afford technology, and get reimbursed at the same stagnant rates regardless of practice size. Independent behavioral health providers are being rolled up into large platforms or collapsing under Medicaid pressure, regulatory whiplash (SAMHSA grant termination then reversal in January 2026, parity rules non-enforcement in May 2025), and wage inflation they can't absorb. Small dialysis operators are already gone—the market is consolidated. Owner-operators who built private practices over decades are cashing out now before valuations fall further. The middle class of healthcare—independent practitioners and regional groups—is disappearing.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is system fragility. In dentistry, DSO consolidation is fast but unproven at scale—many DSOs are lightly capitalized platforms that manage practices but don't invest in clinical durability. If margins compress further (and they will as debt service costs rise and reimbursement stagnates), DSOs could collapse, leaving patients and hygienists worse off. In mental health, the risk is acute: regulatory chaos (SAMHSA swinging between grant termination and reversal in a single day) and the Medicaid FMAP sunset in January 2026 have shaken confidence. If state budgets tighten and enhanced Medicaid doesn't return, a huge population of poor and near-poor patients will lose access, and consolidators will abandon unprofitable markets. Dialysis is locked—DaVita and Fresenius control the industry—but that monopoly could trigger CMS price controls or tighter bundling, compressing margins further. All three sectors are dependent on Medicaid or stagnant commercial reimbursement, which means consolidation is a race to squeeze more value from fewer patients, not to improve care. That creates a hard floor on how consolidated the industry can become before regulators step in.
