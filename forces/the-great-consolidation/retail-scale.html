Scale Wins in Retail

A few giant retailers absorb hundreds of competitors while smaller chains fade into bankruptcy.

Retail is getting smaller and bigger at the same time. The same category that used to support thousands of independent stores and small chains is now dominated by a handful of giants—Home Depot, Lowe's, Dick's, O'Reilly—while the middle disappears. Consolidation isn't coming; it's already here, and the cost of staying independent has become too high.

What changed

Home improvement retail has frozen in place. The housing market is stuck—locked-in mortgage rates from 2021 and 2022 have created what the industry calls the "lock-in effect," where homeowners refuse to sell because refinancing would cost them thousands more per year in interest payments. Housing turnover hit multi-decade lows. That means customers who used to flip houses or downsize are now just staying put, and their spending on renovations flatlines. In 2025, the market grew just 2.3% to $294.6 billion, barely keeping pace with inflation. The two leaders, Home Depot and Lowe's, can't grow; they can only split a frozen pie. But Lowe's found one angle that works: professional services. In 2024, its professional-services revenue surged 15%, because contractors still need work done even when consumers don't. Home Depot is now laying off ~800 corporate workers and closing distribution facilities to cut costs and rely more on AI for supply-chain routing. Smaller chains like Wren Kitchens, which had 15 locations, simply vanished—bankruptcy and liquidation in 2025. Meanwhile, Amazon is quietly climbing: it now captures 12% of online DIY sales and grew 25% year-over-year in home improvement tools, hitting $6.1 billion in 2024. The category is being hollowed out. Labor costs are rising 6–8% annually because workers are scarce. Material costs are up 4–6% on average because of tariffs—kitchen cabinets and bathroom vanities face 15–20% tariff increases, with some proposals pushing to 50%. Smaller retailers can't absorb these costs. They can't negotiate with suppliers. They can't shift to services or e-commerce. They fold.

Auto parts retail is shrinking because cars are changing. The US auto parts industry is $77.1 billion as of 2026, growing at just 1.6%—anemic for retail. The number of service occasions per vehicle is falling because electric vehicles need no oil changes, fewer spark plugs, simpler drivetrains, and less frequent brake service thanks to regenerative braking. The average car on the road is now 12.8 years old (a record), which should drive parts demand, but instead people are switching to service centers and dealerships because modern cars are too complex for DIY repair. E-commerce has captured the price-sensitive buyer: online auto parts sales (excluding Amazon and eBay) reached ~$23 billion in 2025 and are growing at 4.6% annually, but when you add Amazon's $13.7 billion (2026) and eBay Motors and Walmart, the e-commerce channel grows 6.7% annually—far faster than physical stores. Tariff shocks destabilized the entire supply chain. In April 2025, the US imposed a 25% tariff on imported auto parts. By May 2025, after a 90-day truce, that dropped to 30%, but the volatility alone broke smaller retailers' inventory planning and cash flow. Two major suppliers—First Brands and Marelli—filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2025, and analysts are warning of a wave of failures in 2026. The Big Four—O'Reilly Automotive, AutoZone, Advance Auto Parts, and NAPA—are surviving and thriving. O'Reilly posted record revenue in Q1 2026, with an 8.1% increase in comparable store sales and earnings per share up 16% year-over-year. They have scale, credit lines, and the ability to negotiate through tariff chaos. Smaller chains don't.

Sporting goods is consolidating in real time. Dick's Sporting Goods spent $2.4 billion to acquire Foot Locker in May 2025, finalizing the deal by September 2025 to create a combined entity with 3,200+ stores globally. Dick's controls roughly 10% of a highly fragmented $120.6 billion US sporting goods market (2026), but that 10% gives it enough leverage to close Foot Locker's unprofitable mall locations, consolidate back-office functions, and force suppliers to negotiate better terms. The market itself is barely growing—just 1.1% in 2026 and negative 0.5% CAGR from 2021 to 2026. Tariffs on imported footwear and athletic apparel are raising landed costs by over 10 percentage points in some categories, squeezing both wholesale margins and retail shelf prices. Consumers are retracting: middle-income discretionary spending is weakening, pandemic savings have dried up, and tariff pass-through has raised apparel costs. US sporting goods transactions fell 9% in Q4 2025 and Q1 2026. E-commerce is also pulling share—online sporting goods sales reached $39.2 billion in 2025, growing at 2.3% annually. Nike and other brands are rethinking direct-to-consumer strategies, which means they're cutting their own wholesale orders or raising prices to retailers, compressing Dick's margins just as it's integrating Foot Locker. Dick's reported FY 2025 consolidated net sales of $17.22 billion, up 28.1%, but that's because of the Foot Locker acquisition, not organic growth. The company's net margin is 4.7% as of Q2 2026—razor thin. Smaller specialists (Big 5, Academy, independent run shops) can't compete on price, can't negotiate supplier terms, can't build omnichannel systems, and can't survive a tariff shock. They're losing customers and cash simultaneously.

What's new is that scale has become a utility. In all three sectors—home improvement, auto parts, sporting goods—being small is no longer just a disadvantage; it's a terminal condition. A small retailer used to be able to compete on location, service, curation, or local brand loyalty. Now those advantages dissolve in months because e-commerce neutralizes location, tariffs destroy cash flow, supplier consolidation leaves no one to negotiate with, and consumers are switching to services (like Lowe's Professional) or direct brands (Nike, Amazon) rather than old-school retail. The independent hardware store, auto parts shop, or sporting goods specialist has become an endangered species because the margin structure can't support it anymore.

The evidence

  • Home improvement retail (Home Depot, Lowe's, Amazon): $294.6B market in 2025, 2.3% growth, professional services up 15% (Lowe's 2024), smaller chains bankrupt (Wren Kitchens 15 locations, 2025).
  • Auto parts retail (O'Reilly, AutoZone, NAPA, Advance Auto Parts): $77.1B market in 2026, 1.6% growth, O'Reilly comparable store sales +8.1% Q1 2026, smaller suppliers filing (First Brands, Marelli Chapter 11, 2025).
  • Sporting goods retail (Dick's, Foot Locker, Nike DTC): $120.6B market in 2026, 1.1% growth, Dick's acquired Foot Locker for $2.4B (May-Sept 2025), transactions down 9% Q4-Q1 2026.
  • Tariff impact across retail: auto parts 25% tariff April 2025 (dropped to 30% May 2025), home improvement materials up 4–6%, kitchen cabinets/vanities 15–20% increases, sporting goods footwear over 10 percentage points.
  • E-commerce penetration: Amazon $6.1B home improvement (2024, +25% YoY), online auto parts $23B excluding marketplaces (+4.6% CAGR), Amazon auto parts $13.7B (2026), sporting goods online $39.2B (2025, +2.3%).

Who rides it

Dick's Sporting Goods, O'Reilly Automotive, Home Depot, and Lowe's are the winners. They have scale, scale, and more scale. They can absorb tariff shocks by negotiating better supplier terms or shifting cost to consumers. They can close underperforming locations, consolidate back-office functions, and invest in e-commerce and services. Dick's swallowed Foot Locker to gain 3,200+ stores globally and eliminate redundancy. O'Reilly is posting record earnings while smaller competitors file for bankruptcy. Lowe's found a protected corner—professional services—where tariffs and e-commerce don't cannibalize as aggressively. They're not winning by growing; they're winning by absorbing competitors and cutting costs faster than rivals can.

Who's squeezed

Independent hardware stores, small auto parts chains, and specialist sporting goods shops. Wren Kitchens had 15 locations and it's gone. First Brands and Marelli filed for Chapter 11. Smaller retailers are losing to three forces simultaneously: tariffs that destroy cash flow, e-commerce that steals customers, and supplier consolidation that leaves no leverage for price negotiation. A small shop has to compete with Amazon's $6.1 billion home improvement operation, Dick's 3,200-store footprint, and O'Reilly's 8% store-sales growth. They can't. They're trapped between rising costs and falling margins, and the exit door is shrinking as acquisition offers dry up.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The structural risk is that consolidation breeds fragility. When three or four companies control an entire retail category, supply shocks ripple through with no buffer. The tariff swings of 2025—from 25% to 30% on auto parts in a single month—show how little control even the big retailers have over the cost structure. If tariffs stay high or ratchet up further, even O'Reilly and Dick's will struggle to pass costs to consumers without destroying volume. The second risk is that e-commerce and direct-to-consumer strategies will eventually cannibalize the winners too. Amazon is already taking 12% of online DIY and 25% growth in tools. Nike and other brands are recalibrating wholesale strategies, which means they could cut Dick's off or raise prices. If the big retailers get caught between rising supplier costs, tariff risk, shrinking transaction volumes, and brand price hikes, there's nowhere left to cut except payroll and store closures. That's when consolidation turns into a race to the bottom.

In one sentence

Scale wins in retail in a nutshell

Scale has become a utility in retail—too expensive to compete without it, and three or four giants now control categories that used to support thousands of independents.
