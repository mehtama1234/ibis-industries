The patterns

Capital rolls up fragmented Main Street — PE, franchises, REITs, and scale leaders absorb the independents, and financialization wins while owner-operators exit.

Consolidation is reshaping four foundational industries simultaneously, each driven by different mechanics of capital but arriving at the same outcome: scale wins, independents exit. In dentistry, DSO (Dental Service Organization) affiliation nearly doubled from 16.1% of dentists in 2024 to 39% of practices by 2026. The driver is simple and brutal: reimbursement has stagnated since 2021 (60% of dentists report flat rates, 25% report declines), new graduate debt hit $297,800 in 2025, and staffing has become a structural bottleneck—62% of dentists cite hiring as their top concern, with 36,000 missing professionals projected by 2031. Solo practice, which once meant independent ownership and equity building, is now a trap. The US dental market is $196.1 billion in 2026, but margin compression means younger dentists cannot build practice equity the way their predecessors did. Instead, they rent their labor to DSO networks backed by institutional capital that standardize operations, leverage buying power, and extract margin through scale. This happened in residential senior care on steroids. The industry generated $9.0 billion in franchise revenue in 2026 (up from $5.967 billion in 2021), serving 73 million baby boomers with guaranteed occupancy demand. But that same demographic tailwind is what attracted private equity: 871 M&A transactions closed in 2025, a 20.8% jump over 2024's prior record, with assisted living making up 44% of deals. Staffing shortages in nursing, CNAs, and dining push wage costs up 31% since 2019, compressing the 14.8% margins from earlier years. Independent senior care operators and assisted living owners—often 40-year-old family businesses—cannot absorb wage inflation, cannot access capital at PE's cost, and cannot negotiate with insurers and Medicaid at the volume that national platforms can. So they sell. In retail, the consolidation is even starker: scale leaders like Home Depot and Lowe's hold their own in a $294.6 billion market (2025), but smaller competitors simply vanish. Wren Kitchens, a major kitchen specialty retailer, filed for bankruptcy and closed all 15 US locations in 2025. Why? Home Depot and Lowe's invest in e-commerce (Amazon grabbed 12% of online DIY sales and grew 25% year-over-year in 2024), absorb tariff-driven cost increases (material costs up 4-6%, kitchen cabinets up 15-20%), and shift to higher-margin professional services (Lowe's reported 15% revenue growth in professional services in 2024). Independent hardware stores and specialized retailers cannot match these economics. They are crushed by material costs they cannot negotiate, margin compression from Amazon, and the sheer labor cost of competing. Commercial real estate shows the same pattern: while multifamily investors snapped up 124,600 units in Q2 2026 and industrial vacancies stayed low (940 million sq ft leased in 2025), office properties—once the stable revenue engine for regional real estate firms—are in structural crisis. Office vacancy peaked at 19.6% in Q1 2025 and fell only to 17.7% by mid-2026, but the real story is that Class B and C buildings are economically obsolete while Class A trophy assets in central business districts remain desirable. Add $957 billion in CRE loans maturing in 2025 alone and $2.8 trillion through 2028, and small to mid-sized real estate owners face refinancing cliff risk. The scale players and REITs can manage this math. Small operators cannot. Across all four industries, the mechanics of consolidation are identical: reimbursement/pricing power stagnates for the small firm, debt service becomes crushing (whether on practice debt or refinancing), labor becomes scarce and unaffordable, and capital—whether PE, franchisors, or scale retailers—can absorb the squeeze and buy low. The independents get bought, demoted to subordinate roles, or exit.

The through-line

Across dentistry, senior care, home improvement, and commercial real estate, independent practitioners and business owners are being absorbed into scale platforms because debt loads, reimbursement pressure, and labor scarcity make going alone unsustainable.

Who wins across the board

Scale-backed platforms and financial capital are the clear winners. DSO networks absorb dental practices and extract margin through standardized operations and labor leverage. PE firms and franchisors roll up senior care at record pace (871 deals in 2025), managing wage inflation through scale and accessing capital at costs solo operators cannot. Home Depot, Lowe's, and Amazon dominate retail consolidation by absorbing tariff shock and investing in e-commerce. REITs and institutional multifamily investors capture the best assets (the 3.4-million-home shortage and 105% buy-to-rent premium ensure strong returns), while trophy-class office REITs weather the refinancing cliff with scale and diversification. The consolidation is fastest where financialization is deepest: dentistry (39% DSO affiliation in 2026), senior care (871 deals in 2025), and real estate (institutional multifamily growth 39.5% YoY).

Who's squeezed across the board

Independent practitioners, family business owners, and small-to-mid-sized operators are being squeezed out or absorbed at depressed valuations. Dental practice owners who built equity over decades now face a choice: sell to a DSO or watch patient volume stagnate as their margins collapse under reimbursement pressure and wage inflation. Assisted living operators and independent senior care agencies face the same squeeze: $957B in maturing loans (2025), wage costs up 31% since 2019, and insufficient scale to compete with PE-backed platforms that can acquire at volume and manage regulatory/insurance relationships. Independent hardware stores and specialty retailers are being crushed by material cost inflation (tariffs up 4-6%, cabinets up 15-20%), Amazon's 25% growth in tools, and their inability to invest in e-commerce or professional services. Small-to-mid-sized commercial real estate owners face a refinancing cliff ($2.8T through 2028) and obsolescence (Class B and C office buildings are economically dead), forcing distressed sales to institutional players who can manage the portfolio math. The theme is consistent: going solo now means going broke.

The biggest tension

The one to watch

The single biggest risk is that consolidation creates structural brittleness disguised as efficiency. DSOs, PE-backed senior care chains, and retail platforms achieve higher margins by standardizing operations and cutting costs, but this concentration of ownership in a few hands means less innovation, fewer choices for patients and consumers, and reduced flexibility when markets shift. In dentistry, 39% DSO affiliation by 2026 means that when a DSO makes a wrong bet (on pricing, scheduling, technology, or staffing), it affects millions of patients instantly, not thousands. In senior care, 871 deals in 2025 mean most elderly Americans' daily experience is controlled by a handful of PE firms whose incentive is to extract maximum margin, not to innovate in care quality. In retail and real estate, Amazon and institutional REITs dominate, leaving no room for experimentation or customer-specific service. Worse, consolidation makes the system fragile to debt stress: if refinancing becomes unaffordable at scale (commercial real estate's $2.8T maturing through 2028), or if a DSO or PE platform overextends on acquisition debt and defaults, the shock cascades across entire regions and service markets. We are trading adaptability and redundancy for scale and extraction.
