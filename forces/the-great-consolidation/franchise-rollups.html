B · Franchising Main Street

The Franchise Roll-Up

PE and franchising turn fragmented local services into scaled, financialized platforms — squeezing independent operators out.

Franchising has always promised local ownership with system support. But now private equity is buying up franchise networks, tightening standards, squeezing margins, and converting owner-operators into salaried managers. The shift is visible in two industries bearing the load: senior care and casual dining. Consolidation accelerates because demographic demand is certain and financialization makes scale profitable—even when it's not.

What changed

In senior care, demographic tailwinds created a franchise gold rush. The US has 73 million baby boomers entering retirement, and demand for non-medical in-home care is growing 6–9% annually (2026), with projections accelerating through the early 2030s as the over-80 cohort swells. Franchise revenue in residential senior care jumped from $5.967 billion (2021) to a projected $9.0 billion by 2026—a 51% climb in five years. That growth made the sector a perfect target for institutional capital. Private equity flooded in: 2025 saw 871 M&A transactions in senior care, up 20.8% from 2024's prior record of 721 deals. Assisted living made up 44% of those transactions. The pace is accelerating further into 2026, signaled by BrightSpring Health Services' IPO in January 2024 via KKR backing, validating the thesis that home and community-based care at scale was a safe bet. But the catch is immediate: labor is the binding constraint. Registered nurses, CNAs, and dining staff report 40% turnover rates. Over half of home care agencies report ongoing staffing shortages, forcing wage growth of 31% cumulatively since 2019. The industry is now caught between demographic certainty (occupancy at 88.7% nationally in Q3 2025, the 17th consecutive quarterly increase) and margin pressure that forces operators to choose between losing money or rationing service.

In chain restaurants, franchising confronts a different squeeze: structural cost inflation with nowhere to pass it along. Labor costs jumped 39% since pre-pandemic levels, with minimum wages now $16–$20 per hour in major states (California's FAST Act mandates $20/hour for national chains with 60+ locations starting 2024; Washington hit $16.66; Florida is heading to $15 by September 2026). Food inflation sits 34% above pre-pandemic baselines. Tariffs have forced 62% of operators to raise menu prices, yet consumer spending at restaurants is growing only 1.3% in real terms. The market itself is stalling: chain restaurant revenue fell -1.0% in 2026 after +1.7% growth in 2025, with margins already razor-thin. Forty-two percent of operators reported being unprofitable in 2025, and 60% report deteriorated business conditions. This is where franchising's promise breaks down. A franchise fee and marketing tax extract cash from the unit operator. When labor and food costs spiral and consumer demand softens, the franchisor still gets paid first. The franchisee absorbs the loss. The sector is cannibalizing itself: TGI Fridays and Red Lobster filed Chapter 11 in 2024, closing 130+ locations combined. FAT Brands (parent of Smokey Bones) filed Chapter 11 in January 2026, followed by closure of all remaining Smokey Bones locations. On The Border and Bravo Brio followed into Chapter 11 in 2025 (Bravo Brio for the second time in five years). Full-service casual dining was hit hardest, but even fast-casual is gasping: the 142,206 chain restaurant establishments (2025) are fighting over 15.8 million total restaurant industry workers where wages are non-negotiable.

The consolidation angle is now clear. In senior care, PE firms see demographic demand as a moat: occupancy gains are real, the tailwind is decades long, and rolling up 121,000 fragmented elderly/disabled services businesses into branded platforms with centralized procurement, training, and billing lifts margins. In restaurants, consolidation works differently—it's defensive. Casual-dining chains that survive (Chili's, Texas Roadhouse) do so by accepting lower check averages (sit-down meals at $12 undercut limited-service at $15) while ramping unit volumes. Smaller operators and independent franchisees cannot. The capital required to carry thin margins, absorb wage floors in high-cost states, and survive on 1–2% unit economics gets you to just enough scale that PE can buy you whole, strip out independent franchisees, and run you as a wage-labor operation. Franchising promised the franchisee a path to independence. Consolidation turns that promise into a trap: the franchise fee is now a wealth transfer from the operator to the institution, locked in whether the unit is profitable or not.

The evidence

  • Senior care: $9.0 billion franchise revenue projected by 2026, up 51% from 2021 baseline of $5.967 billion (2026 industry data)
  • Senior care: 871 M&A transactions in 2025, 20.8% above 2024's record; assisted living made up 44% of deals (Jan 2026 reporting)
  • Senior care: National occupancy at 88.7% in Q3 2025—17th consecutive quarterly increase, signaling sustained demographic demand (Q3 2025 data)
  • Chain restaurants: 62% of operators forced to raise prices due to tariffs; labor costs up 39% since pre-pandemic; 42% of operators unprofitable (2025-2026 data)
  • Chain restaurants: Market revenue declined -1.0% in 2026 after +1.7% in 2025; casual dining chapter 11 filings accelerating (Red Lobster, TGI Fridays, FAT Brands, On The Border, Bravo Brio in 2024-2026)

Who rides it

Private equity and scale-focused franchisors win. Demographic certainty in senior care makes consolidation a safe bet—KKR-backed BrightSpring's January 2024 IPO validated the thesis. Consolidation in restaurants lets survivors run leaner, absorb wage pressure with volume, and extract economics from scale that independents and small franchisees cannot. Institutional capital can carry thin margins longer than owner-operators can. Wage floors in states like California and Washington ($16–$20/hour) are fixed costs. Only franchisors with hundreds of units can absorb them and still turn a profit.

Who's squeezed

Independent franchisees and owner-operators are squeezed out. In senior care, 40% turnover of nursing and support staff forces wage growth of 31% since 2019—that margin pressure hits the franchisee first, before the franchisor. In restaurants, the franchisee absorbs labor inflation, food inflation, tariff costs, and consumer demand softness all at once. The franchise fee is a fixed extraction regardless of unit profitability. Sixty percent of operators report deteriorated conditions; 42% are outright unprofitable. Chapter 11 filings accelerated across casual dining (Red Lobster, TGI Fridays, FAT Brands, On The Border, Bravo Brio all between 2024–2026). The franchisee's only exit is liquidation or sale to a consolidator who will strip the brand and convert the unit to wage labor.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that consolidation in mature service franchises (restaurants, home care) will hollow out unit economics so aggressively that the survivor base becomes too thin to generate returns. Senior care has demographic tailwinds—occupancy is real and the boomer wave is decades long—so margin pressure is survivable at scale. Restaurants have no such moat. Chain revenue is already negative (-1.0% in 2026). Consumer defection to takeout, delivery, and at-home eating is structural, not cyclical. If PE rolls up casual dining and tries to run it on 1–2% margins while absorbing $16–$20 wage floors, the returns math breaks. The franchisee exits. But the franchisor still owns the real estate, the debt, and a network of units that no longer generate owner-operator entrepreneurship—just wage labor. That's when the moat inverts.

In one sentence

The franchise roll-up in a nutshell

PE rolls up franchised Main Street—absorbing owner-operators, tightening margins, and converting entrepreneurship into salaried labor—because scale and demographic certainty make financialization more profitable than independence.
