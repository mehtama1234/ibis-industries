A · The engine's slow death

The engine & drivetrain

A century-old supply chain is on borrowed time as EVs eliminate transmissions and tariffs collapse the margins of engine makers.

The internal combustion engine built America's car industry. But electric motors need no engine. No transmission. No exhaust system. And when millions of vehicles stop needing these parts, an enormous supply chain—774 engine factories, 338 transmission plants, 90,000 transmission workers—wakes up to an existential problem. The electrification is not killing this industry all at once. It's squeezing it from three directions at once: tariffs, shrinking demand, and the slow shift to motors and batteries.

What changed

Engine manufacturing seems stable on paper—$43.8 billion in 2026, up from $37.3 billion in 2022. But the growth masks a fundamental shift. The industry is no longer making just gasoline engines. It's awkwardly straddling three fuel types at once: traditional gasoline, hybrids (which need hybrid modules and control systems), and mild-hybrid 48-volt systems. OEMs are keeping gasoline engines alive by turbocharging them, adding variable compression, and bolting on electric assist—buying time rather than investing in the long term. CAFE standards were actually reset in June 2025 to exclude EV credits from compliance, which sounds backwards but tells you everything: the government is giving automakers permission to keep selling cheaper gasoline cars. The real story is in transmission manufacturing, where the decline is brutal. Transmissions went from $54.7 billion in 2022 to $44.6 billion in 2026—a 18% drop. The sector lost 1.5% of establishments and shed 68,000 jobs during 2025 alone. Hybrids temporarily bought some time (hybrid sales jumped 20.5% through mid-2026), because hybrids still need multi-speed transmissions. But battery electric vehicles need nothing—EVs have a single-speed gearbox or go straight electric motor to wheels. Battery electric share collapsed from 9.8% to 5.9% by July 2026 after federal tax credits expired in September 2025, but that was tax policy, not physics. The structural death of transmission demand is baked in.

Tariffs are accelerating the consolidation and killing smaller players. The U.S. imposed 25% tariffs on autos and auto parts in April 2025, then Mexico followed in January 2026 with 25-50% tariffs on non-FTA parts. These tariffs added $2,000 to $4,000 to new vehicle prices and squeezed supplier margins to the breaking point. First Brands (which owned Raybestos, Centric, Cardone, Autolite, and FRAM) filed Chapter 11 in September 2025. TransAxle, a drivetrain remanufacturer with 13 locations in New Jersey, shut down permanently in August 2025 and laid off 209 workers with no bankruptcy filing—just closed the doors. Allison Transmission, trying to survive, spent $2.7 billion to acquire Dana's off-highway drivetrain business in June 2026, consolidating to chase $120 million in annual cost savings. These aren't random bankruptcies. They're signals that the margin model for traditional transmission and engine suppliers is broken. A shop with under $2 million in annual revenue can't absorb tariff costs, labor pressures, and shrinking volumes all at once.

The one bright spot is engine remanufacturing—the business of taking an old engine out of a junkyard, rebuilding it, and reselling it cheaper than a new one. This industry, worth $14.59 billion in 2025, is growing at 4.2% a year through 2033, with hybrid-engine remanufacturing expanding at 16.2% annually. The reason is perverse. New vehicle prices exploded because of tariffs. Interest rates stayed high. Used car supplies dried up. So owners of aging vehicles—the average fleet age jumped from 12.4 years in 2024 to 12.8 years in 2025 and is headed to 13 years by 2026—are choosing to stay with their old cars instead of buying new ones. A $10,000 to $15,000 engine replacement is painful but cheaper than a $35,000 new car at 7% interest. That's keeping the remanufacturing shops alive. But even they are squeezed: 44% of repair parts come from overseas, and tariffs have made sourcing engine cores 15-25% more expensive year-on-year. Shops need certification for hybrid and diesel work—labor costs rise. Metals stay expensive. Smaller remanufacturers with low margins (5-7%) can't hold on and are being swallowed by larger regional consolidators.

The evidence

  • Automobile Engine & Parts Manufacturing: $43.8 billion US market in 2026 (2.5% growth in 2026, 1.5% CAGR 2021–2026); Stellantis announced $13 billion US investment plan in October 2025, repositioning Kokomo engine plant to produce four-cylinder GMET4 EVO starting 2026.
  • Automobile Transmission Manufacturing: $44.6 billion US market in 2026 (down 2.9% in 2026, negative 1.7% CAGR 2021–2026); sector lost 68,000 jobs during 2025; Allison Transmission acquired Dana's off-highway business for $2.7 billion in June 2026.
  • Tariffs & Supply Chain: US 25% auto parts tariffs effective April 2025; Mexico 25–50% tariffs on non-FTA parts January 2026; USMCA compliance jumped from 44% (2024) to 67% (2025) to 80+ % (early 2026), forcing US reshoring.
  • Transmission Market Pressure: Hybrid sales jumped 20.5% through mid-2026, but BEV market share fell to 5.9% by July 2026 after EV tax credit expiration September 2025; light vehicle sales hit 16.3 million annualized in July 2026, down 1.4% YoY.
  • Engine Remanufacturing Revival: $14.59 billion market in 2025, growing 4.2% CAGR through 2033; hybrid-engine remanufacturing at 16.2% CAGR; average US vehicle fleet age hit 12.8 years in May 2025 (up from 12.6 years in 2024), reaching 13 years by 2026.

Who rides it

Consolidators win. Denso, Bosch, and Continental are doubling down on EV electronics and thermal systems, positioning themselves as powertrain integrators rather than pure engine makers. Allison Transmission won by acquiring Dana's drivetrain assets for $2.7 billion and chasing $120 million in annual synergies. Remanufacturing shops in regional clusters survive and grow by servicing older fleets—that market is expanding 4.2% a year. And OEMs that can stretch gasoline engine life through turbocharging and 48-volt mild-hybrid systems buy time before committing fully to EV-only lineups. The winners are those big enough to pivot, not those tied to a single fuel type.

Who's squeezed

Small and mid-sized suppliers lose. First Brands filed Chapter 11 in September 2025. TransAxle shut down all 13 locations in August 2025 with no bankruptcy—just done. Transmission workers lose: the sector shed 68,000 jobs in 2025 alone. Shops with under $2 million in annual revenue can't absorb tariff costs and are being consolidated. Gas station owners and fuel distributors lose as EV adoption eventually replaces pump demand. New-vehicle dealers lose margin as competition from used cars (kept alive by cheap engine remanufacturing) intensifies. And the traditional transmission maker—a company built on the skill of designing five-speed automatics—has no real future. That skill is obsolete in an electric-motor world.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that tariffs and geopolitical tensions spiral further, collapsing vehicle demand before the supply chain can adapt. Light vehicle sales are already weakening (16.3 million annualized in July 2026, down 1.4% YoY). If tariffs double, or if U.S.-China tensions push localization further, smaller suppliers may fail en masse before they can retool for EV components. The industry is caught between dying gasoline platforms and expensive EV platforms—there's no third option. A recession that cuts new-vehicle sales even 5% more could trigger a cascade of bankruptcies across transmission plants, engine remanufacturers, and parts suppliers. Consolidation is efficient but also fragile: it concentrates risk into a few large players (Allison, Denso, Bosch) and leaves regional suppliers and workers with nowhere to go.

In one sentence

The engine & drivetrain in a nutshell

Engines and transmissions are not disappearing overnight—hybrids and tariffs are keeping them alive—but the supply chain is shrinking, consolidating, and losing 68,000 jobs a year as the mathematics of electric vehicles crush the demand for parts that motors simply don't need.
