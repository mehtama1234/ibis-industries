A · The engine's slow death

Exhaust, Fuel & the Gas Station

The engine's slow death leaves behind a stranded infrastructure of catalytic converters, gas stations, and fuel retail fighting to survive.

Gas stations still line every highway and city corner in America—147,000 of them across the country in 2026. But the fundamental business is breaking. Fuel margins hover at just 1–2% per gallon, fuel volumes dropped 5.4% in 2025, and electric vehicles are eating into the core reason these stations exist. Meanwhile, the catalytic converter—the device that treated exhaust from tens of millions of cars—is becoming obsolete as EVs eliminate the need for it entirely.

What changed

For a century, the gas station was the backbone of automotive retail. You bought fuel. That's it. But by 2025–2026, that model is cracking. Gas prices fell 5.9% in 2025 to $3.11 per gallon, and fuel volume plummeted from $501.9 billion in 2024 to $476.3 billion in 2025—a 5.4% drop in a single year. Electric vehicles hit 4.5 million registered units in 2025, growing 26.7% year-over-year, and though the federal tax credit expired in September 2025 (causing new EV sales to crater from 10.5% of the market to just 5.8%), the long trend is unmistakable. Gas stations can no longer survive on fuel alone.\n\nThe industry's response has been to abandon fuel as the profit center and rebuild around convenience merchandise and prepared food. In 2026, convenience stores with gas pumps—a $678.9 billion industry—squeeze roughly 40 cents profit per gallon on fuel while chasing margin in hot dogs, coffee, and grab-and-go meals. Inside sales account for 28.5% of revenue now, and foodservice and merchandise hit $341.2 billion in 2025. But even that isn't enough to offset the squeeze: operating expenses rose faster than sales, shrinking per-transaction margins by 7 cents in 2025. Labor costs run $300,000–$500,000 per store annually and are climbing 3.3% each year, while tariffs (25% on Mexican auto parts since January 2025, with Mexico firing back with 5–50% retaliatory tariffs in January 2026) are making everything from coffee makers to point-of-sale systems more expensive. Major chains are retreating: Shell divested 500 company-owned retail sites per year in 2024–2025 to focus on refining. The result is consolidation—Alimentation Couche-Tard closed a $1.57 billion acquisition of GetGo Cafe + Market in June 2025 and snapped up Hutch's the same month, while total gas station count contracted 1.4% annually since 2021.\n\nCatalytic converters—the $9.87 billion US manufacturing sector that treats exhaust emissions—face a steeper cliff. The global market is forecast to contract at -5.7% CAGR through 2034, shrinking from $18.1 billion to $12.3 billion, because electric cars simply don't produce exhaust. The US market is technically still growing at 3.92% CAGR through 2035, but that's a mirage: it's sustained by an aging vehicle fleet (average age over 12 years) forcing emissions upgrades and by a temporary surge in aftermarket theft and replacement. In late 2025, platinum, palladium, and rhodium staged a substantial recovery on South African supply deficits, triggering a catalytic converter theft explosion—137,000+ units stolen in 2025 compared to roughly 14,000 in 2024. That's a ninefold spike, driven purely by precious-metal prices and the scrap value of old converters. But it's a death rattle, not a sign of health. Repair shops and OEM suppliers are hedging by pouring R&D into hybrid-vehicle catalysts and selective catalytic reduction technology. Meanwhile, tariff shocks are killing already-thin margins—manufacturers run 5–6% net margins in 2025–2026, and tariffs are narrowing them further, forcing smaller players out and consolidating the sector around Tenneco, Johnson Matthey, Faurecia, Umicore, BASF Catalysts, and a shrinking roster of others.\n\nWhat electrification made scarce: profitable retail fuel business and the entire exhaust-treatment supply chain. What it made cheap: gasoline, which dropped 5.9% in 2025 as demand flattened, and convenience stations, as weaker operators shut down and major brands divest. What it made risky: being a stand-alone gas station operator or a mid-tier catalytic converter supplier dependent on aging vehicles. What it made newly valuable: the ability to lock customers into prepared food and inside merchandise (Costco and Walmart are already winning the fuel price war by undercutting independent stations by 20–35 cents per gallon), automation and labor efficiency in convenience retail, and recycling of precious metals from old converters.

The evidence

  • Gas station and convenience store operators: fuel volume collapsed 5.4% in 2025 ($501.9B to $476.3B), margins eroded 7 cents per inside-store transaction, and major chains like Shell divested 500 sites annually in 2024–2025.
  • Catalytic converter manufacturers (Tenneco, Johnson Matthey, Faurecia, Umicore, BASF): global market contracting -5.7% CAGR through 2034, tariffs cutting 5–6% margins further, and consolidation consolidating around high-margin hybrid and SCR variants.
  • Aftermarket repair shops: saw 137,000+ catalytic converters stolen in 2025 (up ninefold from ~14,000 in 2024) as precious-metal prices recovered, creating short-term replacement demand but masking long-term demand destruction.
  • Non-traditional retailers (Costco, Walmart): won the fuel price war by undercutting independent gas stations 20–35 cents per gallon, capturing convenience volume while independent operators shrink.
  • EV owners and the broader market: 4.5 million registered EVs in 2025 (26.7% YoY growth) eliminating future catalytic converter demand, though new EV sales share crashed to 5.8% after federal tax credit expiration in September 2025.

Who rides it

Convenience store chains with scale (Alimentation Couche-Tard, Sunoco) are consolidating by acquisition—Couche-Tard spent $1.57 billion to close GetGo in June 2025—and betting on prepared food and merchandise as fuel demand erodes. Non-traditional fuel retailers like Costco and Walmart win by using fuel as a loss leader to drive traffic for higher-margin grocery and goods. In catalytic converter manufacturing, survivors are those investing in hybrid catalysts and advanced emissions tech (Johnson Matthey, Umicore expanding R&D in 2025–2026) or those with scale to absorb tariff shocks and consolidate around premium, high-tech variants.

Who's squeezed

Stand-alone gas station operators and mid-tier convenience store chains are squeezed by razor-thin margins (1–2% on fuel, eroding per-transaction margins inside stores) and rising labor costs ($300K–$500K per store annually, climbing 3.3% each year). Smaller catalytic converter suppliers face margin compression from tariffs and must compete against consolidated giants—the sector counted 150–200 establishments but faces ongoing M&A pressure. SQRL Service Stations, a significant operator, liquidated in Chapter 7 bankruptcy in 2024 after filing Chapter 11 with a $1.2 billion debt burden, signaling the financial stress of traditional gas station economics.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The temporary reprieve from EV tax credit expiration (new EV sales crashed to 5.8% in Q4 2025) masks the underlying destruction: 4.5 million registered EVs in 2025, growing 26.7% year-over-year, will eventually represent 8% of used-car returns by 2026, meaning fuel demand will continue its structural decline. Gas station operators have shifted to inside merchandise and prepared food, but that business is hitting margin limits (per-transaction margins shrank 7 cents in 2025), and it attracts intense price competition from Costco and Walmart. Catalytic converter manufacturers are facing a -5.7% global CAGR contraction, and tariff shocks will accelerate consolidation and supply-chain fragmentation. The entire ecosystem—gas stations, convenience stores, fuel distribution, and emissions treatment—built for a century of internal-combustion dominance, is slowly becoming stranded.

In one sentence

Exhaust, fuel & the gas station in a nutshell

As electric vehicles grow inexorably and fuel demand shrinks, gas stations and catalytic converter makers are trapped between structural decline, razor-thin margins, rising costs, and tariff shocks, forcing consolidation and a desperate pivot to convenience merchandise that can't fully replace the vanishing fuel business.
