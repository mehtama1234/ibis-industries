The Car Becomes a Computer

Software and chips now define the car, not mechanics — and the supply chain is tearing itself apart.

The electric car runs on software. Battery management, autonomous-driving sensors, in-cabin AI, over-the-air updates — none of that exists in a car with a combustion engine. The moment automakers go EV, the whole bill of materials flips. Electronics and semiconductors go from a small cost in the car's total to the bulk of it. But the US supply chain for making those boards and chips is cracking under the weight of change.

What changed

US automobile electronics manufacturing brought in $29.6 billion in revenue in 2025, down 3 percent from 2022 — even as automakers were ordering more EV-grade systems than ever. The margin is squeezed to 4.7 percent, and employment has stalled at around 58,000 to 60,000 workers. What's happening is a bifurcation: large suppliers like Lear and Visteon are surviving by automating and consolidating around EV capabilities. Lear hit $6.0 billion in Q4 2025 revenue, up 5 percent year-over-year, and Visteon guided to $3.625 billion to $3.825 billion for 2026. But smaller Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers are filing for bankruptcy at record rates. In 2025 alone, supplier bankruptcies exceeded any year in the past decade. First Brands Group, which carried $6 billion in debt, filed for Chapter 11 in May 2025. Accuride and Fisker collapsed in 2024 and 2025. The tariff wall made it worse. In January 2026, the US imposed a 25 percent tariff on advanced semiconductor components and threatened broader measures. But automakers had locked suppliers into fixed-fee contracts years ago. Companies couldn't renegotiate easily, so the tariff cost fell on them. They had to absorb the hit or go under. Meanwhile, Mexico is eating their lunch. In 2024, automotive investment in Mexico reached $8 billion, and Mexico was constructing 2,274 hectares of new manufacturing space by end-2025. Foxconn built a $900 million AI-server campus there. ASE put down a $200 million advanced-packaging line. Nearshoring is cheaper and faces no tariffs. The result: domestic volume is shrinking even as the dollar value of each car's electronics content rises. The printed-circuit-board industry — the substrate everything sits on — is in similar distress. Circuit board and component manufacturing brought in $61.3 billion in 2026, up only 1 percent over five years and actually declining 0.5 percent in 2026 alone. Margins are flat at around 6 percent, under pressure from input costs and price wars. But the bifurcation here is even starker. AI servers and 5G infrastructure are driving explosive demand for high-complexity, high-density boards. Layer counts have jumped from 16 to 20 layers up to 28 to 36 layers. AI servers went from 15 percent of PCB demand in 2025 to over 25 percent in 2026. The players with capital are investing. TTM Technologies opened a new Ultra-HDI (ultra-high-density interconnect) facility in Syracuse, New York in June 2026, designed for aerospace and defense. Jabil announced a $500 million multi-year expansion for US cloud computing and AI data-center hardware, starting mid-2026. Ventec International is building a $17 million facility in Thailand to serve the US and Asia. TTM acquired a 750,000-square-foot facility in Eau Claire, Wisconsin for advanced PCB production targeting data-center networking. But mid-tier and small players are getting squeezed out. TTM itself closed plants in Anaheim and Santa Clara in 2024 and 2025. Camston Wrather liquidated. Import tariffs of 25 to 35 percent on Chinese PCBs made it expensive to buy overseas, but US domestic capacity is so bottlenecked that new customers face long waits just for quotes. Many shops refuse new orders. This created a pricing window for whoever can expand — but expansion requires capital that smaller shops don't have. The bifurcation has created a three-way chasm: large, well-capitalized suppliers that can automate and go after aerospace, AI, and next-gen EV platforms; nearshore or overseas manufacturers in Mexico, Thailand, and China competing on cost; and everyone else, shrinking.

The evidence

  • Automobile Electronics Manufacturing: US market fell to $29.6 billion (2025) from $25.0 billion (2022), a -3% decline in a year when EV demand should have lifted it — a sign that pricing power is gone and volume is shifting offshore
  • Circuit Board Manufacturing: $61.3 billion US market (2026) growing only 1% CAGR since 2021, with 0.5% decline in 2026 alone despite AI/5G tailwinds — bifurcation killing mid-tier players
  • Tariff Impact: 25% January 2026 US tariff on advanced semiconductors hit suppliers locked into fixed-price contracts with no renegotiation path, forcing margin compression or bankruptcy
  • Nearshoring: Mexico attracted $8 billion in automotive capex in 2024 with 2,274 hectares under construction by end-2025, plus Foxconn's $900 million AI campus and ASE's $200 million packaging line — wage and tariff arbitrage draining US capacity
  • Supplier Collapse: 2025 saw auto-supplier bankruptcies exceed any prior year on record, including First Brands Group's Chapter 11 filing; consolidation forced Lear to restructure and Visteon to focus on EV/autonomous capabilities to survive

Who rides it

Large, well-capitalized electronics and PCB manufacturers backed by CHIPS Act grants and private equity win. TTM, Jabil, Ventec, and other players with the capital to automate and move upmarket into AI servers, aerospace, and next-gen EV platforms are expanding (Syracuse Ultra-HDI facility June 2026, $500 million Jabil expansion, $17 million Ventec facility). Lear and Visteon, despite pain, are profitable and positioned to capture EV-specific content. Nearshore suppliers in Mexico and overseas makers are also winning, absorbing investment and orders that used to go to US mid-tier shops. Automakers themselves win in the near term because tariffs and supplier collapse give them negotiating leverage — they can pit remaining suppliers against each other and force consolidation on their own terms.

Who's squeezed

Mid-tier Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers are being crushed. They lack the capital to automate or expand into high-complexity, high-density segments (AI servers, aerospace). They can't compete on cost against Mexico or China. They're locked into fixed-price contracts with automakers, so tariff shocks hit their margins directly. Bankruptcy filings in 2025 exceeded any prior year. Smaller PCB shops face 25- to 35-percent tariffs on Chinese imports, but they can't expand domestic capacity without tens of millions in capital, so they're shrinking instead. Domestic employment in auto electronics is flat to declining, and the skilled trades that used to staff assembly and testing are losing hours.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that US auto electronics and PCB manufacturing becomes a high-skill, capital-intensive oligopoly — and smaller suppliers don't exist. That means US automakers become dependent on a handful of domestic suppliers and offshore alternatives. If one of the large suppliers stumbles (TTM just closed two plants in 2024-2025, Camston Wrather liquidated), there's no backup. For EVs, that means if a battery-management or autonomous-driving chip supplier goes down, entire product lines can stall. Tariff walls and nearshoring also mean that US capacity for commodity electronics shrinks even faster — it's not profitable to make simple stuff domestically anymore. In five years, the US might make only high-end AI and aerospace boards, and everything else moves to Mexico, Thailand, or China. That's a fragile supply chain in a tech race.

In one sentence

The car becomes a computer in a nutshell

The car becomes software-defined instead of engine-defined, flipping the supply chain from mechanical parts to chips and boards — but the US manufacturers who make those boards are imploding under tariffs, fixed contracts, and wage competition from Mexico.
