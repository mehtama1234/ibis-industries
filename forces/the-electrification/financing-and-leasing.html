C · The ripple effects

Financing the switch

When the car goes electric, the money stops flowing the old way.

For a century, car finance worked on a simple logic: you borrow, you drive, the car holds value. But EV residual values just collapsed. A three-year-old EV that was worth 90% of its purchase price in 2022 is worth 40% today. That breaks the math for every lease, every captive lender, every used-car dealer. Now the whole auto-finance system is seizing up.

What changed

The US auto-finance market entered 2026 in crisis. Market size was $173.8 billion in 2026, declining 2% from 2025 as affordability collapsed and credit deteriorated. Monthly payments for new cars now run $750 and up — versus $600-650 in 2022 — because of tariffs (vehicles up $3,000-$10,000), higher interest rates (6.39% average in Q1 2026 versus historic lows), and lending stretching to 84-96 month terms just to keep payments manageable. New-vehicle registrations fell 6% year-over-year despite used-car inventory climbing 5%, signaling buyers are stepping back entirely. Subprime delinquencies hit 6.6% in January 2025, the highest in 31 years, and by Q1 2026, 90+ day delinquencies reached 5.6% — all-time record — pushing repossession toward 3 million vehicles in 2025, matching the Great Recession. The subprime sector is imploding: Tricolor Holdings, one of the largest used-car dealers and subprime lenders, liquidated in October 2025 amid fraud allegations and massive delinquencies. Margins are compressing hard, down to 27-28% from historical 30%+, as lenders build reserves and absorb credit losses.\n\nBut the real knife is EV residual value. Late 2025 data shows a $10+ billion loss cascade: captive lenders like Ford Credit and GM Financial locked into EV lease portfolios backed by residual-value estimates that were 50-60 percentage points too high. An off-lease vehicle surge of 25.7% is coming in 2026, flooding wholesale markets and pushing 3-year-old cars down to an average of $31,548. Lessees stopped signing EV leases because nobody knows what that car will be worth in 36 months — the cornerstone assumption of lease economics is shattered. Fleet car leasing, a $10.6 billion US market growing at 3.7% annually, is also choking: tariff shocks (US weighted-average rates hit 20% in mid-2025, with 25% on Mexican and Canadian vehicles) forced operators to choose between eating $5,000-$8,900 per vehicle or jacking prices when corporate customers were already price-sensitive. Operators who specialize in EV fleet conversions are particularly exposed because the residual-value cliff means a $45,000 three-year EV lease deal can become deeply underwater before the lease matures.\n\nPrime borrowers — those with 720+ credit scores — are weathering it because they have cushion. Prime originations stayed at $99.4 billion in Q1 2026. Top-tier lenders like Ally are even seeing lease volumes up 28.6% year-over-year, capturing a bigger slice of a shrinking pie. But subprime, where buy-here-pay-here dealers and credit-focused lenders operate, is shattered. Subprime originations are down despite total volume gains, and the largest used-car specialists are either filing for bankruptcy or retreating to credit-card and fintech pivots — Atlanticus Holdings acquired Mercury Financial in September 2025 to diversify away from auto. The three-way squeeze — unaffordable payments, record delinquencies, and EV depreciation uncertainty — is wiping out dealers and lenders who can't weather repricing, refinancing, or taking losses on lease returns.

The evidence

  • Auto lending: monthly payments hit $750+ in 2026, up from $600-650 in 2022, killing affordability for middle-income buyers and new-vehicle registrations (down 6% YoY)
  • Subprime credit: delinquencies at 6.6% (January 2025) and 90+ days at 5.6% (Q1 2026), both all-time highs, forcing Tricolor Holdings liquidation (October 2025) and sector consolidation
  • EV leasing: residual values collapsed to 40% of purchase price by late 2025 (from 90% in 2022), triggering $10+ billion losses for captive lenders and killing lease demand
  • Fleet leasing: tariffs pushed US weighted-average rates to 20% in mid-2025 (25% on Mexico/Canada), raising vehicle costs $5,000-$8,900 and forcing repricing
  • Used-car inventory: off-lease volumes surging 25.7% in 2026, with 3-year-old vehicles averaging $31,548, flooding wholesale and crushing residual-value economics

Who rides it

Big banks and captive lenders (Ford Credit, GM Financial, Ally) with deep pockets and portfolio scale are absorbing residual-value losses, repricing leases, and tightening credit toward prime borrowers (720+ score). Prime originations held at $99.4 billion in Q1 2026, and top-tier lessors are actually seeing volume up 28.6% year-over-year by concentrating on affluent customers who can handle higher payments. Fintech platforms betting on performance-based and alternative credit structures are positioning themselves to capture younger, riskier borrowers that traditional lenders abandon. Battery makers and EV makers with captive financing arms are best-positioned if they can weather the residual-value cliff and repricing cycle — market discipline is crushing weak players.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market used-car dealers and independent subprime lenders are getting wiped out. Subprime originations are down, delinquencies are crushing margins, and dealers holding 2023-2024 model-year EV inventory are facing $20,000-plus losses per unit when lease returns hit in 2026-2027. Small fleet operators without scale to absorb tariff shocks are passing costs to customers or retreating from EV adoption. Subprime borrowers are being cut off entirely — Tricolor's October 2025 collapse signals that buy-here-pay-here and credit-card-focused lenders see auto as toxic. Anyone locked into long-term lease contracts with residual-value guarantees based on 2024-2025 pricing is facing massive repricing pressure and potential portfolio losses.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The real risk is a cascading refinancing crisis. If used-car inventory stays flooded and residual values continue to fall into 2026-2027, captive lenders holding EV lease portfolios could face cumulative losses that force fire-sale refinancing or even balance-sheet pressure on their parent manufacturers. Meanwhile, rising repossessions (heading toward 3 million in 2025) mean more used-car supply hitting wholesale, which depresses prices further, which makes financed vehicles worth less, which triggers more defaults. The affordability wall ($750+ monthly payments) plus record subprime delinquencies (5.6% at 90+ days) plus an imploding subprime lending sector creates a feedback loop where credit availability contracts sharply for non-prime buyers — exactly when the car industry needs broad consumer demand to absorb EV capacity and offset shrinking ICE volumes.

In one sentence

Financing the switch in a nutshell

The electrification is hollowing out a century-old financing model built on stable car values and steady credit — now subprime lenders are failing, EV leases are underwater before they mature, and only the largest banks can survive repricing their portfolios.
