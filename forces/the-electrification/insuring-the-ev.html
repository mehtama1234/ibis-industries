Insuring the EV

Higher repair costs and battery risk reprice auto insurance and warranties.

The electric car breaks the math that auto insurers have relied on for a century. Repair costs are rising faster than premiums can follow, batteries fail in ways gasoline engines never did, and the industry—already trapped in back-to-back years of massive losses—is being forced to rebuild its entire pricing model on the fly.

What changed

The commercial auto insurance market lost 4.9 billion USD in 2024 alone, with liability claims reaching 6.4 billion USD—the worst single year on record. For 13 consecutive years, the industry has paid out more in claims than it collected in premiums, a death spiral that only works because investment income masks the rot underneath. The numbers kept getting worse: in 2024, corporate defendants lost 135 nuclear verdict cases totaling 31.3 billion USD in awards, a 52% jump from the year before. The median nuclear verdict hit 51 million USD. The industry raised rates 9-15% annually just to slow the bleeding, but it wasn't enough. By 2025-2026, the damage had accumulated so badly that some carriers started exiting markets entirely or redesigning their entire risk model.

Enter the EV. Repair costs for electric vehicles run 20-40% higher than gas cars, and the industry has almost no historical data on battery replacement costs, degradation curves, or what happens when a battery dies at 60,000 miles vs. the claimed warranty of 10 years. A wrecked Tesla with a damaged battery pack becomes a total loss in situations where a gas car would be economically repairable. Diagnostic codes are opaque—only Tesla can read them—and parts like battery modules cost 10,000-20,000 USD each. Traditional body shops can't repair them. The supply chain is thin: only a handful of certified repair facilities exist in most regions. This made EV claims unpredictable, which made pricing impossible.

By Q1 2026, major insurers responded by launching dedicated EV commercial auto products with telematics-based pricing. GEICO and others rolled out usage-based models that track driving patterns, braking, acceleration, and real-time battery health. Louisiana regulators began requiring insurers to actuarially justify these dashcam discount programs—one of the first regulatory signals that AI-powered pricing could become standard. Simultaneously, the passenger auto insurance market shifted: telematics adoption reached 68% of US policies by 2025, enabling AI models to reduce claims by 24% through smarter underwriting. The industry achieved a 95.3 combined ratio in 2025—its best result since the pandemic—but that window is narrowing. Rate growth slowed from 15-16% annually (2023-2024) to 7-10% in 2024-2025, and several major carriers filed for rate cuts in California, Florida, and Louisiana by early 2026. Progressive's monthly premiums finally exceeded State Farm's in March 2026 for the first time in 84 years, a shift driven entirely by algorithmic pricing and online distribution. The consolidation pressure is real: insurance agency M&A deals fell 12% year-over-year in 2025, hitting the lowest quarterly total since 2016.

The warranty market got crushed. EV batteries create a new liability type: degenerative failure. A lithium pack loses 2-3% of capacity each year; at year eight, you're at 80% range. Is that still "covered"? Extended warranty providers had already been hobbled by aging vehicle fleets—used cars now outnumber new-car warranty sales at 3.2:1 (2025)—and the FTC was pursuing fraud charges (CarShield settled for 10 million USD in July 2024; Endurance Warranty was sued in March 2025). With EVs creating new, harder-to-model risks, the warranty industry froze. Assurant, the market leader, pivoted: it launched Vehicle Care Technology Plus in March 2025 to bundle car service with smartphone repair. The number of warranty firms grew 0.1% annually from 2020-2025—effectively flat—signaling consolidation without growth. Battery packs under warranty became the focal point: who replaces a 12,000 USD battery at year six if the car is worth 18,000 USD? No one wants to answer that question, so insurers and makers are writing warranties that kick the problem downstream to the used-car market.

The evidence

  • Commercial auto insurance: 4.9 billion USD in underwriting losses in 2024; 135 nuclear verdicts totaling 31.3 billion USD (up 52% YoY) with median award of 51 million USD.
  • Auto insurance (passenger): 95.3 combined ratio in 2025; telematics adoption at 68% of US policies; AI models reduced claims by 24%; Progressive surpassed State Farm in March 2026 for first time in 84 years.
  • Extended warranties: 32.7-38.7 billion USD market size (2025-2026); used-car sales outnumber new-car at 3.2:1 ratio; FTC settled CarShield for 10 million USD (July 2024); Assurant launched Vehicle Care Technology Plus (March 2025).
  • Insurance M&A: Agency deals fell 12% year-over-year in 2025 to 695 transactions; Q1 2026 saw 6% further decline to 148 deals, lowest Q1 since 2016.
  • EV insurance: Q1 2026 saw GEICO and others launch telematics-based EV commercial auto products; Louisiana required actuarial justification for dashcam discounts, signaling regulatory embrace of usage-based pricing.

Who rides it

Carriers with AI and telematics capabilities win. Progressive's algorithmic pricing machine beat State Farm for the first time in nearly a century because it can price individual risk faster and more accurately. GEICO and other tech-forward insurers launch dedicated EV products that capture the margin opportunity before competitors catch up. Assurant, the warranty leader, diversifies into bundled services (car + phone repair) to escape the commoditized warranty trap. Repair networks that can read EV diagnostics and stock battery modules become gatekeepers; they can charge premiums because they're the only option.

Who's squeezed

Regional and independent agents get squeezed as M&A deals hit 13-year lows and digital channels (34.7% of warranty sales) bypass dealerships. Traditional body shops that can't repair EVs become obsolete. Smaller warranty providers face a 0.1% annual growth rate—essentially flat—while litigation costs and FTC enforcement drain margins; the trust-damaged warranty segment can't raise prices without losing customers. Customers with older vehicles face spiraling premiums as used-car warranty demand (3.2:1 ratio vs. new) collides with aging EV batteries that nobody knows how to price. State Farm, the 84-year market leader, loses momentum as algorithmic competitors steal monthly premium share.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that EV repair costs and battery failures will shatter the insurance industry's 2025 recovery before it even took hold. Battery degradation data doesn't exist yet—manufacturers are still hiding warranties in fine print—and the first wave of five-year-old EVs hitting the used market will reveal replacement costs that make cars uninsurable or total-loss-prone. If that data shock hits in 2027-2028, carriers will be forced into another round of double-digit rate hikes just as regulatory pressure builds to freeze rates. The industry could splinter: tech-forward carriers escape with profits while traditional insurers face another cycle of losses.

In one sentence

Insuring the EV in a nutshell

The electric car breaks the insurance math that has worked for a century—repair costs are rising faster than premiums, batteries fail in ways engines never did, and an already-broken industry is forced to rebuild its pricing model on data that barely exists.
