Electrification's hunger for copper

EVs need 2.9 times more copper than gas engines, and a supply crunch is already here.

Electric vehicles need nearly three times as much copper as gas cars. Add in power grids upgrading for renewable energy, and copper suddenly went from a commodity that was plentiful to one that is scarce. Prices have doubled since 2023, and the shortage is real—500,000 tonnes missing from the global market in 2026.

What changed

When you swap an internal combustion engine for batteries and a motor, you're swapping steel and iron for copper wiring, battery terminals, and grid infrastructure. An EV needs 2.9 times the copper of a gas car just for the drivetrain, and then the power grid that charges those cars needs even more—substations, transmission lines, transformers, all copper-hungry. By May 2026, copper futures hit an all-time high of $6.58 per pound, capping a 40.86 percent gain over the prior twelve months. The price surge came from exactly one place: EVs and renewable energy went from "the future" to "happening right now," and the mines that supply the world couldn't dig fast enough. Global copper demand hit 27.2 million tonnes in 2025, but supply fell short by 500,000 tonnes forecast for 2026. That is the shortfall that made copper expensive and scrambled the entire value chain.

The US copper mining sector, which pulled in $13.8 billion in 2026, faces a crisis of timing. American ore grades are declining—meaning each tonne of rock yields less copper—and major expansion projects like Resolution Copper (a joint venture with Rio Tinto and BHP) won't produce until the mid-2030s. The same permitting delays that hamstring every big mine in the US mean that even though copper prices are at record highs, new supply cannot flood the market fast enough to erase the shortage. Instead, the US has turned to tariffs. In August 2025, the Trump administration slapped a 50 percent Section 232 tariff on semi-refined copper imports, and by 2027, a 15 to 30 percent broad copper tariff looms. The tariffs were designed to protect domestic smelting and refining, but they also locked in higher prices for anyone downstream who needs copper—the manufacturers and construction companies that actually build with it.

Copper rolling and drawing plants—the factories that turn raw copper cathode into wires, tubes, and sheets—expanded to near full capacity in 2025 and 2026 to feed the surge. The market size jumped from $31.8 billion in 2022 to $41.3 billion in 2026, a 30 percent surge in four years. Plants that process copper are running twenty-four-seven and still cannot keep pace. Southwire, the largest wire maker in the US, acquired United Copper Industries in February 2026 specifically to expand capacity for building wire and EV battery harnesses. But here is the trap: the 50 percent tariff on imported semi-finished copper eliminated foreign competition and pushed prices to $13,842 per tonne in January 2026. Smaller processors got squeezed hard. They cannot raise prices fast enough to pass on the tariff costs to customers, working capital strains, and delivery times stretch. The big players weather it; the mid-market gets caught in between.

Aluminum tells a parallel story but with a twist. EV makers need aluminum for battery enclosures and body panels to offset the weight of the battery pack, and aluminum prices surged 25 percent year-over-year by August 2026 to $3,273 per tonne. But the US primary smelting sector—just four smelters making 683,500 tonnes per year—cannot expand because electricity costs have hit $115 or more per megawatt-hour. Data centers and AI chip makers are outbidding aluminum smelters for renewable power. The result: 85 percent of US-made aluminum is now recycled, up from just 20 to 30 percent in the 1980s. Recycled aluminum is cheaper and cleaner, and it scales fast. Novelis and Century Aluminum raced to restart or open capacity in 2025 and 2026, but primary smelting remains half-idled. The electrification is pulling EVs forward, but it is also bidding away the electricity that smelters need. That creates a strange constraint: materials abundance (recycled metal) collides with electricity scarcity.

The evidence

  • Copper mining (US): $13.8 billion market in 2026, 19,700 workers, facing 500,000-tonne global shortfall by 2026 and permitting delays blocking new capacity until mid-2030s
  • Copper rolling and drawing (US): $41.3 billion market in 2026, up 30% from $31.8 billion in 2022, driven by EV wiring and grid upgrades; 50% tariff on imports effective August 2025
  • Aluminum manufacturing (US): $71.8 billion market by 2026 with 6.09% annual growth, but primary smelters half-idled due to electricity costs of $115+/MWh losing out to data centers
  • EV copper intensity: Electric vehicles require 2.9 times more copper than gas cars, creating a step-change in demand as EV sales scale; global copper demand hit 27.2 million tonnes in 2025
  • Talon Metals acquisition of Eagle nickel mine (December 2025) for $84 million marks Lundin's exit from US nickel despite the mine producing half of US primary nickel, signaling profitability challenges in remote operations

Who rides it

Mining companies and primary metal processors win the short term. Copper and nickel miners see record prices and tariff protection that blocks cheap imports. Recycled-aluminum makers and secondary processors thrive because they need less electricity and can scale fast. Talon Metals gets an undervalued asset (the Eagle mine) and plans aggressive exploration. The wire and cable makers like Southwire acquire rivals to lock in capacity and market share. But the real winner is the company that finds a way to substitute copper or reduce the amount of it per EV—or the company that secures long-term mining rights before the shortage gets worse.

Who's squeezed

EV makers and automakers get squeezed on material costs. Battery wiring, motor terminals, and power electronics all cost more because copper is expensive and tariffed. Construction companies and utilities upgrading the power grid face the same pinch—more copper in the spec, higher invoice. Smaller copper processors and mid-market wire manufacturers bear the brunt: they cannot raise prices fast enough to cover tariff costs and input volatility, creating working-capital strain and compressed margins. Aluminum smelters lose the electricity auction to data centers, leaving primary smelting half-idled in the US and forcing a pivot to recycled aluminum. Lundin Mining's exit from US nickel shows that even major global miners cannot afford to operate here profitably at smaller scale.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The supply gap is real and will persist until Resolution Copper and other major projects come online in the mid-2030s. That is eight to ten years of tight supply and high prices. If tariffs climb to 15 to 30 percent on refined copper in 2027, domestic producers will have pricing power but the entire economy pays the tariff tax. If the price stays elevated, EV makers will redouble efforts to substitute aluminum or other materials, but that pivot takes time and redesign. The biggest risk: electricity costs keep climbing because data centers outbid metal makers for renewable power, shrinking US primary smelting even further and forcing imports despite tariffs. A supply crunch that was supposed to be temporary could lock in permanently higher material costs and slower EV adoption.
