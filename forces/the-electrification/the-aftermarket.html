A · The engine's slow death

The Repair Aftermarket

Oil changes are dying, transmission shops have fewer customers, and the century-old parts economy is shrinking.

The electric car needs far less servicing. No oil changes. No spark plugs. Regenerative braking means brake pads last longer. This simple fact is hollowing out an industry that turned wrenches for one hundred years. The repair shops, parts stores, and remanufacturers built on the gasoline engine are watching their core business dry up, even as the cars themselves get older.

What changed

Electric vehicles structurally need fewer repairs and fewer parts than gas cars, and this is now showing up in the numbers. The US auto parts retail market—$77.1 billion in 2026—is growing at only 1.6% annually, a grinding slowdown, because every Tesla or Chevy Bolt that replaces a gas car is a car that no longer needs an oil change every 3,000 miles, no longer needs spark plugs, no longer needs transmission fluid topped up, and no longer burns through brake pads the same way. The average vehicle on the road is now 12.8 years old (a record as of 2025), which should mean more repairs—but electrification cancels that benefit. Meanwhile, e-commerce—Amazon alone did $13.7 billion in auto parts in 2026—has pulled the price-sensitive DIY buyer straight online, bypassing the corner parts store. The result is a retail network designed for peak volume that is slowly starving.

The collision repair shops—$67.7 billion industry in 2026, growing at only 0.6% annually—face a parallel squeeze. Repairable collision claims dropped over 10% in 2025, signaling that cars themselves are safer (ADAS systems preventing crashes before they happen). But when a repair does arrive, it is now far more complicated and expensive. Sensor calibration for advanced driver assist systems runs $300 to $800 per repair and adds 37.6% to the total bill; these recalibrations now show up in one-third of all direct repair program estimates. The shops that can afford the training and equipment for ADAS work are consolidating: Boyd Group bought Joe Hudson's (258 locations, $1.3 billion) in January 2026; Gerber expanded by 311 shops in 2025; Caliber filed for a public offering in July 2025 and has grown to 1,863 locations. The 68.7% of shops that remain independent—105,000 total—cannot compete. The industry needs 110,000 new technicians by 2026 but attracts almost none; median wage is $47,670, and shop owners report being unable to fill entry-level slots. Insurance reimbursement rates lag the rising cost and complexity of work, and deductibles have climbed—over one in four drivers now carry $1,000+ deductibles—so many skip claims entirely rather than pay out of pocket. The shops are trapped between falling volume and rising technical demand.

The remanufacturing sector—which takes used parts apart, restores them, and sells them for 50% less than new—is experiencing a harder blow. The US remanufactured parts market is estimated at $5.2 billion (2021 baseline) and projects to $74 billion globally by 2026, but that growth masks a fundamental transition. For decades, remanufacturing meant taking old engines, transmissions, alternators, and starters apart, restoring them, and selling them cheap to budget-conscious repair shops and fleet operators. Those parts are becoming obsolete. EV powertrains—high-voltage batteries, electric motors, controllers—require entirely different skills and certification. The remanufactured battery and motor segment is growing at 21.8% annually and projected to hit $12.8 billion by 2034, but it is a small base starting from $2.1 billion in 2025. Worse, the industry was hit by a shock in September 2025 when First Brands Group (parent of Cardone, the largest North American remanufacturer) filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy with $9.3 billion in obligations, allegedly tied to a $2.3 billion factoring fraud. Over 1,200 jobs were cut across Ohio and other facilities. The 25% tariff on imported auto parts (April 2025) further compressed margins—import volumes fell 10% year-over-year, which helped North American suppliers gain market share from 41% to 44%, but only for those with scale and domestic supply chains. Smaller remanufacturers, already displaced by the battery transition, were ground between Cardone's collapse and tariff costs.

What made this scarce: routine maintenance work. What made this cheap: remanufactured gasoline-engine components. What made this risky: being a mid-sized parts retailer or remanufacturer bet on internal combustion. What became newly valuable: battery remanufacturing, ADAS repair expertise, and the scale to absorb tariff shocks and train technicians for software-defined car systems.

The evidence

  • Auto parts retail market grew only 1.6% in 2026 (US), versus 4-5% historical growth; Amazon captured $13.7B of the $77.1B total in 2026
  • Collision repair claims fell over 10% in 2025 (US) despite rising vehicle miles, due to ADAS and crash prevention; body shop market is $67.7B with 0.6% CAGR 2021-2026
  • ADAS sensor calibration now appears in over one-third of repair estimates (2025-2026), adding $300–$800 per repair and 37.6% to total cost, forcing shops to invest in training most cannot afford
  • First Brands Group (parent of Cardone, largest North American remanufacturer) filed Chapter 11 in September 2025 with $9.3B obligations; 1,200+ jobs eliminated
  • EV battery and motor remanufacturing projected to grow at 21.8% CAGR from $2.1B (2025) to $12.8B (2034), replacing traditional engine-and-transmission remanufacturing

Who rides it

The Big Four auto parts retailers—O'Reilly, AutoZone, NAPA, Advance Auto Parts—are winning because they have scale to absorb tariff shocks (April 2025: 25% on parts, May 2025 negotiated down to 30%), invest in e-commerce (U.S. online auto parts grew 4.6% in 2025, forecast 6.7% including Amazon/eBay/Walmart), and adapt to a hybrid DIY/DIFM market. O'Reilly reported record Q1 2026 revenue, 8.1% comparable store sales, and 16% earnings per share growth year-over-year. The large collision repair consolidators—Boyd, Gerber, Caliber—are also winning: they can afford ADAS equipment, technician training pipelines, and absorb insurance reimbursement lags. Battery remanufacturers and circular-economy players backed by OEM commitments and environmental regulation are winning because they are riding a 21.8% annual growth tailwind into a segment with no legacy incumbent dominance.

Who's squeezed

Independent auto parts retailers are being squeezed out by e-commerce and tariffs; smaller collision repair shops (68.7% of 105,000 shops are still independent) cannot afford ADAS training and equipment, cannot compete on price or volume, and face insurance reimbursement rates that lag rising technical complexity. Traditional engine and transmission remanufacturers are losing to electrification—that segment comprised 27% of remanufactured-parts revenue in 2021 and is now becoming commoditized and displaced. The entire skilled trades pipeline is collapsing: the industry needs 110,000 new technicians by 2026 but median wage is $47,670, average technician age is 41, women comprise only 3% of the workforce, and 95% of shop owners cannot fill entry-level slots. Mid-sized remanufacturers hit by the Cardone bankruptcy and tariff volatility (import volumes fell 10% in 2025) are being liquified or acquired at fire-sale prices.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The labor crisis is the single biggest risk. The repair industry needs 110,000 new technicians by 2026 but attracts almost none. Average age is 41, gender diversity is near zero, and wages—$47,670 median—are not competitive against software engineering, skilled trades in renewables, or the skilled manufacturing jobs now returning to the US due to reshoring incentives. If the talent pipeline fails, repair shops will close or turn customers away, collision estimates will take months instead of weeks, used-car values will compress (because fleet managers worry about repair availability), and the entire used-vehicle aftermarket will seize. The second risk is tariff volatility: the 25% tariff on parts (April 2025) dropped to 30% in a 90-day truce (May 2025), and this whipsaws inventory planning, squeezes margins further, and accelerates bankruptcies among smaller players. A return to 25% would be devastating.

In one sentence

The repair aftermarket in a nutshell

The electric car needs far less maintenance—no oil changes, simpler drivetrains, regenerative brakes—and the $77 billion US auto parts retail industry and the body shops that depend on routine repairs are slowing to a crawl while tariffs, consolidation, and a collapsing technician pipeline hollow out the independents.
