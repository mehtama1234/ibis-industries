E · The big picture

The electrification

Value drains from the engine and the pump to the battery, the chip, and the mine.

Electric cars don't just swap an engine for a battery. They tear up the century-old machine that made gasoline cars cheap and simple. Engines, transmissions, gas stations, and all the parts that went into them built entire industries. Batteries, semiconductors, copper, and software are building different ones. The shift is real but lumpy—some pieces are dying, others are being stretched and contorted to survive.

What changed

US battery manufacturing grew from $9.7 billion in 2021 to $61.1 billion in 2026—a 38.5% annual growth rate powered by $27+ billion in federal subsidies and automaker investment. Capacity tripled in five years, from 90 GWh in 2022 to over 160 GWh by 2026. But here's the problem: demand stopped growing. EV sales growth has slowed sharply in the US. Panasonic delayed its Kansas battery plant ramp in July 2025 by 30 GWh because EV demand wasn't there. General Motors shut down Ultium production at two plants through mid-2026. The industry built warehouses for cars nobody's yet buying at these prices. Margins got crushed—manufacturers pivoted frantically to energy storage, a thinner-margin business. By 2026, grid energy storage is already approaching oversupply despite the build-out, which means even the fallback plan is weakening.

What made copper and nickel valuable was the opposite problem: supply can't catch up. An EV uses 2.9 times more copper than a gas car. Renewable energy infrastructure needs even more. Copper prices doubled from 2023 lows to $6.58 per pound in May 2026—an all-time high—because mines can't scale fast enough. A 500,000-tonne supply shortfall is forecast for 2026 alone. Arizona's mines are hitting declining ore grades. Major expansion projects like Resolution Copper won't start producing until the mid-2030s. Lundin Mining sold its Eagle nickel mine to Talon Metals in December 2025, marking a retreat from US mining by a large player because smaller, remote operations can't stay profitable at those scales. Copper was made scarce and valuable by a force nobody controlled—EV adoption just needed to happen a little, everywhere at once.

Gas stations were made irrelevant by a temporary reprieve. The federal EV tax credit expired on September 30, 2025, and EV sales crashed from 10.5% share in Q3 2025 to 5.8% by Q4 2025 and into 2026. Suddenly, fuel demand stopped collapsing. The gas station industry, which had been contracting—147,000 total stations in 2026, down 1.4% annually since 2021—got a breathing room that might not last. But the business itself became structurally worse. Fuel margins are razor-thin: 1-2% per gallon. Convenience stores and prepared food now account for 28.5% of in-store sales; that's where operators chase profit. Shell exited 500 gas stations per year in 2024-2025 to focus on upstream refining. Costco and Walmart undercut gas prices by 20-35 cents per gallon in their pockets, starting price wars that destroy margins for independent operators. Crude is forecast to fall to $52 per barrel in 2026, keeping retail prices low and crushing what little margin was left.

Gasoline engines themselves didn't die; they mutated. Gasoline engine parts manufacturing stayed near flat—$43.8 billion in 2026 versus $37.3 billion in 2022, a meager 1.5% CAGR. OEMs stretched the life of gasoline platforms instead of replacing them. Turbocharging, variable compression, and 48-volt mild-hybrid systems became the way to eke out more miles per gallon and comply with a weakened CAFE standard (which in June 2025 stopped allowing EV credits to count toward compliance). Tariffs—US 25% on auto parts effective April 2025, Mexico 25-50% on non-FTA parts starting January 2026—forced suppliers into nearshoring and consolidation. Smaller players like First Brands filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy in September 2025. Denso, Bosch, and Continental doubled down on EV electronics and thermal systems instead of engines. Engines became a support system for hybrid modules and software. That's the shift: the platform is no longer the engine, and the supplier landscape is being squeezed down to those who can manage complexity in powertrains, not just blocks and bolts.

The evidence

  • Battery manufacturing: $61.1 billion US market in 2026 with 50,442 employees (2026), up 4.3% from 2025, but growth rate fell from 38.5% CAGR (2021-2026) to 3.0% in 2026 alone
  • Copper mining: 500,000-tonne supply shortfall forecast for 2026; copper prices hit record $6.58/lb in May 2026 (40.86% gain over prior 12 months), driven by EV demand and renewable electrification
  • Gas stations: 147,000 establishments in 2026, declining 1.4% annually since 2021; fuel margins at 1-2% per gallon; convenience/food now 28.5% of in-store sales
  • Auto engines: $43.8 billion market in 2026 (1.5% CAGR since 2021) as OEMs pivot to 48-volt mild-hybrid and turbo platforms; CAFE rules reset June 2025 to exclude EV credits
  • EV tax credit expiration: Federal credit ended September 30, 2025, causing EV market share to plunge from 10.5% (Q3 2025) to 5.8% (Q4 2025 onward), temporarily halting fuel demand destruction

Who rides it

Battery and chip makers, copper miners, and EV OEMs win—but unevenly. Battery makers have the scale (50,000+ employees by 2026) and federal protection to survive the current contraction; the pivots to energy storage and LFP chemistry are showing real volumes (57.6 GWh of US LFP cell production in 2025, up 30% year-over-year). Copper and nickel mining win because the demand is real, even if permitting is broken—Talon Metals bought Eagle for $84 million and plans aggressive exploration because US nickel is half the nation's supply. Semiconductor and software suppliers (Denso, Bosch, Continental) are rewarded for complexity; they're the integrators now, not the engine makers. Costco and Walmart win at gas stations—they use fuel as a loss leader to drive store traffic and compensation the margin loss with prepared food and convenience margins of 7-8%. Tesla and Chinese EV makers win if adoption recovers because they've burned through years of losses to prove the platform works; the capital-intensive first-mover is finally becoming an asset again.

Who's squeezed

Gasoline engine suppliers and independent gas station operators get squeezed hardest. Engine parts makers lost pricing power—flat 1.5% growth since 2021—and face tariff shocks (25% on US auto parts effective April 2025, 25-50% on Mexican non-FTA parts starting January 2026) that force smaller suppliers like First Brands into bankruptcy (Chapter 11, September 2025). Independent gas stations have nowhere to hide. Fuel margins are 1-2%; convenience margins are 7-8%; but you can't run a station on just snacks. Major chains like Shell are exiting retail (500 sites per year in 2024-2025) and focusing on profitable refining upstream. Crude is forecast to fall to $52 per barrel in 2026, keeping prices low and crushing what remains. Oil and refining companies that relied on retail networks lose retail presence and are forced to become upstream-only players—profitable but smaller in scope. Lundin Mining's exit from US nickel signals that traditional mining companies can't compete in the smaller, remote, capital-intensive US operations anymore; consolidation is squeezing out mid-tier players.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that demand never catches up to the battery and copper build-out, and the whole system breaks under its own weight. Battery manufacturers built 160 GWh of US capacity on the bet that EV adoption would keep climbing. It didn't. GM paused Ultium production. Panasonic delayed its Kansas ramp by 30 GWh. Energy storage was supposed to be the fallback, but by 2026 grid storage is already approaching oversupply. If that's true, then the $27 billion in subsidies and automaker investment was spent on idle capacity. Copper is the reverse problem: mines can't scale, permitting is broken, and Resolution Copper won't produce until the mid-2030s. If EVs finally do take off—or if renewable buildout accelerates—then the supply shortage becomes a genuine bottleneck, and copper stays expensive, which makes EVs stay expensive, which keeps adoption slow. Either way, something breaks: either overcapacity and wasted capital (batteries), or undersupply and stalled growth (copper). The gap between supply and demand was supposed to close; instead it's widening in both directions at once.

In one sentence

The electrification in a nutshell

The electrification made batteries expensive to build and copper expensive to find, while engines and gas stations became structurally unprofitable, squeezing suppliers and operators caught in between the old machine and the new one.
