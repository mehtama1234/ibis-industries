B · What replaces it

The battery is the new engine

The car's power source went from commodity fuel to specialized hardware—and created an entirely new supply chain nobody quite knows how to run yet.

For a hundred years, the engine was the car. You swapped oil, rebuilt transmissions, and scrapped cars for a few hundred bucks. That machine is over. Now the battery is what costs $10,000 to $15,000, determines range and power, and has to be made, recycled, and managed like a critical material. The whole industry is racing to build capacity for a wave of EVs that won't actually arrive for years—and in the meantime, billions of dollars are sitting in half-empty factories.

What changed

US battery manufacturing exploded on paper. From 2021 to 2026, the industry grew from $9.7 billion to $61.1 billion—a 38.5% compound annual growth rate, driven by the Inflation Reduction Act and automaker bets. The Feds and companies poured $27+ billion into new capacity, expecting EVs to fill every factory. Capacity jumped from 90 GWh in 2022 to over 160 GWh by 2026. It should have been a triumph. But reality hit. EV sales growth stalled. Panasonic delayed its Kansas gigafactory ramp in July 2025, pushing back 30 GWh of capacity. GM halted production at two Ultium plants in Ohio and Tennessee in late 2025, pausing through mid-2026 while the company figured out what to actually make. The problem was simple: the cars weren't coming fast enough. Factories built for 2023 demand were running 30-40% full by 2025. Volume dropped. Margins compressed. The battery industry, which had invested in scale, was drowning in it.

So manufacturers pivoted hard. GM and LG retooled their Spring Hill, Tennessee plant to make lithium iron phosphate batteries for grid storage instead of cars—a $70 million retool that started in March 2026. LG Energy Solution began targeting 60+ GWh of LFP storage capacity globally. US LFP cell production grew 30% year-over-year to 57.6 GWh in 2025. It was the only growth lever left. The trouble is that energy storage margins are thinner than EV batteries, and grid storage itself is already approaching oversupply in the US by 2026. Manufacturers are chasing a market that's also filling up.

Meanwhile, battery recycling—supposed to be the circular-economy hero—is broken. The recycling market reached $6.76 billion in 2025 but is growing at only 9.81% CAGR through 2035 while lithium-ion recycling itself sprints at 22.24% CAGR. That sounds good until you look at the actual numbers. Recycling capacity in the US hit 144,000 tons in 2024 and is racing to 300,000 tons by 2026. But the feedstock won't arrive. The EV recycling wave won't hit 1.3 million units annually until 2030 at earliest. Plants are running 25-30% full. The real killer is chemistry. NMC and NCM batteries (nickel-heavy, older designs) yield about $1,700 per pack in recovered value before operating costs. LFP batteries—which are now rising fast in EVs because they're cheaper and safer—often have negative recovery value. Recyclers lose money on them. In April 2026, Ascend Elements filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy after the DOE canceled $480 million in grants due to slipping timelines. Redwood Materials, which raised $425 million in a January 2026 funding round, cut 10% of its workforce (135 jobs) in early 2026 due to Chinese battery price pressure. The second-life battery market—storing EV packs in grids for 5-10 years instead of shredding them immediately—is filling the void, with 120 GWh of EV packs worldwide reaching end-of-vehicle life in 2026 at 70-80% capacity. But that delays economics, not fixes them.

The core shift is this: the battery went from a fungible commodity (oil) to a specialty asset (engineered hardware) that has to be sourced, managed, and recovered like a natural resource. That made mining, refining, and recycling into bottlenecks. Clarios announced a $6 billion ten-year investment plan in 2026, allocating $1.9 billion for critical minerals processing and $2.5 billion for advanced battery expansion. The industry is betting that it can control its own supply chain. But it's also locked into a bet that didn't pay off fast enough.

The evidence

  • Manufacturing: US battery manufacturing hit $61.1 billion in 2026 on 38.5% CAGR since 2021, but capacity utilization fell to 30-40% by 2025 as EV demand stalled
  • Energy storage: LFP cell production grew 30% year-over-year to 57.6 GWh in 2025 as GM and LG retooled Spring Hill, Tennessee plant for grid storage instead of cars (March 2026 pivot)
  • Recycling: Ascend Elements filed Chapter 11 in April 2026 after DOE canceled $480M in grants; Redwood Materials cut 135 jobs (10% workforce) in early 2026 due to Chinese price pressure
  • Battery chemistry: LFP batteries now command 70-80% capacity but have -$1,800 net recovery value per pack in 2026, while older NMC batteries yield ~$1,700 per pack
  • Feedstock drought: US recycling capacity raced to 300,000 tons by 2026 but actual EV battery recycling feedstock won't arrive at scale until 2030, leaving plants running 25-30% full

Who rides it

Battery makers and recyclers who control the supply chain for critical minerals win. Clarios is betting on this with a $6 billion ten-year plan including $1.9 billion for critical minerals processing. Automakers like Tesla and GM who can secure feedstock or design for second-life batteries also gain. Second-life battery operators and grid storage companies win from the 120 GWh of EV packs reaching end-of-vehicle life in 2026 with 70-80% capacity still usable—that's 5-10 years of revenue before recycling has to kick in. Chinese battery makers, by keeping prices low, forced US competitors to cut jobs and consolidate.

Who's squeezed

Traditional battery recyclers and startups without integrated supply chains lose. Ascend Elements' bankruptcy and Redwood's 10% job cut in early 2026 show the squeeze. Recyclers with LFP-heavy portfolios face negative economics—they pay to dispose of batteries instead of recovering value. Smaller independent plants struggle to fill 300,000 tons of capacity when demand won't arrive until 2030. Panasonic, which delayed its Kansas ramp, sacrificed near-term margin to avoid building for phantom demand. The worst hit: any company that bet the cycle would arrive by 2025 or 2026.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is oversupply turning to stranded assets. US battery and recycling capacity has tripled on subsidies and forecasts that haven't materialized. If EV sales don't recover to 2023 growth rates by 2027-2028, factories built for $61+ billion in demand will sit half-empty for a decade, turning federal investment and company cash into sunk costs. Energy storage, the fallback pivot, is already approaching oversupply by mid-2026. Recycling won't have enough feedstock until 2030. The battery industry could end up with too much infrastructure competing for too little material, collapsing margins further and forcing another round of consolidations and job losses.

In one sentence

The battery is the new engine in a nutshell

The battery industry exploded in capacity on forecasts that didn't arrive, leaving factories half-empty and recyclers running at 25-30% utilization while chasing an EV wave that won't peak until 2030.
