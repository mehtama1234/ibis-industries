E · The big picture

The Pricing-Power Collapse

Medicare cuts and payer power strip pricing from providers and device makers — volume rises with aging, but the price per unit keeps falling, and only scale survives.

Healthcare reimbursement is locked in a squeeze. Medicare cuts rates by 1–2% yearly while demand grows 3–4%, so you need to see more patients just to stay flat. Payers control the price, and only the biggest operators can absorb the margin compression.

What changed

The pricing-power collapse across healthcare has made volume abundant but price per unit scarce. Start with dialysis: the US industry treats 516,837 patients on dialysis in 2025, generating $36 billion in revenue at just 11.7% operating margin—and Medicare rate increases are running at 2.2% for 2026 and only 1.1% projected for 2027, far below wage inflation and labor costs. Fresenius and DaVita are both growing treatment volume 2–3% annually, but revenue-per-treatment is falling in their commercial mix, and CMS's final 2027 ESRD PPS base rate of $299.55 per treatment—a $17.84 increase—translates to only 1.1% total growth after phosphate binder bundling. The message is ruthless: you need more patients, not higher prices.

Orthopedic device makers face the same pressure inverted. Medicare reimbursement for hip replacements has fallen 39% since 2017, and all orthopedic procedures saw a 26% cut from 2016 to 2024, with CMS applying a fresh 2.5% efficiency adjustment in 2026. Yet the aging US population—10,000 Americans turning 65 daily, with 37% of diagnostic tests and 34% of inpatient procedures now driven by the over-65 crowd—still drives steady demand. Stryker captured 67% of US knee procedures on its MAKO robotic platform by year-end 2025, proving that robotic surgery (a $2.6 billion market growing 16–17% annually) is the only escape hatch from commodity pricing. The orthopedic industry margins compressed to 5.6% as of 2022, and consolidation is accelerating: J&J is spinning off DePuy Synthes (valued $20 billion+) precisely because the orthopedic business can't compete at scale against focused players.

Generic pharma is broken. The market contracted from $40.6 billion in 2022 to $28.5 billion in 2026—a -10% CAGR over five years—as PBMs took 80% control of the market and set prices to near-commodity levels. Teva reported 15% US generics growth in 2024 but issued subdued 2025 guidance under ongoing pricing pressure and patent cliffs. India and China now supply 58% of US pharmaceutical imports by weight, with India shipping $10.5 billion annually while depending on China for 70% of raw materials. Congress's February 2026 legislation delinking PBM compensation from drug prices is the most significant regulatory intervention in decades, but it won't raise manufacturer prices—it just shifts the PBM's profit model and leaves makers facing tariff threats (100% tariffs on generic drugs threatened by August 2028) and the race to biosimilars. Generic manufacturers with 5–15% margins are forced to exit the commodity business entirely and pivot to complex molecules and biosimilars (20–40% margins) to survive.

Finally, insurers are the one sector that just recaptured pricing power. After medical cost inflation crushed margins in 2023–2024, the Big Three (UnitedHealth, Humana, CVS/Aetna) are hiking premiums aggressively in 2025–2026: employer-sponsored premiums are rising 9%—the highest in 15 years—while ACA marketplace premiums jumped 18% for 2026, and benchmark plans jumped 26%. The expiration of enhanced ACA subsidies on December 31, 2025 means unsubsidized individual premiums will more than double from $888 to $1,904 annually. Medicaid shrinkage—down 7.6% in 2025 and growing only 0.2% in 2026 due to new work/reporting requirements—lets insurers shed the lowest-margin segment. But that power is temporary and defensive: tariff pressures are raising device and supply costs, Medicaid enrollment is evaporating, and new entrants like Amazon (which launched a GLP-1 management program in April 2026 at $25/month in ~3,000 cities) and TrumpRx.gov (a direct-to-consumer pharmacy that launched February 2026) are siphoning off the most profitable segments—employee health, primary care, and specialty drugs.

Made scarce: pricing power over units and premium growth. Made cheap: individual insurance coverage for the unsubsidized (due to subsidy cliffs), generic drugs (PBM leverage), and outpatient procedures (site-neutral payment rules). Made risky: being a small operator without scale or being locked into low-margin commodity products. Made valuable: being a consolidator with scale (dialysis giants DaVita and Fresenius), owning proprietary innovation (Stryker in robotic surgery), and being an insurer with enough enrollment to absorb margin compression through premium hikes.

The evidence

  • Dialysis: DaVita and Fresenius treat 516,837 US dialysis patients in 2025 at $36 billion annual revenue, 11.7% margin, yet face 2.2% Medicare rate increases for 2026 and only 1.1% projected for 2027—below wage inflation.
  • Orthopedic devices: Medicare reimbursement fell 26% from 2016 to 2024 (39% cut in hip arthroplasty since 2017), yet Stryker's MAKO robotic platform captured 67% of US knee procedures by end-2025, growing at 16–17% annually in a 4.3% overall market.
  • Generic pharma: Market contracted -10% CAGR from $40.6B in 2022 to $28.5B in 2026 as PBMs control 80% of pricing; India and China supply 58% of US pharma imports by weight, undercutting domestic margins.
  • Insurance: Employer premiums up 9% for 2025–2026 (highest in 15 years), ACA benchmark plans up 26% for 2026; expiration of enhanced subsidies December 31, 2025 doubles unsubsidized premiums from $888 to $1,904 annually.
  • New entrants: Amazon's GLP-1 program at $25/month launched April 2026 across ~3,000 cities; TrumpRx.gov (February 2026) offers direct-to-consumer drugs at manufacturer-discounted cash prices, bypassing insurer intermediation.

Who rides it

Consolidators with scale win. DaVita and Fresenius control 65% of US dialysis, absorbing Medicare cuts through operational efficiency and volume growth. Stryker owns the robotic surgery moat—67% of US knee procedures on MAKO by year-end 2025—and can charge premium prices because outcomes improve and hospitals depend on it. Insurers recapture pricing power in 2025–2026 through 9% employer premium hikes and 18–26% ACA increases, allowing them to pass cost growth to employers and individuals. New digital entrants (Amazon, TrumpRx) capture the most profitable segments: primary care, specialty drugs, and self-pay customers who bypass traditional insurance altogether.

Who's squeezed

Independent and regional operators get squeezed. Small dialysis operators can't negotiate with Medicare; small orthopedic device makers can't afford the R&D required for robotic platforms or biosimilars. Generic manufacturers are trapped: Teva's subdued 2025 guidance reflects pricing pressure that no cost-cutting can offset—commodity generics simply don't sustain 5–15% margins anymore. Unsubsidized individual insurance buyers face a cliff: when enhanced ACA subsidies expired December 31, 2025, annual premiums doubled from $888 to $1,904. Medicaid providers lose enrollment (down 7.6% in 2025) as work/reporting requirements shrink the pool, and the rates they receive don't compensate for complexity or volume loss.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is a political fracture over who bears the cost of volume growth. Aging is real—10,000 Americans turn 65 daily, and 37% of diagnostic tests and 34% of inpatient procedures are now driven by the over-65 crowd. Demand for dialysis, joint replacements, and heart procedures will keep growing 3–4% annually. But Medicare cuts reimbursement 1–2% yearly, insurers hike premiums 9–26%, and patients face subsidy cliffs that double their out-of-pocket cost overnight. At some point, the fraying middle (the newly unsubsidized) will demand price controls or break the insurer model entirely. Congress's February 2026 PBM legislation is the first domino; tariff threats on generic drugs and medical devices are the second. If tariffs land (100% on generics by August 2028 is being threatened), or if price controls spread from Medicare to commercial, the entire reimbursement architecture collapses, and no amount of scale will protect consolidators from a sudden repricing shock.

In one sentence

The pricing-power collapse in a nutshell

Medicare cuts reimbursement 1–2% yearly while demand grows 3–4%, making pricing scarce but volume abundant—only consolidators and proprietary innovators survive, and everyone else either exits, merges, or gets bought.
