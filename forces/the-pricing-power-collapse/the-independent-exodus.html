C · The payers hold the power

The independent-practice exodus

Solo dentists and mental health clinics sell to networks and PE to escape the pricing squeeze.

Reimbursement stagnation is forcing independent practitioners out. Dental practices that once thrived solo are now selling to DSO networks at record pace. Mental health clinics are consolidating just as fast, chasing economies of scale and digital scale that independents can't afford. The math is simple: margins compress, costs rise, and only large platforms can negotiate with payers and absorb the hit.

What changed

The pricing squeeze is real and measurable. In dentistry, nearly 60% of independent practitioners report that reimbursement rates have stagnated since 2021, and another 25% say rates have actually fallen. Yet patient demand is climbing—one in five Americans will be 65 or older by 2030, driving volume up. But volume doesn't matter if the price per visit keeps dropping. Dental practice margins fell from 15.3% in 2022 to 15–16% in 2026 as costs pressured profits despite higher throughput. The result is a stampede into DSO (Dental Service Organization) networks. In 2024, about 16% of dentists were DSO-affiliated. By 2026, that number nearly tripled to 39% of practices. In the first half of 2026 alone, 175-plus dental practice locations sold to DSOs and other buyers. New graduates are especially vulnerable: average dental school debt hit $297,800 in 2025, making the solo practice path financially treacherous when reimbursement is flat.

Mental health clinics face a parallel but slightly different squeeze. The market is growing at 4.8% year-over-year, and demand is real—buprenorphine prescriptions nearly tripled to 15.4 million in 2024, and overdose deaths fell from 110,000 in 2023 to about 75,000 in 2024. But reimbursement is tightening. Telehealth claim denials jumped 84% from 2024 to 2025, squeezing the revenue margin for clinics that depend on remote visits. Wages for psychiatrists and counselors are rising faster than revenue can keep up. A 2021 baseline margin of 5.8% is being compressed by wage inflation, and there's a projected shortage of 43,660 to 93,940 adult psychiatrists by 2037. The M&A boom accelerated to 104 behavioral health deals in 2025 (up from 73 in 2024), with mental health subsector accounting for over 80 of those transactions. Solo and small clinics are selling out because they can't compete on scale, technology, or digital reach.

What makes this collapse distinct from typical consolidation is that reimbursement doesn't improve much for the buyers either—they're not conquering the payers, they're just surviving better by cutting overhead, spreading fixed costs across more locations, and building the digital infrastructure payers now demand. A DSO can negotiate slightly more aggressively than a solo practice with 10 chairs, but not enough to reverse the trend. The exodus is driven by desperation, not ambition. The consolidators (PE-backed DSO platforms like Smile Doctors, which acquired myOrthos with 70-plus orthodontic practices across 13 states in 2025) and health systems don't win on price—they win on survival. They absorb the 5.8% margins in mental health and the 15–16% margins in dentistry, cross-subsidize with adjacent services, and outlast the independents.

Graduate debt policy is accelerating the shift. In July 2026, federal Grad PLUS student loans were eliminated for new dental students, and new income-driven repayment plans shifted to 1–10% income-based calculations. This reshapes the calculus: a dentist with $298,000 in debt can repay faster working inside a DSO with a guaranteed salary than betting everything on a solo practice in a reimbursement-flat market. The independence that defined the profession is now a liability, not an asset.

The evidence

  • Dentistry: DSO affiliation jumped from 16.1% in 2024 to 39% of practices by 2026, with 175+ practice locations sold in H1 2026 and average new graduate debt at $297,800 in 2025
  • Dental reimbursement: 60% of practitioners report rates stagnant since 2021, 25% report declines, only 7% received increases, compressing margins despite 1.9% market growth in 2026
  • Behavioral health: 104 M&A deals in 2025 (up from 73 in 2024), with mental health leading at 80+ transactions; market at $42 billion in 2026 growing 4.8% CAGR
  • Mental health reimbursement: Telehealth claim denials rose 84% from 2024 to 2025; margins constrained at 5.8% (2021) by wage inflation outpacing revenue growth
  • Behavioral health workforce: 43 of 44 US states report active psychiatrist and counselor shortages; projected deficit of 43,660 to 93,940 adult psychiatrists by 2037

Who rides it

DSO platforms and PE-backed consolidators. They don't win on negotiating better reimbursement rates—payers still have all the power. They win by spreading fixed costs across dozens of practices, reducing overhead per chair, and building the digital infrastructure (AI imaging, teledentistry, practice management software) that payers now expect. Smile Doctors acquired 70-plus orthodontic practices in 2025; health systems acquire mental health clinics in bulk. They also win on talent acquisition—a dentist fresh out of school with $298,000 in debt can take a guaranteed $200,000+ salary from a DSO, versus the 3-5 year grind of building a solo practice. The system survives by consolidating, not by fighting back against payers.

Who's squeezed

Independent practitioners and small practices. Solo dentists and solo mental health clinics are disappearing. Dental margins fell from 15.3% in 2022 to 15–16% in 2026. Mental health margins stay trapped at 5.8% or worse because wages rise faster than revenue. Staffing shortages—62% of dentists cite hiring as their top concern, and psychiatrist shortages project to 43,000–94,000 by 2037—make it harder for small practices to scale. New graduates are especially squeezed: they inherit $298,000 in debt and face stagnant reimbursement and workforce shortages. The independence that once defined these professions becomes a competitive disadvantage.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The consolidation doesn't solve the underlying problem—it just postpones it. DSOs and health systems are absorbing razor-thin margins and betting on volume and adjacency revenue to survive. Dental margins at 15–16% and mental health margins at 5.8% leave no room for error. If payer reimbursement tightens further (as it historically does during government budget constraints) or if wage inflation accelerates faster than productivity, even the big platforms will struggle. The other risk is that consolidation into 3–4 mega-platforms per region gives those platforms temporary negotiating power, which payers then crush with reference pricing or capitated contracts. The real danger: consolidation stops being an escape route and becomes just another trap.

In one sentence

The independent-practice exodus in a nutshell

When pricing power collapses, independent practitioners have no choice but to sell to DSOs and PE platforms—trading autonomy for survival in a volume-over-price market.
