B · The makers get squeezed

The device price grind

Device makers face 1-2% annual price cuts as Medicare freezes reimbursement and hospitals consolidate their buying power.

Device makers built their business on the assumption that an aging population means growing prices. It turns out they only got half the deal. Yes, volume is rising—more hip replacements, more dialysis, more surgical robots. But the price per unit keeps falling, year after year, and there's nowhere to hide.

What changed

The orthopedic device market is the clearest case. US orthopedic products manufacturing was a $60.6 billion market in 2025, growing at 4.3% annually through 2035, which sounds healthy until you look at the numbers behind it. Margins sit at 5.6%—squeezed down from higher levels just a few years ago. Medicare reimbursement for hip replacements fell 39% since 2017. For standard knee replacements, reimbursement dropped 26% from 2016 to 2024. In January 2026, CMS added a 2.5% efficiency adjustment on top. Hospitals are consolidating their purchasing power at the same time device makers are consolidating their companies. When you're one of 480 establishments competing for contracts from a shrinking number of hospital systems, you have nowhere to go but down on price. Stryker took the MAKO robotic knee surgery platform and made it the standard of care—67% of US knee procedures and 33% of hip procedures ran on MAKO by end of 2025. But even dominance doesn't save you. The price pressure is structural. J&J spun out DePuy Synthes, a $20 billion orthopedic business, signaling that even the giants don't want to be trapped in that margin grind anymore.\n\nDialysis equipment tells the same story with a twist—the volume is there but the reimbursement isn't following. The US dialysis equipment market hit $14.3 billion in 2025 and is growing at 4.8% to 5.2% annually, but margins are just 5–6%, well below the medical device industry average of 12%. Manufacturers face a double squeeze. First, Medicare: the 2026 ESRD base rate rose to $281.71, only 2.2% higher than the year before—far below inflation—so clinics are deferring equipment purchases and pushing that pain onto makers. Second, tariffs are now a material cost. US duties on medical devices from China hit 25–54% in 2025, and when Mexico imposed 35–50% tariffs on Chinese goods starting in January 2026, the cost of components spiked. Baxter sold its entire kidney care business to Carlyle in January 2025 for $3.8 billion, signaling retreat. Even Fresenius, the market leader, is acting defensive—it spent EUR 312 million in September 2025 just to buy out the remaining non-physician stakes in Interwell Health, tightening control over the supply chain because the alternative is losing margin on every unit sold. The one bright spot for device makers is portable and implantable kidney technology in trials, but that's years away. For now, the business is volume with a falling price tag.\n\nThe entire medical device sector faces a restructuring that favors only the largest players. The US medical device manufacturing industry generated $59.4 billion in 2026, growing just 2.4% year over year, with EBIT margins around 12–12.4% under pressure from tariffs and labor costs. A 10% global tariff hit in April 2025, and syringes and needles faced tariffs of 50–100%. J&J alone warned of $400 million in added costs for 2025. Abbott warned of hundreds of millions more. The FDA's new Quality Management System Regulation took effect February 2, 2026, requiring tighter supplier controls and traceability—compliance overhead that only large companies can absorb. The result: $36.5 billion in medtech M&A in the first half of 2026, with consolidation accelerating. The 1,067 establishment players in the industry are shrinking. The top 10 OEMs are buying up smaller innovators, not to grow share but to secure patents and consolidate the cost burden. Scale is the only defense. A company with $60 billion in revenue can absorb tariff shock, FDA compliance, and 1–2% annual price cuts. A company with $500 million in revenue cannot.\n\nWhat did this force make scarce, cheap, risky, and valuable? It made pricing power vanishingly scarce. It made volume cheap—the orthopedic market grew 4.3% annually, but price-per-unit fell 1–2% every year, so real revenue growth came from doing more procedures for less money. It made remaining independent risky—if you're a mid-size device maker, you're either being squeezed out of contracts by larger competitors or forced to compete on price alone. And it made scale newly valuable. Companies with global supply chains, tariff-resilient sourcing, and compliance infrastructure at scale are winning. Smaller players are being acquired or shuttered.

The evidence

  • Orthopedic products: US market $60.6 billion (2025), 4.3% CAGR through 2035, margins 5.6%, but Medicare hip-replacement reimbursement fell 39% since 2017
  • Dialysis equipment: $14.3 billion US market (2025), 4.8–5.2% CAGR, but margins only 5–6% and 2026 ESRD rate rose just 2.2% to $281.71
  • Medical devices: US manufacturing $59.4 billion (2026), 2.4% YoY growth, EBIT margins 12–12.4% under pressure; tariffs on syringes/needles reached 50–100% in 2025
  • Consolidation: $36.5 billion in medtech M&A in H1 2026 as large OEMs absorb smaller players and tariff compliance overhead
  • Robotics exception: MAKO orthopedic surgery market grew to $2.6 billion (2026) at 16–17% annual growth, far outpacing industry 4.3%, but pricing remains under siege

Who rides it

Large device manufacturers with global scale—Medtronic, Abbott, Boston Scientific, Johnson & Johnson, Edwards Lifesciences—are winning. They can absorb tariff shock because they have volume and pricing power with their biggest customers. They can invest in FDA compliance infrastructure and spread the cost across thousands of products. They are buying up smaller innovators to secure patents and consolidate supply chains. Robotic-surgery makers like Stryker are also winning, at least on volume—MAKO procedures are now the standard of care for knee and hip replacement, giving Stryker pricing leverage it didn't have before. But even Stryker is not immune to the margin grind. The real winners are hospital systems and payers, who are consolidating and using their scale to demand 1–2% price cuts year after year.

Who's squeezed

Mid-size and independent device makers are being squeezed out. If you have $500 million to $2 billion in annual revenue, you can't absorb tariff shocks the way Medtronic can. You can't spread FDA compliance costs across 50,000 SKUs. You can't negotiate favorable tariff treatment because you don't have the lobbying power of the giants. You end up being acquired at a discount, or you exit the market. Baxter exiting kidney care is the signal. J&J spinning out DePuy Synthes is the same message in a different package. For smaller orthopedic innovators, the choice is to be bought by Stryker, Zimmer Biomet, or whoever else, or watch your margins compress 1–2% a year until you're no longer viable.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that pricing power doesn't come back. Device makers are betting that once tariff chaos settles or inflation slows, they can raise prices again. They are probably wrong. Medicare reimbursement is set by law, and Congress is not going to vote to pay more for hip replacements. Hospital systems are consolidating and getting more skilled at driving prices down. The aging population keeps getting older, which means volume is still growing, but volume without pricing power is a race to the bottom. If you're a device maker in 2026, you're making more units for less money, and the only way to stay profitable is to get bigger, cut costs more aggressively than your competitors, or exit segments where scale is no longer defensible. That sounds survivable until you realize that tariff costs, labor costs, and compliance costs are all going up in absolute terms even if volume is growing.

In one sentence

The device price grind in a nutshell

Device makers are being forced to choose between scale or exit—there is no middle ground left.
