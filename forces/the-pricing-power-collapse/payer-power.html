C · The payers hold the power

Who Holds the Purse

Insurers are regaining pricing power through premiums and audits, while Medicare Advantage collapses and squeezed providers have nowhere to go.

The insurers hold the purse in American healthcare. They decide how much to charge, what to cover, and how much to pay doctors and hospitals. In 2025-2026, they're pulling the purse tighter than it's been in 15 years—raising premiums aggressively, auditing providers, and exiting markets that don't make money. But the higher they raise premiums, the less room they have to negotiate, and the more providers fight back.

What changed

The big shift is this: for years, health insurers had thin margins and rising medical costs. Employers and patients paid more every year, but doctors and hospitals kept demanding higher fees. Starting in 2023-2024, that broke. Medical cost inflation squeezed insurer margins so hard that in 2025-2026, they stopped negotiating and started dictating. Employer-sponsored premiums rose 9% for 2026—the highest jump in 15 years. ACA marketplace premiums jumped 18%, with benchmark plans jumping 26%. Most dramatically, enhanced ACA subsidies expired on December 31, 2025, meaning unsubsidized individual premiums flipped from $888 to $1,904 annually—more than double overnight. That's not a gradual pinch; that's a market shock. But here's the rub: premiums rose while actual medical costs rose faster. Earned premiums grew 14.3% in 2025, but incurred claims grew 15.5%—insurers are losing money on individual policies even after those steep hikes.

Medicare Advantage is the canary in the coal mine. MA is supposed to be the profit engine—insurers get a capitated per-member fee and pocket the savings if they keep people healthy. Except they can't. In 2025, for the first time in two decades, MA enrollment fell. CMS expected it to drop from 35 million in 2024 to 34 million in 2025, and 2.9 million seniors were forced to switch plans because insurers simply exited unprofitable markets. Meanwhile, the costs were killing them. In 2023, 17% of MA contracts ran above 100% medical loss ratio (MLR), meaning insurers paid out more in claims than they collected in premiums. Another third sit in the 90-95% range with no cushion at all. GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic became surprise costs that no pricing model anticipated. So CMS, facing MA's collapse and its own budget pressure, launched aggressive audits starting May 2025, targeting "upcoding"—where providers report patients as sicker than they are to justify higher risk-adjusted payments. For the first time, CMS is auditing at scale and fast-tracking cases. The threat of clawbacks forces MA plans to tighten networks, cut benefits, and demand deeper discounts from providers.

This is what payer power looks like in practice. Dental insurance shows the same pattern at smaller scale. Plan maximums haven't budged since the 1980s—still $1,000 to $1,500 annually—meaning they're worth 1/17th their 1975 purchasing power. Dentists refuse to take those networks at those reimbursement rates. Network participation collapsed to just 32% by 2024 in major PPO networks. Insurers can't deliver the care their customers paid for. So they're raising premiums and shrinking benefits. Seventeen states passed dental insurance reform laws in 2025, and 30+ more followed in 2026, mandating minimum dental loss ratios, human review of AI claim denials, and bans on retroactive coverage denials. The insurers have less room to maneuver every month.

What makes scarcest right now is provider capacity at any price. Medicaid enrollment is shrinking for the first time since COVID—down 7.6% in 2025 due to new work and reporting requirements. Employers are defecting from group dental, down 2.3% in 2024-2025. And the unsubsidized individual market is about to crater when subsidies expire. Insurers need to raise premiums to cover rising medical costs, but raising premiums drives people out of insurance altogether, especially in the individual market. Volume is collapsing just as costs rise. Tariffs from the Trump administration added 2-3.6% to premium bills across device and supply costs, making it worse. Meanwhile, new entrants like Amazon—now offering GLP-1 drugs at $25/month through One Medical and same-day pharmacy delivery in 3,000 cities by mid-2026—are siphoning the most profitable segments. TrumpRx.gov, launched in February 2026 with GoodRx integration, lets patients buy drugs at manufacturer-discounted cash prices without insurance altogether. Insurers are losing pricing power over drugs even as they're trying to exercise it over providers.

The evidence

  • Health Insurance Market: US health insurance market hit $1.6 trillion in 2026 with 3.9% CAGR growth 2021-2026; margins recovered to 8.30% in 2022 baseline but came under severe pressure 2024-2025, rebounding via rate increases in 2025-2026.
  • Medicare Advantage: HMO provider market reached $359.2 billion in 2026 with 2.4% YoY growth, but profitability collapsed to 3-5% target margins, compressed from historical 4.4%, with 35 million members shrinking and 2.9 million forced exits in 2025.
  • Medicaid Enrollment: Medicaid enrollment shrank 7.6% in 2025 and is expected to grow only 0.2% in 2026 after new work and reporting requirements in the 2025 reconciliation bill, marking the first contraction since COVID.
  • Dental Insurance: Dental insurance market reached $99.9 billion in 2026 with 2.62% CAGR 2026-2035, but enrollment declined 2.3% in 2025 vs 2024 amid network collapse and 37+ state regulatory reforms passed in 2025 alone.
  • Amazon GLP-1 Platform: Amazon launched GLP-1 management program through One Medical in April 2026 offering drugs at $25/month with same-day delivery in 3,000 cities, expanding to 4,500 by year-end, bypassing traditional insurer intermediaries.

Who rides it

Large insurers like UnitedHealthcare, Humana, Cigna, and Aetna are taking back pricing power in 2025-2026 through premium hikes and aggressive cost management, especially in employer-sponsored plans where they can force employers to absorb increases. Dental service organizations (DSOs) are consolidating practices faster than ever (16.1% by 2024, up from 7.2% in 2015), concentrating supply-side bargaining power and reducing insurers' leverage—when one practice is worth negotiating with, insurers have to negotiate. Patients on employer plans with subsidies win by locking in coverage before unsubsidized premiums double. And new entrants like Amazon, bypassing insurance entirely with cash prices, are winning the marginal customer who can't afford premiums.

Who's squeezed

Providers lose hardest. Doctors and hospitals see MA network exits force them to renegotiate at lower rates or lose entire patient populations. Dentists watch network participation collapse as insurers refuse to pay, then get accused of leaving networks when they exit. Solo practices and small group practices can't absorb margin compression and are consolidating into DSOs or going cash-only. Patients in the individual ACA market lose access when subsidies expire—enrollment will crater when unsubsidized premiums double. Regional MA plans like Health Alliance and FirstCarolinaCare ceased all operations except MA by January 2026, abandoning hundreds of thousands of Medicaid and ACA enrollees. Medicaid beneficiaries lose most acutely: enrollment is shrinking for the first time since COVID, and states are cutting faster than CMS anticipated.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that insurers can't actually keep raising premiums without destroying their own customer base. The individual market is one shock away from death spiral. When unsubsidized premiums double from $888 to $1,904 in 2026, enrollment will crater. Younger, healthier people will drop out, leaving only sick and older people in the pool. Claims per member will spike. Insurers will raise premiums again. More people drop out. Each cycle narrows the pool until the market doesn't exist. That's not speculation—it happened in the pre-ACA individual market in the 2000s. Meanwhile, the payer power itself is being undermined. CMS audits for upcoding and tariffs from the Trump administration are raising costs faster than premiums can follow. GLP-1 drugs cost more than anyone modeled. New entrants are siphoning profitable segments. And providers, especially consolidated DSOs and large hospital systems, are pushing back harder—consolidation means fewer players to negotiate with, and they can say no.

In one sentence

Who holds the purse in a nutshell

Insurers regained pricing power by raising premiums sharply in 2025-2026, but medical costs are rising faster, enrollment is collapsing, and new entrants are bypassing them entirely.
