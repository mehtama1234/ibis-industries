D · The tensions

Volume up, price down

Aging brings more patients, but Medicare's 82-87 cent payment per dollar of care means volume alone can't save anyone from margin collapse.

The aging population is real. US hospitals, home care agencies, and device makers are seeing patient and service volume rise year after year. Medicare expanded ambulatory surgery center procedures in 2026. Home care demand grew 6.7% annually from 2021 to 2026. But here's the trap: the more you grow volume, the more you bleed on price. Medicare reimburses hospitals at only 82-87 cents per dollar of actual care cost. Home care agencies face stacked payment cuts—2026 brought a 1.3% reduction after proposing 6.4%. You can't cost-cut your way out of that math.

What changed

Aging was supposed to be a tailwind for healthcare providers. The US population is getting older, and older people need more care—dialysis, joint replacements, wound care, home health aides. The industry grew to 1.5 trillion dollars in 2025 and is adding 2.4 million more home care workers by 2026. But volume without price power is a trap. Medicare sets the price, and it hasn't budged off its punishing rate: hospitals get 82-87 cents back for every dollar of care they deliver, leaving a 13-18 cent shortfall per case. Meanwhile, the hospitals that provide that care face labor costs that exploded 42% since 2020. Nurses struck at Kaiser in January 2026—30,000 workers—because wages haven't kept pace. Home care agencies confront a darker version of the same squeeze: caregiver wages rose from $13.07 per hour in 2014 to $16.77 in 2024, but the agencies can't raise prices to match because Medicare cuts keep falling. The industry absorbed a 1.3% payment cut in 2026 alone, on top of four straight years of cuts. No amount of volume compensates for that gap.

The math is forcing consolidation and collapse. Hospitals went from 7.6% margins in 2022 to just 1-2% by 2025—a structural profitability crisis. Hospital M&A fell to 46 deals in 2025, the lowest in 15 years, but rebounded sharply in Q1 2026 with 22 transactions worth 14.5 billion dollars as survivors went hunting for scale. Twenty healthcare bankruptcies hit in 2025, the highest count in years; Steward Health Care became the largest hospital-sector bankruptcy in 30 years, and Prospect Medical Holdings shed 10 of its 16 hospitals. Home care is worse: 34% of agencies reported profit concerns in 2025-2026, and many run at near-zero or negative margins. The industry is fracturing. Rural and underserved home care agencies are closing outright. Consolidation is aggressive: General Atlantic bought TEAM Services Group for 3 billion dollars in Q2 2026, the largest home care deal on record. Private equity now backs 41% of deal volume, betting that only platforms large enough to absorb the payment cuts and labor inflation will survive.

What's particularly brutal is that the volume growth is real but doesn't help individual providers. Ambulatory surgery centers—which don't carry hospital overhead—now do 9% more procedures than in 2023 and are stealing profitable work from hospitals. Hospitals know this, so they're buying ASCs themselves (Ascension's 3.9 billion dollar AmSurg deal), cannibalizing their own core business. Home care agencies report 95% of their workforce is under moderate-to-severe staffing stress and are turning away 25% of patient requests because they can't afford to staff up at current reimbursement rates. The turnover is grueling: 75% annual turnover among caregivers. Volume, in other words, is rising, but the people delivering it are leaving as fast as they're hired because the work is hard and the pay is low. Only large platforms with access to cheap capital, technology that drives automation (75% of health systems now use at least one AI tool, up from 59% in 2025), and enough scale to negotiate leverage can weather the margin collapse. Everyone else is being squeezed out of the market.

The evidence

  • Hospitals: US market at $1.5 trillion in 2025 with margins compressed from 7.6% (2022) to 1-2% (2025) as Medicare reimburses only 82-87 cents per dollar of care and labor costs rose 42% since 2020
  • Home Care: Market grew 6.7% annually (2021-2026) to $191.6B in 2026, but 34% of agencies reported profit concerns as Medicare cuts stacked for four straight years (1.3% cut finalized for 2026)
  • Consolidation: Hospital M&A dropped to 46 deals in 2025 (lowest in 15 years) before Q1 2026 surge of 22 transactions worth $14.5B; home care PE-backed deals hit 41% of volume
  • Labor: Hospital labor costs up 42% since 2020; home care caregiver wages rose to $16.77/hour in 2024 (from $13.07 in 2014) but 75% annual turnover persists as pay lags other healthcare roles
  • Technology: AI adoption in health systems jumped to 75% (up from 59% in 2025), and General Atlantic's $3B TEAM Services acquisition (Q2 2026) largest home care deal on record, driven by PE consolidation for scale

Who rides it

Large integrated platforms with AI capability and access to capital are winning. Hospital systems that can afford to consolidate, buy ambulatory surgery centers to recapture profitable work, and invest in technology are surviving the margin collapse. Private equity firms are betting on scale: 41% of 2026 home care deals are PE-backed, and the largest acquisitions (General Atlantic's 3 billion dollar TEAM Services buy, Kinderhook's Enhabit take-private) are all bets that only platforms with enough volume and automation can negotiate their way out of Medicare's pricing power. Humana's 5.7 billion dollar Kindred Healthcare acquisition ensures full ownership of a home care giant. These players can absorb the payment cuts, weather the labor shortage, and use technology to reduce headcount and improve margins on the back side.

Who's squeezed

Independent and mid-sized providers are getting squeezed out. Rural home care agencies are closing because they can't absorb Medicare's stacked cuts and can't compete on labor costs. Prospect Medical Holdings filed Chapter 11 in 2025 and shed 10 of 16 hospitals. Steward Health Care became the largest hospital-sector bankruptcy in 30 years. Mid-tier hospitals are consolidating up (M&A surged in Q1 2026 as larger systems acquired regional players for scale) or closing down. For home care, the pain is immediate: agencies turn away 25% of patient requests due to staffing shortages, and 75% caregiver turnover means the workforce itself is voting with its feet. Workers are squeezed too—caregiver wages rose, but not fast enough to offset the brutal conditions and low pay relative to other healthcare roles.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The system is fracturing, and access is degrading even as volume rises. Agencies are closing in rural and underserved areas. Hospitals in weak geographic markets have no scale to negotiate. Home care agencies are turning away a quarter of their patient requests. The underlying problem—Medicare's refusal to raise reimbursement rates while wages and supply costs inflate—is structural, not cyclical. Policymakers could change it, but doing so would require raising Medicare taxes or cutting benefits, neither of which is popular. So the squeeze will persist, leading to consolidation into a few large platforms, smaller providers exiting, and gaps in coverage for vulnerable populations in rural areas. The aging population will get the care it needs, but only if it can access the consolidated platforms that survive.

In one sentence

Volume up, price down in a nutshell

Volume is booming due to an aging population, but Medicare's flat reimbursement rates and soaring labor costs mean only consolidated platforms with scale and automation can survive the pricing collapse.
