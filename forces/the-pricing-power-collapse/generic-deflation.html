B · The makers get squeezed

Generic-Drug Deflation

Medicare-driven price cuts and PBM dominance compress margins and force smaller makers out of commodity generics.

The generic drug market in the US went from $40.6 billion in 2022 to $28.5 billion by 2026—a 30% collapse in four years. This isn't a demand crisis; Americans are taking more pills than ever as the population ages. The price per pill just keeps falling. PBMs now control 80% of what gets paid, and federal regulators are finally cracking down on their markups, but that regulatory whip doesn't help manufacturers. It tightens the screws even harder.

What changed

The generic drug business became two industries in 2026. One is the commodity trap: pills that cost a few cents to make and get squeezed down to fractions of a cent by massive buyers. PBMs—pharmacy benefit managers like Caremark, Express Scripts, and OptumRx—hold the whip. They decide which drugs insurers will cover and at what price, and they pocket the spread between what they pay manufacturers and what they charge employers. The FTC documented how these three companies exploit that gap on specialty generics. By February 2026, Congress had had enough and passed legislation to delink PBM compensation from drug prices, forcing them onto flat service fees instead. That was a landmark moment. But it doesn't raise generic drug prices for manufacturers. It just kills one revenue stream for PBMs without giving manufacturers back their margin.

The other industry is the escape route: complex generics, biosimilars, and first-to-market plays where price compression moves slower. Generic pharmaceutical manufacturers—429 businesses in 2025, down 2.4% annually since 2021—face a simple math problem. Margins on commodity generics collapsed to 5–15%, sometimes lower when a dozen makers chase the same 500-tablet bottle. Branded generics (reformulations, new dosages, combination therapies) still hold 20–40% margins, and biosimilars, in their early years, command higher prices before competition arrives. Teva, the world's largest generic maker, reported 15% US generics growth in 2024 but gave subdued guidance for 2025, a public signal that the commodity game is ending. Sandoz, the generics arm of Novartis, launched three biosimilars in Europe in late 2025 (Afqlir, Jubbonti, Wyost) and signaled it would target mid-to-high single-digit growth through biosimilar expansion in 2026—a frank admission that commodity generics are not a growth business anymore.

The global supply chain is now a second pressure valve. India ships $10.5 billion in pharmaceutical goods to the US annually and depends on China for 70% of raw materials. These imports now account for 58% of US pharmaceutical imports by weight. That abundance keeps commodity generic prices international lows, but it also means any US manufacturer trying to compete on volume will get outbid by players who can run factories in Hyderabad for pennies on the dollar. The Trump administration has signaled 100% tariffs on generic drugs by August 2028, and Congress has advanced the PILLS Act to incentivize US-based manufacturing with subsidies. Both moves are explicit economic warfare against the import-dependent commodity generic model—a signal that the political consensus has shifted. But tariffs don't arrive until 2028, and by then the structural winners (scale players like Teva, Sandoz, and Aurobindo) will have already pivoted away. The FDA, under GDUFA III through September 2027, is accelerating generic approvals—87 new first-time generics approved in 2025—which floods the market with new competitors the day any therapy loses patent protection. That's exactly backward for a compressed-margin business.

What this force made cheap: generic pills themselves—fractions of a cent each. It made risky the business of making commodity generics in the US; the cost of goods is no longer the binding constraint, and regulatory scrutiny, tariff uncertainty, and PBM price-setting are. It made scarce the old profit model of mid-sized generics makers who relied on market share volume. And it made newly valuable the ability to own a portfolio of complex, difficult-to-copy therapeutics—biosimilars, niche molecules, specialty formulations—where a single manufacturer can retain pricing power across a multi-year period before the next maker catches up.

The evidence

  • Generic pharmaceuticals (US): $28.5B market in 2026, -10.0% CAGR 2021-2026; margins 5-15% on commodities, 20-40% on branded; 429 businesses (down 2.4% CAGR) as PBMs and import competition squeeze mid-sized makers.
  • PBM pricing power (US healthcare): 80% of drug-pricing decisions controlled by three firms (Caremark, Express Scripts, OptumRx) until February 2026 congressional intervention moved compensation to flat fees, easing no margin pressure on manufacturers.
  • Biosimilar escape route (Europe, 2025): Sandoz launched Afqlir, Jubbonti, Wyost and signaled mid-to-high single-digit growth targets for 2026 as commodity generics decline—explicit industry pivot to complex molecules.
  • Global import pressure (US pharma, 2025): India and China account for 58% of US pharmaceutical imports by weight; India ships $10.5B annually and depends on China for 70% of raw materials, anchoring commodity generic prices at global lows.
  • FDA approval acceleration (US FDA GDUFA III, 2025): 87 first-time generics approved in 2025, widening competition each time a patent cliff opens—exactly backward for a margin-compressed business.

Who rides it

Scale players with global footprints and biosimilar portfolios win. Teva, Sandoz, Aurobindo, and Cipla—the household names in generics—are large enough to absorb the margin compression and pivot to high-margin specialty and biosimilar products. Teva reported 15% US generics growth in 2024 precisely because it has the scale to escape commodity generics faster than smaller rivals. Biosimilar makers win as pricing power returns: a biosimilar to a $100,000-a-year biologic captures far more than a $0.10 generic pill ever will. FDA accelerating approvals under GDUFA III actually helps the largest makers, who can afford rapid clinical and regulatory work; smaller players can't sprint through the approval gauntlet.

Who's squeezed

Mid-sized generic manufacturers—the regional and specialty players that built their business on volume and moderate scale—get crushed. A company with one factory and a portfolio of 50 commodity generics can't compete on price with Teva's global supply chain or match Sandoz's biosimilar pipeline. Domestic US manufacturers of commodity generics lose the hardest. They can't beat Indian and Chinese costs, and the tariff shield doesn't arrive until August 2028. By then, the consolidation will be done. PBMs lose their most profitable revenue stream once the February 2026 legislation takes full effect and flat-fee compensation replaces the price-spread arbitrage—but that's a redistribution to insurers and employers, not to manufacturers, so it's a win for payers, not a win for the people making pills.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is a structural orphaning of scarce, older generics that don't have scale economics. If a small manufacturer is the only maker of a 30-year-old drug and can't compete on price with imports, it will exit, and supply can tighten. Generic shortages in oncology, antibiotics, and cardiac drugs have already snarled US hospitals multiple times. The FDA is aware and is pushing manufacturers to secure supply chains before tariffs arrive, but the incentive structure is broken: a manufacturer facing 5% margins has no cash to invest in US-based redundancy. The other risk is tariff-driven chaos in 2028. If the Trump administration follows through on 100% generic tariffs by August 2028, import-dependent supply chains will snap, and US manufacturers won't have had time or capital to rebuild domestic capacity. Shortage would follow, and prices would spike—a Pyrrhic victory that harms patients more than it helps domestic makers.

In one sentence

Generic-drug deflation in a nutshell

Generic drug makers are trapped between collapsing commodity margins, PBM price-setting, global import competition, and an accelerating FDA approval pipeline—only the largest and the specialized survive.
