A · The providers get cut

Death by reimbursement cut

Medicare trims rates every year while labor costs rise — providers and device makers watch margins collapse even as volume grows.

The story of US healthcare providers in 2025-2026 is a squeeze you can measure in basis points. Medicare keeps cutting. Inflation keeps rising. Volume ticks up because America is aging and getting sicker, but the price per treatment falls year after year. Only the biggest players with the scale to absorb the cuts survive.

What changed

Start with dialysis. In 2025, 516,837 Americans were on dialysis—a number that will keep rising because the US population is aging and diabetes is endemic. The market is $36 billion today, projected to reach $37.7 billion by 2026, a healthy-sounding 3.5% annual growth. But here's the trap: Medicare rate increases for 2026 are 2.2%. For 2027, they drop to 1.1%. These are the official numbers from CMS. Dialysis labor costs—wages for nurses, techs, and clinic managers—are running 3-5% annually. So every year the gap widens. Dialysis clinics are now at 11.7% operating margins for the strongest players like Fresenius (Q2 2026), and that's only by squeezing harder: cutting costs, shifting mix toward higher-acuity premium services like hemodiafiltration, and pushing patients toward home dialysis (now 17.4% of the dialysis population in 2022, up from 10% in 2016). The two giants—DaVita at 38% market share and Fresenius at 27%—have the scale to invest in new technologies like Fresenius's 5008X system (deployed in 227 US clinics by Q2 2026) and DaVita's high-efficiency dialyzer programs. Smaller independent dialysis operators don't. They're getting picked off or absorbed by regional PE roll-ups that bet on operational excellence to survive the margin grind.\n\nThe same structural trap plays out in ambulatory surgery centers, which is a $46.5 billion market growing at 6.3% year-over-year. That sounds like expansion—and volume-wise it is, because America's aging population needs more procedures. But reimbursement is growing at 2.4-2.9% annually. Wage inflation and operational cost increases are running 3-5%. The result is a bifurcation: large, integrated platforms like USPI maintain 38.6% EBITDA margins in 2025 by absorbing smaller independent ASCs into consolidated operations. Smaller independents, which make up 67% of the 6,500+ Medicare-certified ASCs in the US, are being squeezed to the low-to-mid 30s. Ascension Health's $3.9 billion acquisition of AmSurg (2024), expanding from 58 to 300+ centers across 34 states, is the playbook: buy independents, consolidate overhead, enforce standardized protocols, and negotiate better supply contracts. CMS added 560 new procedures to the ASC Covered Procedures List in 2026—cardiac ablations, lumbar fusion, and others—but these high-acuity procedures carry thinner margins at ASCs than at hospital outpatient departments because site-of-service payment differentials are baked in. The scale players can afford to absorb the thinner margins on a few procedures if it means filling the OR schedule and spreading fixed costs. Smaller operators can't.\n\nDiagnostic imaging is the starkest case of reimbursement collapse. The market is $26.3 billion in 2026, growing at just 1.7% CAGR since 2021. Medicare's conversion factor dropped 2.83% in 2025 alone. Since 2003, MRI reimbursement has fallen 76.5%. CT imaging has fallen 69.1%. These are cumulative declines over two decades, and they're baked into current payment. Imaging centers are at 17-18% margins, below where they need to be to reinvest in AI and infrastructure. Radiologist shortages are acute: 961 diagnostic radiology residency applicants went unmatched in 2025. It takes 130 days to fill a radiology position. Hospitals hold 41.5% of outpatient imaging claims and are capturing share from freestanding centers. Radiology Partners, one of the largest independent imaging networks, had to raise $720 million in fresh equity in February 2026 just to reduce debt—a sign of financial distress. Private equity is consolidating the market aggressively (13 acquisitions in 2025), betting that AI automation and platform scale can restore margins. But smaller independent imaging centers—the ones without PE backing or hospital integration—are being pushed out.\n\nWhat does this force make scarce, cheap, risky, and valuable? It makes small-scale independent providers risky. It makes operational excellence valuable—the ability to cut cost without cutting quality. It makes labor cheap in the sense that providers are forced to compete harder for talent on wage suppression, which means flooded job markets in some geographies and chronic shortages in others. It makes scale a moat: the bigger you are, the more leverage you have to negotiate with suppliers, to spread fixed costs like IT and compliance across more revenue, and to absorb year-over-year margin compression. It makes consolidation valuable—every major player we see (DaVita, Fresenius, USPI, Ascension, Surgery Partners, Radiology Partners) is either acquiring competitors or raising capital to do so. And it makes innovation in the specific direction of operational efficiency—AI-driven diagnostics, high-volume modalities like hemodiafiltration, automated home-based systems—the only viable path to recovery.

The evidence

  • Dialysis: Medicare 2026 rate increase of 2.2%, 2027 projected at 1.1%, vs. 3-5% wage inflation; Fresenius Q2 2026 margin 11.7% (down from 15-16% historical)
  • ASC market: Reimbursement growth 2.4-2.9% annually vs. 3-5% cost inflation; USPI EBITDA margin 38.6% (2025) while smaller independents compress to low-to-mid 30s
  • Imaging: Medicare conversion factor down 2.83% in 2025; MRI payments fallen 76.5% since 2003, CT by 69.1%; 961 radiology residents unmatched in 2025
  • Consolidation: Ascension AmSurg deal ($3.9B, 2024); Radiology Partners equity raise ($720M, Feb 2026); 13 imaging PE acquisitions (2025)
  • Dialysis volume: 516,837 patients on dialysis in 2025; home dialysis 17.4% of population (2022), up from 10% (2016); new-patient peritoneal dialysis starts at 14.1% (2023)

Who rides it

The big, consolidated players survive and even thrive. DaVita and Fresenius control 65% of the US dialysis market and have the scale to invest in new modalities (Fresenius's 5008X deployed in 227 clinics by Q2 2026, DaVita's high-efficiency dialyzer rollout). They can absorb Medicare's 1-2% rate cuts because they're spreading costs across hundreds of clinics and can negotiate supply contracts as a bloc. Large ASC platforms like USPI maintain 38.6% EBITDA margins by rolling up independents and standardizing operations. Hospitals acquire independent imaging centers and leverage their existing radiology infrastructure to cut costs. Private equity firms consolidate these fragmented markets (imaging had 13 PE acquisitions in 2025 alone) and use AI and platform scale to restore margins. The consolidators win. The tech winners are also clear: Fresenius and DaVita are investing in high-volume, premium modalities; imaging centers that adopt AI for automation and teleradiology reduce labor costs and fill capacity. GE HealthCare's $2.3 billion acquisition of Intelerad (Nov 2025) signals that AI medical imaging software is now a prerequisite for scale."

Who's squeezed

Independent and regional providers get squeezed out. Small-to-midsize dialysis operators without DaVita or Fresenius's resources watch margins collapse faster than they can cut costs. Smaller ASCs—the 67% that are independently owned—face 2.4-2.9% reimbursement growth against 3-5% wage inflation and are losing market share to hospital-integrated and PE-backed platforms. Independent imaging centers without hospital backing or PE capital can't afford the AI infrastructure needed to compete. Radiologists feel this too: 961 went unmatched in residency matching (2025), but those who find work face pressure to increase throughput because centers need volume to survive margin compression. Rural providers are especially vulnerable: dialysis clinics in rural areas report labor shortages because wage suppression means they can't compete with urban centers, and smaller facilities can't afford turnkey staffing solutions like locum tenens (which ASCs are using up 17% more of since 2024). The device and diagnostic software supply chain also faces pressure: as margins compress, providers demand steeper discounts and longer payment terms, shifting risk to suppliers."

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is a hollowing out of the middle market—independent and regional providers disappearing so fast that only national consolidated platforms and hospitals remain. This concentrates market power and removes competition. Dialysis is already at 65% market share for two players (DaVita + Fresenius). Imaging is consolidating just as fast. ASCs are polarizing. If reimbursement keeps falling while labor costs keep rising, smaller operators won't have time to invest in the operational efficiencies or AI automation that could save them—they'll be acquired or closed first. The second risk is that this margin compression forces providers to cut corners on care. Dialysis clinics that are perpetually margin-squeezed might defer equipment upgrades or underfund patient support services. Imaging centers that can't afford radiologists might delay diagnoses. A third risk is geographic access: rural and lower-income urban markets are the least profitable for dialysis, ASCs, and imaging. If only the big platforms survive, coverage will concentrate in higher-income, higher-payer-mix geographies, leaving entire regions without accessible services."

In one sentence

Death by reimbursement cut in a nutshell

Medicare cuts compound every year while labor costs rise, forcing independent providers into a choice between consolidation, collapse, or radical cost cuts that hollow out the middle market.
