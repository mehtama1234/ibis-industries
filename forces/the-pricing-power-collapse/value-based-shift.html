D · The tensions

The value-based bet

Providers and insurers are racing to move surgery and complex care out of hospitals and into cheaper sites — but reimbursement lags behind the costs of making it work.

Hospitals and insurers know they're losing money on every claim. The fix they're betting on: move surgeries and procedures out of expensive inpatient settings into cheaper ambulatory surgery centers, urgent care, and home-based models. Reimbursement is low. Labor is scarce. But if you can run at massive scale, the math might work.

What changed

The $46.5 billion US ambulatory surgery center market is the proving ground for this gamble. Hospitals and private equity are buying up independent ASCs at a frantic pace—Ascension Health paid $3.9 billion for AmSurg in 2024, expanding from 58 to over 300 ASCs across 34 states in a single swallow. Surgery Partners shed hospital assets (selling two Idaho hospitals for $1.15 billion to Intermountain in 2025), signaling a hard pivot: surgeries belong at ASCs, not hospital outpatient departments, because the site-of-service payment gap is real money.

The problem is reimbursement growth does not keep up with reality. CMS added 560 new procedures to the ASC Covered Procedures List in 2026—cardiac ablations, lumbar fusion, complex spine work that used to live in hospitals. But the payment rates for those procedures at ASCs are thinner than in hospital outpatient settings, by design. Meanwhile, wage inflation and operational costs have not slowed. Large platforms like United Surgical Partners (USPI) still hit 38.6% EBITDA margins in 2025, but smaller independents have been compressed into the low-to-mid 30s. Anesthesia shortages are acute—locum tenens use jumped 17% since 2024 and is projected to climb another 5% in 2026. Nursing shortages are equally severe, as hospitals and ASCs bid against each other for the same scarce staff.

The result is hard polarization. Scaled platforms—Ascension, Cleveland Clinic (via Regent Surgical), General Atlantic (via the National Platform acquisition of Legent Health in October 2025)—can absorb margin pressure because they negotiate at volume and spread overhead across hundreds of centers. They can also absorb the compliance burden. A solo or small-group ASC cannot. The value-based escape hatch works only if you run enough volume to drop the per-unit cost below what a hospital can achieve. That math is physics: if CMS pays the same rate for a cardiac ablation at an ASC as at a hospital outpatient department, but the ASC has half the overhead, the ASC wins on margin. But if the ASC has to pay locum anesthesia at $3,000 a day because staff is scarce, and the hospital outpatient department has staff on salary, the hospital wins. Scale determines which side of that crossover you land on.

The HMO side reinforces the pressure. Medical loss ratios (MLRs) are in crisis: 17% of Medicare Advantage contracts ran above 100% MLR in 2023, and another third sit in the 90–95% range with no cushion. Earned premiums rose 14.3% in 2025, but incurred claims grew 15.5%—meaning insurers are losing money on the policies themselves. GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic are surprise costs that no model predicted. CMS is auditing upcoding aggressively (May 2025 directive). And the aging population is not choosing cheaper sites—it is arriving sick and complex. So the only lever left is to steer volume away from expensive venues. Medicare Advantage enrollment itself contracted for the first time in two decades, falling from 35 million in 2024 to around 34 million in 2025, with as many as 2.9 million seniors forced to switch plans because insurers exited unprofitable markets. By January 2026, regional insurers like Health Alliance and FirstCarolinaCare had ceased all operations except Medicare Advantage itself—a kind of death spiral where the only line of business left is the one losing the most money.

The evidence

  • Ambulatory Surgery Centers: $46.5 billion market (2026) with 6.3% CAGR and 38.6% EBITDA margins for large platforms, but reimbursement growth of 2.4-2.9% annually fails to match wage inflation
  • Medicare Advantage HMOs: 17% of MA contracts above 100% MLR in 2023; earned premiums rose 14.3% in 2025 but incurred claims grew 15.5%, forcing insurers to exit unprofitable markets
  • Provider Consolidation: Ascension's $3.9 billion AmSurg acquisition (2024) and Surgery Partners' $1.15 billion hospital divestment (2025) signal hard shift from hospital inpatient to ASC-based care
  • CMS Procedure Expansion: 560 new procedures added to ASC Covered Procedures List in 2026, including cardiac ablations and lumbar fusion, but with site-of-service payment differentials favoring hospitals
  • Labor Market Squeeze: Anesthesia locum tenens use jumped 17% since 2024 and projected to climb 5% more in 2026; nursing shortages equally acute as hospitals and ASCs compete for staff

Who rides it

Scaled ASC platforms and integrated health systems win. They have the volume to negotiate rates, the capital to absorb margin compression, and the scale to absorb wage inflation across hundreds of centers. Ascension, Cleveland Clinic, and the new platforms launched by General Atlantic and Hospital for Special Surgery can move complex procedures to cheaper sites and still hit acceptable margins because they amortize overhead and staffing costs across massive patient volumes. For them, the pricing collapse is not a crisis—it is consolidation opportunity, a chance to buy independents at fire-sale prices and fold them into systems where the economics work at scale.

Who's squeezed

Independent and mid-size ASCs are squeezed. They cannot negotiate volume discounts with suppliers or labor. They pay locum anesthesia at market rates ($3,000+ per day) because they have no staffing bench. They lose higher-margin procedures to integrated platforms because health systems steer patients to their own ASCs. Small HMO providers and regional insurers are squeezed hardest—Health Alliance and FirstCarolinaCare have effectively surrendered, ceasing all operations except Medicare Advantage (the loss-making line) as of January 2026. Solo physicians who own ASCs are being bought out or pushed into ACOs where they lose autonomy and accept lower per-procedure fees in exchange for volume.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The value-based escape hatch depends on a bet that is not certain: that the cost of moving volume to cheaper sites will fall faster than reimbursement declines. But the data shows reimbursement growth (2.4-2.9% annually) is already lagging wage inflation and operational cost increases. Labor shortages are worsening, not improving—anesthesia and nursing are both under acute pressure, and there is no obvious way to automate those roles. If labor costs keep rising faster than reimbursement, the margin advantage of ASCs over hospitals evaporates, and the entire consolidation thesis breaks. Worse: if CMS audits upcoding more aggressively (as it promised in May 2025) and starts denying claims, or if it cuts ASC reimbursement to align with hospital outpatient departments, the economics reverse overnight. The scaled platforms can survive that shock because they have other lines of business. Independent ASCs and small HMO providers cannot.

In one sentence

The value-based bet in a nutshell

When price-per-unit falls, the only escape is to squeeze costs by moving procedures to cheaper venues like ambulatory surgery centers — a bet that only scaled operators can win.
