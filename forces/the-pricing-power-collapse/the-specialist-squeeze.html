A · The providers get cut

The specialist squeeze

Medicare cuts and administrative burden push solo practitioners into consolidation and PE deals.

Specialists used to own their practices. Not anymore. Medicare reimbursement cuts, rising overhead, and the sheer weight of billing compliance have made solo practice unsustainable. Cardiologists, dermatologists, and OB-GYNs are getting absorbed into hospital systems and private equity platforms—not because they want scale, but because they can't survive alone.

What changed

Medicare's squeeze on procedure pricing is relentless and specific. Cardiologists saw their reimbursement fall 29% from 2005 to 2021 while hospital payments rose 70%—a 99-point gap that tells you everything about where the money goes. Then in 2026, the CMS took a sledgehammer: stress tests dropped from $311.40 to $220.60 per procedure, amyloid PET imaging was slashed 57%, and left atrial appendage closures—critical for heart failure patients—lost 27% in work value. A cardiologist doing 20 stress tests a month just lost $1,820 in gross revenue overnight. Dermatologists are watching a 9% reimbursement cut proposed for 2027, even as they're still heavily dependent on Medicare (though they're hedging by expanding cosmetic practices, where margins hit 30-50% instead of 10-15%). OB-GYNs face a different squeeze: birth rates hit an all-time low of 1.6 children per woman in 2026 (3.6 million births, down 1% from 2024), which means fewer obstetric cases, but they're also stuck in abortion-ban states where Idaho alone lost 35% of its OB-GYN workforce between mid-2022 and end-2024. Residency applications to ban states dropped 6.7% in 2024 while applications to abortion-legal states barely budged at 0.4% growth. The result: fewer physicians in high-restriction states doing fewer deliveries, while those who remain face expanded liability and regulatory uncertainty.\n\nThe cost side of the equation is moving just as fast. Private practice economics are no longer viable. The cardiology industry has compressed from 240,000 establishments in 2021 to 165,000 in 2025 as consolidation accelerates; 90% of cardiologists now work for hospitals or PE-backed groups instead of running their own shops. Dermatology is 85% fragmented (12,000 dermatologists in 6,000 establishments), but 35+ PE-backed multi-state platforms are swallowing independent practices at record pace—16 buyout deals completed through October 2025, with multiples at 3-5x EBITDA for solo practices and 4-9x for small groups. The draw isn't prestige or scale efficiency; it's survival. EMR mandates, compliance complexity, and rising staff costs are eating into margins that Medicare cuts have already shrunk. A solo cardiologist used to earn a 13.9% net margin (2025 baseline); that's before paying for the new AI workload and the administrative staff it now takes to track 7,700 procedural codes that CMS changed in one rule drop.\n\nTechnology is adding a strange wrinkle. Remote patient monitoring in cardiology grew to $4.68 billion in 2025, and 87% of physicians said telehealth adoption will keep rising. AI-enabled ECG interpretation and DermaSensor (FDA-approved in 2024 for melanoma detection in primary care) are shifting diagnostic work to non-specialists. That's volume erosion for specialists who relied on referral streams—a cardiologist's patient population can now be partially managed remotely or screened by a primary-care doctor with an AI tool. Meanwhile, cardiologist shortage is severe: the ratio will deteriorate from 1 per 1,087 patients (2025) to 1 per 1,700 patients (2035), with 547 cardiologists retiring annually by 2031. Burnout is real. None of this is getting better.\n\nThe only path forward for most specialists is consolidation. Hospital systems now employ 47% of OB-GYNs (2024), up from a fragmented private-practice model a decade ago. PE platforms in dermatology saw dermatologists gain 3-5% reimbursement improvements within 18 months of acquisition—not from new prices, but from operational leverage and coding optimization. Cardiologists have already made the move: H1 2025 saw six PE acquisitions in cardiology, matching the entire 2024 volume. They're also expanding adjacent services—cosmetic dermatology margins are so much fatter (12.4% CAGR growth in the medical aesthetics market, global market jumped from $19.55B in 2025 to $22.59B in 2026) that dermatologists are pouring investment into lasers and injectables as insurance against Medicare. For OB-GYNs, the pathway is into health systems that can absorb malpractice liability, hire midwives to fill obstetric slots, and diversify revenue into preventive care and contraception—which is booming even as births crash.

The evidence

  • Cardiologists: Medicare stress test reimbursement fell from $311.40 to $220.60 per procedure in 2026, while the cardiology industry collapsed from 240,000 establishments (2021) to 165,000 (2025) as 90% of cardiologists moved into hospital or PE-backed employment
  • Dermatologists: 16 PE acquisitions closed through October 2025 with 35+ active platforms acquiring solos at 3-5x EBITDA, as Medicare proposed a 9% reimbursement cut for 2027 while cosmetic dermatology margins (30-50%) outpaced medical dermatology (10-15%)
  • OB-GYNs: Idaho lost 35% of its OB-GYN workforce from mid-2022 to end-2024 due to abortion bans, fertility hit a historic low of 1.6 children per woman in 2026, and residency applications to ban states dropped 6.7% in 2024
  • Cardiologists: The cardiologist-to-patient ratio will deteriorate from 1 per 1,087 patients (2025) to 1 per 1,700 patients (2035), with 547 cardiologists retiring annually by 2031 and burnout-driven workforce shortage projected
  • Dermatology: The global medical aesthetics market jumped from $19.55B (2025) to $22.59B (2026) at 12.4% CAGR, with Botox (cosmetic) valued at $9.84B in 2025 and expected to reach $18.76B by 2035, driving consolidation around cosmetic-heavy PE platforms

Who rides it

Hospitals and PE-backed platforms win. Hospitals gain physician employment (47% of OB-GYNs in 2024), liability insurance bundled with the health system, and the ability to absorb reimbursement cuts through operational leverage. PE platforms in dermatology see acquisitions yield 3-5% reimbursement gains within 18 months post-close through coding optimization and billing at scale. Larger groups can hire compliance staff, negotiate EMR contracts as a bloc, and diversify revenue streams—cardiologists adding remote monitoring ($4.68B market in 2025), dermatologists expanding cosmetic services (12.4% CAGR growth). The big platforms also have capital to invest in AI and technology—AI-enabled ECG interpretation, DermaSensor for primary-care melanoma screening—that shifts routine work down the chain or automates it away, preserving profits on the high-value cases that only specialists do.

Who's squeezed

Solo and small-group specialists get squeezed. Cardiologists going it alone can no longer absorb $91/procedure cuts on stress tests (a routine revenue item) or 57% slashes on imaging. Dermatologists face a 9% Medicare cut in 2027 while their solo-practice economics deteriorate. OB-GYNs in abortion-ban states are fleeing—Idaho's 35% exodus is just the visible part; smaller state-level losses add up. Even as they're forced into consolidation, specialists surrender autonomy, accept lower profit-share agreements with PE buyers (working for PE at lower take-home rate but with the platform's compliance and billing infrastructure), and face volume compression from AI-assisted primary-care screening and telehealth. Birth volume is collapsing (1.6 fertility rate, 3.6M births), cardiologist supply is shrinking relative to an aging population that needs more care, and dermatologists are competing for a fixed pool of Medicare referrals. The solo specialist model is functionally extinct.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that consolidation simply passes Medicare cuts down the line without improving outcomes. A PE platform acquires 20 solo dermatology practices, rolls them into one billing system, and gains 3-5% efficiency on the margins—but that gain is temporary. The 9% reimbursement cut in 2027 erases those gains within months, and the PE owner either forces volume expansion (which hits saturation) or cuts corners on care. For cardiologists, the math is brutal: 547 retiring annually by 2031, 1 cardiologist per 1,700 patients by 2035, and an aging population with rising heart disease prevalence (41.9% obesity, 7.7 million heart failure cases). There aren't enough cardiologists to go around, yet reimbursement keeps falling. Remote monitoring and AI screening may take some load off, but they're not a replacement for interventional procedures or complex diagnosis—and those are exactly the procedures Medicare is cutting. For OB-GYNs, the risk is geographic collapse of obstetric capacity in ban states. If 35% of physicians flee, residency slots empty, and births decline further, rural obstetrics disappears—and then abortion-legal states become the only places with robust maternity capacity, forcing patients to travel or forcing births to happen with inadequate care. The system isn't rebalancing toward better efficiency; it's fracturing.

In one sentence

The specialist squeeze in a nutshell

Medicare cuts and administrative burden have made solo specialist practice uneconomical, forcing consolidation into hospitals and PE platforms that gain efficiency but may simply extract value without solving the underlying shortage of specialists.
