A · The body wears out

The chronic-disease machine

Kidney and heart disease scale with age into recurring, consolidated, reimbursement-dependent care.

America's 516,837 dialysis patients and millions with heart disease are locked into recurring clinical visits, medication refills, and device checks. This is the care machine: massive, unavoidable, profit-thin, and increasingly controlled by consolidators squeezing wages while Medicare slowly strangles reimbursement.

What changed

The boomers entering their 60s and 70s have made chronic disease the backbone of American healthcare. Kidney failure used to be rare. Heart disease was an acute event. Now both are chronic machines that run for decades—dialysis every other day, heart monitoring every week, medication adjustments every month. The dialysis industry alone hit $36 billion in revenue in 2025, growing at a steady 3.5% per year through 2035, treating more patients than ever before. But reimbursement is collapsing. CMS raised the ESRD base rate only 2.2% in 2026 to $281.71 per treatment, and for 2027 it's projecting a 1.1% increase to $299.55—both far below wage inflation and supply-chain costs. When Medicare pays slowly and grows slower, clinics stop buying new equipment. Dialysis equipment manufacturers face the same squeeze: they grew at 4.8% in 2025 but operate on razor-thin 5–6% margins, locked into a market where half the buyers are government programs with hard ceilings. Meanwhile, two giants—DaVita and Fresenius—control 65% of the dialysis market, using scale to absorb the margin pressure. Fresenius reported an 11.7% operating margin in Q2 2026, but independent and smaller regional operators can't match that. What was once a collection of local dialysis clinics is now a consolidated industry run by PE roll-ups and two public companies racing for cost control.

Cardiology tells the same story from another angle. The cardiologist shortage is real: for every 1,087 cardiac patients in 2025, there's one board-certified cardiologist. By 2035, that ratio craters to one per 1,700 patients. Heart disease still kills more Americans than anything else—one in four deaths—and obesity at 41.9% in 2025–2026 keeps pushing heart failure cases up from 6.7 to 7.7 million in under five years. But Medicare payments fell 29% from 2005 to 2021, while the paperwork load exploded. Ninety percent of cardiologists now work for hospitals or consolidators instead of private practice, because solo practice is dead. The 2026 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule slashed payment for critical procedures: stress tests fell from $311.40 to $220.60 per visit, amyloid PET imaging was cut 57%, and left atrial appendage closures dropped 27% in work value. A cardiologist needs to see more patients or compress visits to earn the same income, both of which guarantee burnout. The field is projected to lose 547 cardiologists per year by 2031 from retirements and exits. Private equity made six cardiology practice acquisitions in the first half of 2025 alone—matching the entire 2024 volume—because consolidators can squeeze costs and automate admin in ways solo doctors can't.

What did the aging population make scarce? Cardiologists. Nephrologists. Qualified dialysis technicians. Rural capacity. Clinical time. What it made cheap? Labor for smaller operators, because the giants bid down wages by consolidating supply. What it made risky? Independence. Small regional dialysis operators and private-practice cardiologists face margin compression so tight they have to sell or fail. What it made newly valuable? Scale, automation, remote monitoring, and the ability to negotiate with Medicare. Baxter saw the writing on the wall in January 2025 and sold its kidney care business to Carlyle for $3.8 billion, spinning off Vantive with $1 billion committed to wearables and implantables by 2028—betting that home dialysis and portable devices are where real margins might exist. Fresenius doubled down, investing EUR 312 million in September 2025 to buy out non-physician stakes in Interwell Health, tightening control of home delivery networks. Both moves signal the same insight: the future profit is not in clinic visits or traditional dialysis but in portable, wearable, and eventually implantable therapies. Home dialysis grew from 8.5% of the US dialysis population in 2012 to 14.5% in 2025, and CMS is incentivizing the shift with expanded reimbursement codes and training add-ons. But that shift takes capital, R&D, and regulatory navigation—exactly what two big players and well-backed startups can do, not small clinics.

The evidence

  • Dialysis: DaVita and Fresenius commanded 65% of the $36B 2025 US market, with Fresenius reporting 11.7% operating margins in Q2 2026 on its 5008X CAREsystem deployed in 227 clinics.
  • Dialysis Equipment: US manufacturers faced tariff squeezes (25–54% US duties on Chinese devices in 2025, Mexico 35–50% on Chinese inputs from Jan 2026) while operating on 5–6% margins in a market frozen by Medicare rate caps.
  • Home Dialysis: Adoption climbed from 8.5% in 2012 to 14.5% in 2025, with Fresenius investing EUR 312M in Sept 2025 to acquire all non-physician stakes in Interwell Health, signaling capital-intensive consolidation around home delivery.
  • Cardiology Consolidation: Six PE-backed cardiology acquisitions occurred in H1 2025 (matching all of 2024), as Medicare cuts (stress tests −$91/procedure, amyloid PET −57%, LAA closure −27%) forced 90% of cardiologists into hospital or PE employment.
  • Cardiology Shortage: The field faces 1 cardiologist per 1,700 patients by 2035, losing 547 doctors annually by 2031, while heart failure cases grew from 6.7 to 7.7M adults in under five years and obesity hit 41.9% in 2025–2026.

Who rides it

Consolidators—DaVita, Fresenius, and PE firms backing regional roll-ups and cardiology groups—win by absorbing margin pressure through scale, cost control, and M&A. They can weather a 2.2% Medicare rate hike because they squeeze labor, negotiate supply, and deploy automation. Baxter and Fresenius win by pivoting toward home dialysis and wearable/implantable therapies, where margins might exist if they can beat the tariff squeeze and prove portable devices to regulators. Telehealth and remote patient monitoring vendors win by riding consolidator spend on automation and care-coordinate software—RPM revenue in cardiology hit $4.68 billion in 2025 as a cost-control tool, not clinical innovation.

Who's squeezed

Independents and small regional operators get squeezed out. The 142,000–156,000 dialysis workers face wage pressure as DaVita and Fresenius bid down labor in rural areas with leverage. Cardiologists lose: private practice is dead (down to 10% of the field), and those still employed watch reimbursement fall while caseload per doctor climbs from demographic demand. Solo or small-group practices can't negotiate with insurers and can't afford the backend admin and EHR costs that consolidators absorb. Rural areas and small-population regions lose access—clinics close or consolidate, forcing patients to travel farther. Equipment makers lose if they can't scale or consolidate: tariffs eat into the 5–6% margins, and frozen Medicare rates mean clinics defer purchases.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that consolidation and reimbursement pressure combine to worsen access exactly when demand is climbing. If dialysis clinics close in rural areas because Medicare rates don't cover costs, kidney patients have nowhere to go. If cardiologist shortages keep widening—projected to hit 1 per 1,700 patients by 2035—patients wait months for appointments, and acute heart events go unmanaged. Tariffs on medical devices could compress margins further and force more mergers or bankruptcies among equipment makers, collapsing competition and raising prices for clinics. The deeper risk: Medicare keeps cutting reimbursement to control costs, but the demographic wave—more kidney disease, more heart failure, longer patient lifespan—keeps increasing volume. Consolidators can handle it by ruthlessly controlling costs and wages. Everyone else exits or gets absorbed. The machine runs, but at lower quality and less competition.

In one sentence

The chronic-disease machine in a nutshell

Chronic disease is now the engine of American healthcare, but reimbursement caps and consolidation concentrate profit in two dialysis giants and PE-backed cardiology groups while squeezing out small operators, rural access, and clinical workforce.
