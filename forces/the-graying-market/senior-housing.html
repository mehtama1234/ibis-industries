Senior housing at scale

Boomer demand fills beds—but short staff and wage spirals are eating the margins.

The baby boom is moving into the frail years, flooding the assisted living and home care markets with demand. Occupancy rates hit their highest levels in a decade. But the very businesses capturing this wave are being squeezed by a caregiver shortage so acute that staffing costs are rising faster than revenue. The result: consolidators and deep-pocket operators thrive, while independent small homes get stuck choosing between losing money or rationing care.

What changed

In 2026, the senior residential care market is $415 billion in size and growing 6-9% annually—accelerating as the oldest boomers age into their 80s starting in 2027. The industry franchises alone (non-medical in-home care) hit $110 billion in 2026, up from $5.967 billion in 2021, a projection of $9 billion for that year showing explosive branching. Over 220,000 people now work in elderly services, compared to 163,813 in 2021; the Bureau of Labor Statistics projects 711,700 new home care jobs between 2022 and 2032. Occupancy hit 88.7% nationally in Q3 2025, the 17th straight quarter of increases, meaning beds fill almost as fast as they open. The demographic engine is real and relentless.\n\nBut here is the bind: bed growth lags demand. Assisted living beds grow just 1-2% annually, while the over-80 population expands at 4%. That shortage pushes overflow demand into franchised home and community-based care, which sounds lucrative until you hit the staffing wall. Registered nurses, certified nursing assistants, and dietary staff report 40% annual turnover. Over half of home care agencies say they can't fill open positions. Wages for caregivers have climbed 31% since 2019 alone. Profit margins, which sat at 14.8% in 2021, are compressing as operators face a choice: pay the rising wage rates or lose staff and service quality. Even operators with the strongest market positions report that labor is now the binding constraint on growth.\n\nConsolidation is the response. In 2025, there were 871 M&A transactions in the senior care space—20.8% above the prior record of 721 in 2024. Assisted living made up 44% of those deals. On the current burn rate, 2026 is surpassing both prior years. Private equity sees the demographics as a tailwind and the fragmentation as an opportunity: the market includes 121,000 businesses in elderly and disabled services, most of them small operators with no economies of scale on purchasing, staffing, or financing. BrightSpring Health Services, backed by KKR, went public in January 2024, validating the thesis that institutional players can run home and community-based care at scale. The FTC and DOJ opened a formal investigation into PE acquisition surge in March 2024—still no enforcement, but the scrutiny signals a coming crack in the consolidation story.\n\nWhat the force made scarce is beds and caregivers both. Assisted living capacity is rationed by occupancy; home care workers are scarce and expensive. What became newly valuable is the ability to operate across multiple modalities (memory care, assisted living, home care, adult day programs) to smooth labor costs, negotiate with payers from scale, and absorb the M&A premiums consolidators pay. Private equity has the capital to roll up fragments and implement standardized staffing models—though those models often mean lower per-client touch, not higher quality. Independent operators and small chains, absent a buyer, face relentless margin compression and the risk of being undercut on price by a regional roll-up backed by institutional capital.

The evidence

  • Assisted living franchises grew from $5.967 billion revenue (2021) to a 2026 projection of $9 billion, with the workforce expanding from 163,813 (2021) to over 220,000 (2026).
  • Occupancy reached 88.7% in Q3 2025 after 17 straight quarterly gains, signaling severe bed scarcity and forced upselling into higher-cost home care modalities.
  • Home care agencies report over 50% ongoing worker shortages while caregiving wages climbed 31% since 2019, with operators squeezing margins or cutting service hours.
  • In 2025, 871 M&A deals (20.8% above prior peak) targeted senior care, with PE firms using institutional capital to roll up 121,000 fragmented operators into regional and national chains.
  • BrightSpring Health Services IPO (Jan 2024, KKR-backed) and FTC/DOJ scrutiny (Mar 2024) bracket the consolidation wave, validating scale economics while signaling regulatory risk.

Who rides it

Private equity and large regional operators win decisively. The wage spiral, occupancy crunch, and fragmentation reward capital, scale, and multi-modal operations. A PE-backed chain can absorb a 31% wage climb, negotiate payer rates from institutional leverage, and cross-subsidize margin-thin assisted living with higher-margin home care or memory care. Regional consolidators with 20-50 facilities can afford compliance staff, centralized purchasing, and trained front-line managers in ways a single 60-bed home cannot. The KKR thesis holds as long as reimbursement rates keep pace—and demographic tailwinds are strong enough that payers have limited leverage to push back.

Who's squeezed

Independent and small-chain senior housing operators get progressively squeezed. A 60-bed assisted living home with 70 staff members sees wage costs climb faster than occupancy revenue can absorb, especially if it competes with a PE-backed cluster that can move staff, negotiate insurance contracts, and subsidize losses with adjacent businesses. Operators without acquisition buyers face a choice: invest capital to modernize and staff competitively (a bet on their own operational improvement), merge quietly into a roll-up (often at a discount), or slowly lose both census and staff as residents transfer to better-funded homes. The 121,000 small operators are the residual—the unleveraged, the local-only, the under-resourced.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Reimbursement claws back faster than labor costs stabilize. Medicare and Medicaid (which fund 60%+ of assisted living and home care) are already cutting per diem rates and tightening home care hours in several states. If wage growth stays at 3-4% annually but payer rates flatten or decline, the margin story inverts—even consolidated operators hit a wall. PE valuations are being written assuming that revenue growth (occupancy and rate) outpaces cost growth (wages and benefit); if that flips, the 2025-2026 M&A wave looks like the peak before the squeeze. Small operators that relied on a buyer to bail them out will have sold at the wrong time.

In one sentence

Senior housing at scale in a nutshell

Boomer demand fills beds faster than operators can staff them, making scale and capital the only reliable path to profitability—and consolidation the only exit for everyone else.
