D · The tensions

Who pays for all this

Medicare Advantage is the card at the table, but the deck is rigged — premiums went vertical, but insurers are hemorrhaging money on the sicker population they said they'd manage.

Medicare Advantage owns the eldercare market now. These managed-care plans carved out $359.2 billion in 2026, pulling in 34 million seniors. But the deal that made sense five years ago — insurers take risk, trim fat, profit on efficiency — has inverted. The companies that bid to cover boomers are now pinned between rising demand they can't refuse and payment cuts they can't negotiate.

What changed

Medicare Advantage premiums surged because the math broke. Earned premiums rose 14.3% in 2025, yet insurers' actual claims climbed even faster at 15.5% — meaning they're losing ground on every policy, even after jacking up prices. Seventeen percent of MA contracts ran at a loss (above 100% medical loss ratio) in 2023, and another third live in the knife's edge between 90–95%, with no cushion for surprises. The surprise came: GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic exploded as demand from obese and diabetic seniors flooded the system, and these drugs cost 2–3 times what insurers budgeted. Tariffs from the Trump administration tacked on another 2–3.6% to medical supplies and device costs in 2025–2026. The pricing power insurers grabbed — hiking premiums aggressively — has a shelf life. CMS is now running aggressive audits for "upcoding," where insurers get paid more by coding patients as sicker than they are. For 2026 alone, the agency launched enhanced audits targeting exactly this fraud, which means the margin squeeze can only tighten.

The insurer response has been swift and brutal. UnitedHealthcare, Humana, Aetna, and CVS all announced market exits and benefit cuts for 2026 MA plans in 2025. Cigna sold off its entire Medicare business (MA, Part D, supplemental) to Health Care Service Corp. in March 2025. Smaller regional carriers like Health Alliance and FirstCarolinaCare stopped offering Medicaid and ACA coverage entirely on January 1, 2026, keeping only MA. CMS predicted MA enrollment would fall from 35 million in 2024 to 34 million in 2025, but the real story is worse: 2.9 million seniors were forced to switch plans because insurers exited their markets. The insurers making money on Medicare Advantage today are not growing; they're retreating to the ZIP codes and risk profiles where they can still turn a profit.

Outside Medicare Advantage, the whole insurance ecosystem is in repricing shock. The broad health insurance market ($1.6 trillion in 2026, up from $1.13 trillion in 2022) is finally recovering margins after they crashed in 2023–2024 — but this recovery is built on squeezing both ends. Employer-sponsored premiums jumped 9% for 2026, the highest in 15 years. ACA marketplace premiums jumped 18% for 2026, and benchmark plans jumped 26%, far exceeding what insurers proposed. The December 31, 2025 expiration of enhanced ACA premium tax credits means that subsidized enrollees will watch their annual out-of-pocket premiums double from $888 to $1,904 in 2026 — a shock that will push millions into uninsured status or Medicaid. Medicaid itself is shrinking for the first time since COVID: enrollment fell 7.6% in 2025, driven by new work and reporting requirements in the 2025 reconciliation bill, and is forecast to grow only 0.2% in 2026.

Dental insurance tells the same story of payment collapse from a different angle. The market hit $99.9 billion in 2026, growing only 2.62% annually through 2035. Plan maximums — the annual cap on what the insurer will pay — are stuck at $1,000–1,500, which is 1/17th of their 1975 value in real dollars. Patients increasingly find procedures cheaper out-of-pocket than through insurance, rotting the fundamental value exchange. Dentist network participation cratered to 32% in major PPO networks by 2024, meaning most patients now pay out-of-pocket rates anyway. Dental service organizations (DSOs) have consolidated 16.1% of the market by 2024 (up from 7.2% in 2015), shifting bargaining power away from insurers to practice owners. Regulators intervened hard: 37 state dental insurance reform laws passed in 2025, the largest single reform wave in the industry, mandating minimum loss ratios, human review of AI claim denials, and transparency on retroactive denials.

The evidence

  • Health Insurance (broad US market): $1.6 trillion in 2026, growing 3.9% CAGR 2021–2026, margins recovering to 8.30% in 2025–2026 after collapse in 2023–2024 via premium rate increases
  • Medicare Advantage: $359.2B in 2026, but 17% of contracts above 100% MLR and 33% in 90–95% range; incurred claims grew 15.5% in 2025 vs. earned premiums 14.3%, creating per-policy losses
  • ACA Marketplace: Benchmark plan premiums jumped 26% for 2026; unsubsidized premiums double from $888 to $1,904 annually on Jan 1, 2026 after enhanced tax credit expiration
  • Dental Insurance: $99.9B in 2026, enrollment down 2.3% in 2025; plan maximums ($1,000–1,500) worth 1/17 of 1975 value in real dollars; 32% dentist network participation in major PPO by 2024
  • Medicaid: Enrollment fell 7.6% in 2025 due to work/reporting requirements; forecast growth only 0.2% in 2026

Who rides it

Big insurers are consolidating around Medicare Advantage and wringing pricing power out of employer and ACA markets — but only if they can exit the unprofitable MA ZIP codes and patient cohorts. UnitedHealthcare, Humana, and Cigna are retreating strategically: they're keeping the high-margin MA segments, hiking premiums 9–26% across employer and ACA, and buying time with policy exits. Employers with enough scale are winning too — they're self-insuring or moving to defined-contribution plans to escape premium inflation. Tech entrants like Amazon (GLP-1 at $25/month through One Medical as of April 2026) and TrumpRx.gov (direct-to-consumer pharma bypassing insurance) are siphoning off the most profitable and easiest-to-manage segments: primary care, drug fulfillment, and chronic condition management. Regional DSOs in dentistry are consolidating 16.1% of practices (up from 7.2% in 2015) and shifting bargaining power away from insurers.

Who's squeezed

Seniors on MA plans that stay open will see benefit cuts — narrower networks, higher out-of-pocket costs, more prior authorization. The 2.9 million seniors forced off plans in 2025–2026 are being pushed into traditional Medicare or replacement MA plans in worse zip codes. Unsubsidized ACA enrollees face a cliff: their premiums double from $888 to $1,904 on January 1, 2026, a shock that will drive millions into uninsured status or into Medicaid (which itself is shrinking by 7.6% in 2025). Dentists are squeezed on both ends: insurers keep reimbursement flat while DSO consolidation pressures independent practices to sell. Providers of all types (hospitals, medical device suppliers, dental practices) face margin compression: they face insurers holding the line on payments while tariffs and labor costs rise 2–3.6% annually.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is demand inelasticity meeting hard reimbursement ceilings. Boomers entering their care years have chronic diseases (diabetes, heart disease, obesity) that require continuous, expensive treatment — they can't skip care. But Medicare Advantage is losing money on every patient over 90–95% MLR, and CMS audits for upcoding are tightening the screws further. Insurers can't raise premiums fast enough to cover claim growth (15.5% in 2025 vs. 14.3% premium growth), and they can't negotiate down claims because seniors demand care and providers are consolidating into DSOs and hospital networks with more pricing power. The death spiral risk is real: as insurers exit unprofitable markets and raise out-of-pocket costs, only the sickest and most-vulnerable seniors remain, pushing MLRs even higher and triggering more exits. The 2026 MA contraction is just the start.

In one sentence

Who pays for all this in a nutshell

Medicare Advantage is the de facto payment system for elderly America, but it's trapped between seniors who need constant care and a government that keeps cutting what it pays for managing that care.
