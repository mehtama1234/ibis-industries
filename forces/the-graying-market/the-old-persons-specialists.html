The Specialists of Old Age

Age-skewed outpatient specialties boom as boomers age into their skin-cancer and chronic-pain years, but Medicare cuts and consolidation narrow the field.

The demographic wave hits hardest in specialties tied to aging bodies: dermatologists screen for skin cancer, allergists manage lifelong rhinitis, chiropractors treat the aching spine. The US dermatology market alone sits at $8-9 billion in 2026, growing 7-8% annually, while the allergy market jumped from $24.74 billion (2025) to $26.56 billion (2026). But reimbursement cuts and workforce scarcity are reshaping who survives.

What changed

Dermatology is a masterclass in the squeeze. The industry pulls in $8-9 billion (2026) with 7-8% annual growth, yet 85% of the ~12,000 practicing dermatologists still work in solo or small-group practices—exactly when Medicare threatens a 9% reimbursement cut in 2027. Cosmetic dermatology is the escape hatch: the medical aesthetics market exploded from $19.55 billion (2025) to $22.59 billion (2026), a 12.4% jump, with Botox alone valued at $9.84 billion in 2025 and on track to reach $18.76 billion by 2035. Dermatologists are buying Botox licenses and lasers to shift from insurance-dependent skin cancer screening (10-15% margins) into self-pay cosmetic work (30-50% margins). Meanwhile, private equity has backed 35+ multistate platforms—Schweiger, U.S. Dermatology Partners, Forefront, Pinnacle—and closed 16 deals through October 2025, including Schweiger's landmark acquisition of California Skin Institute. Solo practitioners fetch 3-5x EBITDA, small groups 4-9x, but the real prize is scaled platforms at 12-15x; solos are getting picked off.

Allergists face a different poison: the market is growing 8.8-9.1% annually through 2035, but practice consolidation is hollowing out the specialty. In 2023, there were 3,417 allergists (10% growth from 2015) but only 1,585 practices—down 11% from 1,782 in 2015. Allergists per practice doubled from 7 to 11, meaning the field is merging into multispecialty networks at breakneck speed. Young graduates post-2010 show 65% adoption of multispecialty groups versus 42% for earlier cohorts, a signal that solo practice is vanishing. Medicare reimbursement fell 3.4% in 2024 and another 2.83% in 2025 (the conversion factor dropped to $32.35), crushing margins that were already tight at 14.4-14.5% EBIT. At-home allergy testing kits ($60-200) now undercut office diagnostics, and telehealth is growing 17.4% annually—patients can consult remotely without visiting. Physician burnout affects 41.9% of allergists (2025), with staffing shortages and admin burden driving the exodus. The paradox: allergy incidence has risen 100% per decade for rhinitis and 21% for pediatric peanut allergies since 2010, so demand is surging, but the specialty can't capture it profitably at the solo level.

Chiropractors sit in the strangest spot: a $24 billion market (2026) with only 2.6% annual growth, 38,000 individual practitioners, and 70,000+ establishments, yet 70% are still independent. Franchise chains like The Joint (950+ locations, July 2026) and venture-backed digital startups (ChiroHD raised $26 million in 2025) are eating their lunch. Insurance reimbursement rates shrink annually; cash patients are priced out by high deductibles and cost-of-living stress. Digital competitors offer AI-driven diagnostics, employer contracts, and remote monitoring—things traditional chiropractors can't match. Private equity is hunting platform targets at 2.8x-3.8x EBITDA, betting consolidation unlocks profitability. But the industry's real hope—expansion of Medicare coverage to include evaluation, imaging, and non-manipulation services under the Chiropractic Medicare Coverage Modernization Act—has stalled at 167 House cosponsors as of August 2026, leaving practitioners in limbo. Without that legislative win, consolidation is the only path.

All three specialties are age-ready: dermatology rides skin-cancer screening in aging skin, allergies climb as boomers manage chronic rhinitis and food sensitivities, and chiropractic thrives on back pain in the 65+ set. But the economics reward scale, not specialization. Dermatologists who build cosmetic aesthetics operations escape into high-margin self-pay; allergists who join multispecialty networks share overhead and cross-sell to dermatology/ENT patients; chiropractors who franchise or go private-equity-backed survive. The lonely solo practitioner in any of these fields is facing an impossible choice: sell now, merge with a network, or build a cash-pay moat before reimbursement kills the model entirely.

The evidence

  • Dermatology: 12,000 practitioners, 85% solo/small-group; 35+ PE platforms consolidating at 16 deals through October 2025, with cosmetic aesthetics jumping from $19.55B (2025) to $22.59B (2026) at 12.4% CAGR (Healio, 2025).
  • Allergy: Market grew 7.3% to $26.56B (2026) while practice count fell 11% (1,782→1,585); allergists per practice doubled (7→11), signaling consolidation into multispecialty networks dominated by post-2010 graduates (IBISWorld, 2025-2026).
  • Chiropractic: $24B market with 2.6% growth; 70% still independent solos, but The Joint (950 locations, July 2026) and private equity (ChiroHD raised $26M in 2025) are capturing market share via franchises and digital diagnostics (Entrepreneur, IBISWorld, August 2026).
  • Medicare cuts: Dermatology faces 9% reimbursement cut in 2027; allergists saw 3.4% (2024) and 2.83% (2025) cuts; chiropractors waiting on legislative expansion (Medicare Coverage Modernization Act stalled at 167 House cosponsors, August 2026).
  • Margin squeeze: Dermatology's self-pay cosmetic segment (30-50% margins) vs. insurance skin cancer work (10-15%); allergy EBIT stable at 14.4-14.5% but at-home testing and telehealth (17.4% annual growth) compete directly; chiropractors at 2.6% growth with rising admin costs compressing the 19.5-19.7% margin (Healio, IBISWorld, 2025-2026).

Who rides it

Dermatologists who own cosmetic aesthetics operations escape Medicare reimbursement cuts into high-margin self-pay work; Botox is on track to reach $18.76 billion by 2035. Multispecialty networks win the allergy game—consolidated practices can negotiate better insurance rates, share overhead, and cross-sell dermatology and ENT services to the same aging patient. Franchised chiropractors and private-equity-backed platforms (ChiroHD, The Joint) win by standardizing workflow, adding AI diagnostics (ChiroTouch's Rheo, August 2025), and capturing employer contracts—advantages solo practitioners cannot match. Large platforms command 12-15x EBITDA multiples, a 2-3x premium over solos.

Who's squeezed

Solo practitioners in all three specialties are squeezed: dermatologists in low-margin insurance screening; allergists managing overhead with shrinking Medicare reimbursement (2025 conversion factor down to $32.35); chiropractors losing cash patients to cost-of-living stress and digital competitors. Physicians in group practices who joined early get diluted by at-home testing kits and telehealth. Young allergists face burnout (41.9%, 2025) and administrative burden with no path to ownership. Chiropractors are held hostage by Medicare expansion legislation that stalled at 167 House cosponsors, freezing them out of imaging and diagnostic coverage. The 38,000 independent chiropractors collectively lose market share to 950 Joint locations; the 1,585 allergy practices shrinking from 1,782 tell the story of consolidation grinding down autonomy.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that Medicare cuts accelerate and hollow out these specialties before consolidation can restore margins. Dermatology could see primary-care AI screening (DermaSensor FDA approval, January 2024) slash referrals faster than cosmetic demand can compensate. Allergists face a death spiral: Medicare cuts → burnout → exodus → fewer practices → higher costs → more cuts. And chiropractors have no Plan B if the Medicare Coverage Modernization Act stays dead—they are trapped between franchise consolidation and legislative hostage-taking, with no path to profitability at scale on their own. The aging population generates real demand, but the reimbursement model is breaking faster than the market can adapt.

In one sentence

The specialists of old age in a nutshell

Age-driven demand floods into dermatology, allergies, and chiropractic, but Medicare cuts and consolidation pressure force practitioners to sell, merge, or escape into self-pay and franchise models.
