When the senses fade

Hearing and vision loss create steady aging-market demand, but reimbursement limits and tariff shocks are reshaping who captures the billions.

Thirty-seven and a half million Americans report hearing difficulty—one in three people aged 65 to 74 struggle to follow conversation. Vision losses are just as widespread: over half of adults over 75 need vision correction. These are not lifestyle upgrades. They are daily necessities. And the billions flowing into hearing aids, contact lenses, and eyeglasses reveal a market under stress: consolidation squeezing independents, tariffs doubling overnight, and big retailers capturing margin while manufacturers fight for scraps.

What changed

Hearing aid manufacturing—a $2.2 billion US industry in 2026—has fragmented into three distinct tiers as FDA deregulation since 2022 upended the entire market. Premium AI-driven devices from Sonova, Demant, and Starkey still command $2,400 to $3,000-plus and enjoy 3–5% annual growth among those who can afford them. The over-the-counter segment, now legal since October 2022, was supposed to democratize access with $800–$1,500 devices at retailers like Costco and Amazon. Instead, OTC has stalled at a niche 5–10% adoption rate—most people with mild hearing loss still delay treatment, and those who do buy often choose distribution over innovation. The real growth is happening at the margins: big-box retailers (Costco, Walmart, CVS) are now the fastest-growing channel by volume, but they demand ever-lower wholesale prices in return. Import penetration has settled at 48% of demand, but tariff threats and reshoring economics are making foreign suppliers less attractive; in July 2025, Nano Hearing Aids announced it was moving best-selling US models to domestic manufacturing to dodge tariffs. The industry's biggest structural shift arrived in 2026 when Amplifon (the world's largest hearing aid retailer) merged with GN ReSound (the third-largest global manufacturer) in a €2.3 billion deal—vertical integration that locks supply and retail together and raises the competitive bar for everyone else.

The eyewear market—$23.2 billion in retail, $7.7 billion in US manufacturing—is under a different kind of pressure. Contact lenses were growing at 7% annually through 2024, but decelerated to just 4% in 2025 as consumers pulled back on buying annual supplies, rebate programs contracted from 12-month to 6-month cycles, and e-commerce exploded from 35% to 40% of unit sales in a single quarter. Online retailers like Zenni and Warby Parker (which posted 15–18% growth through early 2025) undercut traditional brick-and-mortar prices by 20–70%, forcing optometry clinics to slash margins to match. Where is growth happening? Myopia management—soft multifocal lenses and orthokeratology (overnight corneal reshaping)—is now the bright spot, growing at 10.2% annually and reaching $2.8 billion by 2025, especially in affluent households and regions with insurance coverage. Specialty lenses (daily silicone hydrogel, toric) hold price power, but commodity frames and basic single-vision lenses are drowning in import pressure.

Then tariffs hit. In April 2025, duties on Chinese optical products jumped to 154.5%—contact lens tariffs alone—while plastic eyeglass frames from China faced combined tariffs of 64–155%. The Vision Council found that 50% of eye care providers expected to pass price increases directly to consumers. Tariff payments on Chinese optical imports alone are projected to nearly double from $160 million in 2024 to $333 million annually. Manufacturers scrambled: Bausch + Lomb, J&J, and others began near-shoring to Mexico and Southeast Asia, accepting 18% production cost increases to escape China penalties. Consolidation accelerated as cover—EssilorLuxottica acquired Optegra (70+ eye hospitals), Alcon scooped up Lensar and LumiThera, and J&J spent $1 billion-plus on manufacturing expansion in Jacksonville, Florida. By Q2 2025, the market had bifurcated: exam prices climbed 14% year-over-year, but volume of visits dropped 4.5%—people were spending more per visit but going to the doctor less often.

Blue-light-filtering glasses—a category that barely existed a decade ago—hit $2.9 billion in 2024 and is forecast to double to $5.8 billion by 2034 as screen time drives a new demand segment among younger users. But this growth sits atop a mature base where prescription inelasticity (you have to correct your vision to function) masks the real dynamics: aging baby boomers need hearing aids and stronger prescriptions, but they are price-sensitive and often delay treatment; affluent households can afford premium AI-driven devices and myopia control, while middle-income users are trapped between expensive retail prices and quality concerns about online OTC alternatives; and independent optometrists and small hearing aid dispensers are caught between margin compression from tariffs and online discounting on one side and vertically integrated mega-retailers and manufacturers on the other.

The evidence

  • Hearing aid manufacturing: US market $2.2B in 2026 with 3.6% YoY growth, but OTC adoption stalled at 5–10% since FDA deregulation (October 2022), while Amplifon-GN merger (€2.3B, end-2026) consolidates supply and retail.
  • Contact lenses: $4.42B US market in 2025, decelerating to 4% YoY growth from 7% in 2024; online retail now 40% of unit sales (up from 35% one quarter prior), exerting 20–70% price pressure on traditional optometry.
  • Eyewear manufacturing: $7.7B in 2025, growing only 2.4% YoY; plastic frames face 155% tariffs, Chinese optical materials 22–25% stacked duty, and manufacturers accepting 18% production cost increases to near-shore away from China.
  • Myopia control lenses: fastest-growth segment at 10.2% CAGR, reaching $2.8B in 2025; orthokeratology and soft multifocals gaining clinical adoption but cost-to-patient remains a barrier outside affluent regions with insurance reimbursement.
  • Tariff shock: April 2025 duties on Chinese optical products jumped to 154.5% (contact lenses), driving projected tariff payments from $160M (2024) to ~$333M annually; Vision Council survey found 50% of providers expected price pass-through to consumers.

Who rides it

Premium device makers (Sonova, Demant, Starkey, J&J Vision, Alcon) and consolidators (Amplifon-GN, EssilorLuxottica, Bausch + Lomb) who can absorb tariff costs and near-shoring expenses while offering specialty segments (AI-driven hearing aids, myopia control, therapeutic lenses) that hold pricing power. Big-box retailers (Costco, Walmart, CVS, Amazon) capture volume and margin by negotiating ruthlessly with manufacturers and offering convenience. E-commerce players like Warby Parker and Zenni win by exploiting the price gap between online and offline, though tariffs are beginning to compress their margins too.

Who's squeezed

Independent optometrists and small hearing aid dispensers are squeezed between margin compression from tariffs, online discounting, and pressure to match low prices while serving older, price-sensitive customers who delay treatment. Consumers in the middle—those with moderate hearing loss or vision correction needs who cannot afford premium AI-driven devices or myopia management and are wary of online OTC quality—face higher prices as tariffs ripple through and fewer low-cost options as consolidation continues.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The tariff shock of 2025 is structural, not temporary. Reshoring to Mexico and Southeast Asia adds 18% to production costs permanently; those costs cannot come out once tariffs are baked in. Manufacturers will pass them on or accept lower margins. Consumers will pay more and buy less—exam volume already dropped 4.5% in Q2 2025 even as prices climbed 14%. The real risk is that price-sensitive older adults delay hearing aid adoption or skip annual contact lens renewals, leaving potential demand unrealized and leaving the industry with overcapacity it built for demographics that never materialized at the prices it set.

In one sentence

When the senses fade in a nutshell

As boomers age and demand for hearing aids and eyewear soars, tariffs, consolidation, and online discounting are fracturing the market into tiers the poor and middle-income cannot afford, while manufacturers and retailers fight over shrinking margin.
