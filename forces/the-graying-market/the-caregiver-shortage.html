D · The tensions

The Caregiver Shortage

When 73 million boomers need care but nobody's home to give it, wages spike and profits crumble.

Demand for home care and residential senior services is exploding—the market hit $191.6 billion in 2026 and is growing 6.7% annually. But there's a hard wall: the people who actually do the work are vanishing. Ninety-five percent of home care agencies report moderate-to-severe staffing shortages, and caregiver turnover sits at 75% annually. The shortage is so acute that agencies are turning away up to 25% of patient requests. The math doesn't work.

What changed

The caregiver shortage isn't coming—it's already here, and it's reshaping the entire senior-care economy. Home care aides, certified nursing assistants, and registered nurses are the actual constraint. Home care wages have climbed from $13.07 per hour in 2014 to $16.77 in 2024, a 28% jump in a decade, yet turnover stays locked at 75% annually because the pay is still low compared to other healthcare roles and the work is physically grueling with no benefits. This wage spiral is being driven by sheer shortage: agencies can't fill positions fast enough, so they have to keep raising what they offer just to keep beds staffed. Meanwhile, the Census Bureau projects 711,700 new home care jobs will be created between 2022 and 2032. Where do they come from? They don't. Instead, agencies choose: lose money covering overtime, close beds and turn away patients, or merge with larger platforms that can absorb the costs.

The shortage is fragmenting the market but only for the small operators. Home care is the most fragmented healthcare sector in America, with 437,000+ establishments—mostly mom-and-pop agencies—and 75% annual turnover was already chewing up margins before wages started spiking. Now, 34% of home care agencies reported profit concerns in 2025-2026. Some are near zero or negative margins. The strong performers target 15-20% SDE margins, but they're the exception. Meanwhile, private equity owns 41% of M&A deal volume in 2026 and is aggressively rolling up the market: 55 home health and hospice deals in the first half of 2026 alone. Private equity can weather the labor cost squeeze better than independents because they can deploy technology (cloud-based EMRs, remote patient monitoring are now mainstream), hire corporate HR teams, and transfer workers across their portfolio. A single franchisee or small agency cannot. The consolidation is accelerating precisely because the shortage makes scale valuable.

The government is tightening the purse at the same time labor gets expensive. Medicare cut home health payments by 1.3% in 2026 (after proposing a 6.4% cut initially), and these cuts have run for four straight years. That's $220 million yanked out of the system in 2026 alone, even as caregiver wages demand upward pressure. This squeeze is already visible: rural and underserved areas are seeing agency closures because the reimbursement no longer covers the labor floor. Residential senior care franchises face the same pressure—occupancy is strong (88.7% in Q3 2025, a 17-quarter high), but it's also squeezed staffing: registered nurses, CNAs, and dining staff report 40% turnover, and over half of home care agencies still report ongoing shortages. Franchises have grown from $5.967 billion in 2021 to a projected $9.0 billion by 2026, but that top-line growth is noise if margins are collapsing. The industry is also shifting to value-based care models where CMS rebalances quality scorecards and pushes financial risk onto lower-performing agencies—another margin haircut for the fragmented base.

Assisted living beds are growing only 1-2% annually while the over-80 population grows at 4%. That mismatch is forcing demand into franchised home and community-based care, where operators should be enjoying wider margins. Instead, the staffing crisis is clamping them. Baby boomers enter the 80+ cohort starting in 2027, which will intensify all three pressures simultaneously: rising demand, falling supply of workers, and tighter reimbursement. The 2025 M&A wave—871 deals, 20.8% above 2024's record—shows private equity knows exactly what's coming. Assisted living deals made up 44% of those 871 transactions. But the FTC and DOJ opened a formal investigation into the PE acquisition surge in March 2024, signaling regulatory scrutiny of consolidation. That's the real risk: if regulators throttle roll-ups, the fragmented base won't have an escape route when labor costs go vertical.

The evidence

  • Home Care Providers: 95% report staffing shortages, 75% annual turnover, agencies turning away up to 25% of patient requests (2025-2026).
  • Residential Senior Care Franchises: Assisted living beds growing only 1-2% annually while over-80 population grows 4%, forcing demand into home care (2026).
  • Wages in home care have risen 28% since 2014 ($13.07 to $16.77/hr), yet turnover stays at 75% and 34% of agencies report profit concerns (2024-2026).
  • Private equity owns 41% of home health M&A deal volume in 2026; 55 deals in first half of 2026 alone, driven by consolidation pressure from labor squeeze.
  • Medicare home health payment cut 1.3% in 2026 after four straight years of reductions, forcing rural agency closures even as caregiver wages spike.

Who rides it

Private equity and large platform operators win. They can absorb wage inflation through scale, deploy technology (cloud EMRs, remote monitoring), transfer workers across their portfolio, and weather the margin squeeze that kills independents. The 41% of 2026 M&A volume that's PE-backed isn't accidental—consolidators see the shortage as a moat. Humana's $5.7 billion acquisition of Kindred Healthcare (2024), General Atlantic's $3 billion grab of TEAM Services Group (Q2 2026), and Kinderhook's $1.1 billion take-private of Enhabit (Q1 2026) show institutional capital betting that scale beats fragmentation in a labor shortage. Large franchises with multiple locations can also shift labor between branches and amortize HR costs across hundreds of facilities. For the franchisee segment (121,000 businesses), the demographic tailwind is real—73 million boomers entering retirement with guaranteed demand—but only the ones who can get bigger survive the labor cost floor.

Who's squeezed

Independent and small home care agencies get squeezed from both sides. Medicare is cutting reimbursement ($220 million cut in 2026 alone, after four years of cuts) while caregiver wages are rising 31% cumulatively since 2019. Rural and underserved area agencies are closing because the reimbursement floor no longer covers labor. Single-location franchisees and mom-and-pop operators face 40% CNA turnover and ongoing staffing shortages that force them to either lose money on overtime, turn away patients, or get acquired. The 34% of home care agencies reporting profit concerns in 2025-2026 are mostly small operators who lack the scale to absorb wage inflation or the cash to wait for consolidation deals. Patients in rural areas are the real losers—as agencies close and urban operators shed unprofitable branches, care deserts expand.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that labor shortages get worse before consolidation scales fast enough to fix them. Baby boomers enter the 80+ cohort starting in 2027, which will spike demand exactly when the workforce is still undersupplied. If the FTC and DOJ—which opened a formal investigation into PE acquisition surge in March 2024—begin enforcing restrictions on roll-ups, the fragmented base won't have an escape route. Private equity deals could slow, leaving thousands of small agencies caught between rising labor costs, frozen reimbursement, and the demographic wave. The result: more closures, more care deserts, and rationed care for seniors in underserved areas. The math simply doesn't close unless one of three things happens: wages stop rising (it won't, shortage is real), reimbursement goes up (Congress won't do it), or consolidation speeds up (regulators might block it).

In one sentence

The caregiver shortage in a nutshell

Home care demand is exploding as boomers age, but a 75%-turnover caregiver shortage and frozen Medicare rates are turning growth into a margin death spiral for anyone not big enough to survive it.
