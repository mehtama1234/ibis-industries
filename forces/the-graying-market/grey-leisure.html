C · The money side of aging

Grey Leisure & the Active Old

Boomers retire with money to spend on leisure, but retail contraction, price resistance, and structural shifts are reshaping where the cash actually goes.

Active retirees have cash and time. You'd think leisure—hiking gear, amusement parks, campgrounds—would boom. But the money is crashing into a broken supply chain and a customer base getting pickier about what they spend on. The winners are those who control scarce supply; everyone else is fighting over a flattening pie.

What changed

The outdoor retail industry that peaked during the pandemic is now in freefall. Hiking and outdoor equipment stores shrank from $7.1 billion in 2022 to $6.3 billion in 2026, a contraction of roughly 11 percent even as 183 million Americans still reported outdoor participation in 2025. REI, the category leader, has become a cautionary tale: it shut down its entire Experiences division (tours and classes) in January 2025 after those offerings served only 40,000 of its 23 million co-op members. The company laid off 180 full-time and 248 part-time employees in the same quarter, trimmed another 257 retail positions in October 2024, and posted losses exceeding $300 million in 2022 and 2023. Dick's Sporting Goods, which tried to compete head-to-head with premium formats, abandoned its Public Lands brand after it collapsed from eight locations to three. The culprit is simple: boomers are browsing Amazon, comparing prices online, and buying less frequently. Margins are razor-thin (3.5 to 4.3 percent) because inventory still reflects pandemic-era ordering mistakes, and both Amazon and Walmart are moving upmarket on price, squeezing independents out of the middle. Smaller shops continue to close. Consolidation accelerates.

Amusement parks tell a different story—one of nominal growth masking real trouble underneath. The industry hit $33.4 billion in 2026, growing at a 9.7 percent CAGR since 2022, but the growth is heavily skewed by blockbuster new assets (Universal's Epic Universe opened in May 2025 with projections to serve 5.2 million visitors its first year and climb to 9.2 million by 2026). Disney's domestic parks attracted 76.5 million visitors in 2024—essentially flat year-over-year—and the company raised peak-day ticket prices to $224 at Disneyland and over $200 at Magic Kingdom in October 2025. That same move was accompanied by deeper discounts than the parks have offered in years, a signal that consumers are price-conscious and the market is stratifying: families with cash still spend; everyone else is staying home. Labor costs consume 35 percent of operating expenses and wage inflation remains sticky at 2.9 percent annually. The Cedar Fair-Six Flags merger, completed in July 2024, created a combined operator controlling 27 regional parks with a $5.5 billion debt load and declining attendance (down 1.4 million in Q2 2025 versus the prior year). Tariffs on imported ride systems and equipment are beginning to bite into capex budgets. Climate risk is now visible too: Hurricanes Helene and Milton imposed a $120 million operational cost on Disney in October 2024, a reminder that centralized destination parks carry infrastructure fragility.

The campgrounds and RV parks industry reveals the most interesting dynamic. Revenue grew 5.2 percent in 2025, but total reservations actually fell 1.0 percent for the first time in platform history—a textbook signal of a market shifting from volume growth to pricing power. Average nightly rates for medium parks jumped 16.6 percent to $83 per night. The sector is being remade by two forces: supply scarcity and generational mix. Building a new campground requires 24 to 48 months to permit and construct, at a cost of $60,000 per site, which creates a structural moat for existing operators and deters new entrants. That moat protects pricing. But who's using it? Glamping—upscale, experiential camping—is the only accommodation type growing; it surged 43.6 percent in 2025 while conventional RV parks stagnated. The money is going to premium, younger campers willing to pay for the experience, not to the core demographic of retirees in Class A motorhomes. Supply is so tight that cap rates (8.0 percent) sit above loan costs (8.7 to 9.3 percent), meaning new acquisitions start with negative leverage—returns depend entirely on operational improvements and rate optimization, not asset appreciation. The 2025 Guadalupe River flood and Hurricane Helene exposed uninsured waterfront risk and prompted Texas to tighten safety codes. Operators are retrenching: Sun Communities divested its entire Safe Harbor marina portfolio in October 2025 to exit marine leisure entirely. The RV industry itself is electrifying piecemeal (Ruby's Inn added Tesla Superchargers in July 2025), but infrastructure buildout is uneven and the passenger fleet remains overwhelmingly combustion-powered.

Who rides it

The winners are monopoly and near-monopoly operators in constrained categories. Universal's new Epic Universe was built to pull traffic away from competitors, not to grow the pie. Disney, despite raising prices and facing resistance, still controls the largest installed base. In campgrounds and RV parks, the 88 percent of the market still independently owned is being picked off: institutional operators like Sun Communities and KOA are consolidating supply, locking in pricing power, and betting that supply scarcity will keep rates rising faster than inflation. Glamping operators—younger, experiential, premium—are growing their segment at 43.6 percent annually because they're selling a different product to a different demographic. Equipment makers selling directly to consumers (brands like REI Co-op or private-label Amazon outdoor gear) sidestep retail erosion entirely. The losers in outdoor retail are losing so badly that REI, the co-op gold standard, had to destroy its own Experiences division to survive.

Who's squeezed

Retail chains and independent outdoor shops are being squeezed into irrelevance. Dick's Sporting Goods tried to compete on quality and failed: its Public Lands brand went from eight stores to three. REI, the stronger player, is now a shell of its pandemic peak, shedding staff and shuttering profitable-adjacent services. The entire outdoor retail category is contracting—1,382 establishments in 2025, losing establishments and employees (20,834 total) every quarter. Regional amusement park operators under the Cedar Fair-Six Flags umbrella are trapped: $5.5 billion in debt, flat or declining attendance, and labor costs consuming 35 percent of operating expenses with no way to cut further. Consumers buying RV park visits are being pushed into a choice between premium (glamping, which is up 43.6 percent) or commodified (cheap, no-frills parks). The middle—mid-range RV parks, mom-and-pop outdoor shops, regional theme parks—is hollowing out.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that this isn't cyclical—it's structural. Retail outdoor equipment probably won't rebound because consumers have permanently shifted to online browsing and price comparison. Amusement parks are dependent on blockbuster new assets (Epic Universe, new Magic Kingdom lands) to grow attendance; Disney is already at the limit of price resistance (peak tickets at $224) while operating margins erode from labor and tariff costs. Campgrounds face a convergence of climate risk (Hurricanes Helene and Milton exposed uninsured waterfront exposure) and infrastructure brittleness (RV charging, water systems, road access are aging). The real worry is that active, wealthy retirees—the supposed beneficiaries of the grey leisure boom—are being bifurcated: the very wealthy still spend freely on premium experiences and high-end gear (Epic Universe, glamping, $224 Disney tickets), but the mass affluent middle is becoming price-sensitive and trading down. If economic slowdown hits retirement accounts, grey leisure could contract sharply across the board. REI's collapse from category darling to layoff machine happened in five years; the system proved fragile.

In one sentence

Grey leisure & the active old in a nutshell

The boom in grey leisure is real, but it's concentrating in constrained supply (premium parks, glamping, branded experiences) while retail and mid-market recreation contract under price pressure and structural change.
