C · The money side of aging

Insuring a longer life

Living longer turns into an insurance and annuity market—but carriers face a pricing bind that's reshaping who wins and who pays.

The US life and long-term care insurance industry is worth $1.1 trillion as of 2026, but it's caught in a squeeze. Boomers are living longer, which should mean endless demand. Instead, carriers are wrestling with policies they priced wrong twenty years ago, new rate hikes that scare customers away, and a public Medicaid system that undercuts them on price. The money is there—but so are the headwinds.

What changed

The long-term care insurance market became a victim of its own optimism in the 1990s and 2000s. Carriers like Genworth and Prudential issued aggressive policies at rates that drastically underestimated both how long people would live and how expensive cognitive decline claims would be. The median time from an Alzheimer's diagnosis to a nursing home is 3.3 years—far costlier than underwriters predicted. Now the bill is coming due. Genworth alone is carrying $31.2 billion in cumulative rate increase approvals across all its policies. A 55-year-old woman buying coverage in 2025 pays roughly $3,750 per year; a man the same age pays $2,200. When policies renew, customers face sticker shock: increases of 10–25% are common, and some older policyholders see jumps of 40% or more. The market is shrinking because of it—growth was only 2.4% annually from 2020 to 2025, and the market size fell from $31.6 billion in 2021 to $25.6 billion in 2025. State regulators are tightening the screws. Connecticut passed legislation in May 2026 requiring public hearings for any rate hike above 10%, forcing carriers to justify their numbers in front of elected officials. The Federal Long-Term Care Insurance Program—which covered federal employees—remains suspended as of April 2026 because even the government decided pricing had become unsustainable. That's a signal.\n\nAnnuities and guaranteed income products, by contrast, are on fire. US retail annuity sales hit a record $464 billion in 2025, up 7% from 2024, marking the fourth consecutive record year. Every quarter now exceeds $100 billion in sales. The driver is simple: four million Americans hit retirement age each year, and they're terrified of running out of money. Fixed-indexed annuities (which offer upside participation in stock indexes while protecting against downside) nearly doubled to $126 billion in 2024. The newer Registered Index-Linked Annuities (RILAs) exploded, surging 270% from $24 billion in 2020 to $65 billion in 2024. These products appeal to affluent retirees who lived through 2008 and 2022 and want guaranteed income, not volatility. But this growth is happening in a shrinking population of traditional insurance buyers. The individual life insurance market is stalled. Sales are flat in 2025 and projected to grow only 1% in 2026, according to LIMRA. The problem is generational: Gen Z and younger millennials believe term life insurance costs ten to twelve times more than it actually does. They also lack the traditional triggers—marriage, children, mortgages—that pushed prior generations to buy coverage. The life insurance industry's regulatory capital hit an unprecedented $536 billion in Q3 2025, well above pre-pandemic levels, which freed carriers to chase yield in riskier assets. Insurance companies now hold 31.4% of their portfolios in private credit, up sharply as they hunt for returns in a low-rate world. That's a bet on defaults staying low.\n\nWhat became scarce is affordable protection for people in their late fifties and early sixties who want to lock in long-term care coverage before health issues price them out. What became cheap, paradoxically, is guaranteed income—record annuity sales mean fierce competition among carriers and aggressive distribution through fintech platforms and fee-only advisors. What became risky is holding the old playbook: the carriers who issued policies on 1990s mortality assumptions are now forced to choose between bleeding money or hiking rates so aggressively that customers flee to Medicaid (which is basically free for the poor) or skip coverage entirely. What became newly valuable is disciplined underwriting and curated care networks. Genworth's October 2025 launch of CareScout—available in 40+ states with 500+ pre-negotiated providers by end 2024—is a bet that the future of long-term care insurance looks less like "pay any provider" and more like a managed care network where carriers can negotiate rates and outcomes.

The evidence

  • Long-term care insurance (Genworth, Prudential): Market size $25.6B in 2025, down from $31.6B in 2021, with growth stalling at 2.4% CAGR (2020-2025) due to affordability crises and rate hikes of 10-25% annually
  • Annuities (MetLife, Lincoln National, Principal, Voya): Record $464 billion in US retail annuity sales in 2025, up 7% annually, with RILAs surging 270% from $24B (2020) to $65B (2024) as four million annual retirees seek guaranteed income
  • Life insurance (603 enterprises, largest players MetLife, Prudential): Market size $1.1 trillion in 2026 with 0.6% growth in 2025 but -1.4% contraction in 2026; individual life sales flat in 2025, projected 1% growth in 2026
  • Insurance regulatory capital (all carriers): Hit $536 billion in Q3 2025, driving 31.4% private credit allocations by end 2024 as carriers chase yield amid low rates and mortality normalization
  • Federal Long-Term Care Insurance Program (FLTCIP): Remains suspended as of April 2026 for new applications due to cost volatility and diminished market—a signal that even government pricing models failed

Who rides it

The big annuity and life insurance carriers—MetLife, Prudential, Lincoln National—win on guaranteed income. Retirees desperate for stability in a volatile world are buying annuities at record volumes, generating steady revenue and higher margins than volatile equity-linked products. RILAs in particular offer carriers a middle ground: upside participation that keeps customers happy, downside protection that keeps claims controlled. Fintech platforms and fee-only advisors also win, becoming the preferred distribution channel for annuities because they reach affluent retirees directly and handle the paperwork. Genworth wins by shifting away from the legacy long-term care playbook and launching CareScout—a managed network model that lets them control costs and outcomes instead of bleeding money on unpredictable claims. Connecticut regulators win because the new transparency rules (public hearings for rate hikes above 10%) force carriers to justify their numbers and protect consumers from the next round of shock increases.

Who's squeezed

Customers in their late fifties and early sixties lose because long-term care insurance is becoming unaffordable right when they need to lock in coverage. A 55-year-old woman pays $3,750 per year and will see that rise 10-25% annually. Many simply skip the insurance and bet on Medicaid, which is basically free for the indigent. Carriers holding legacy long-term care books lose because they're trapped: they issued policies at rates that don't cover claims, so they must either raise rates (driving customers away) or absorb losses. Genworth and Prudential are already struggling with this choice—Genworth alone carries $31.2 billion in approved rate increases it still has to implement. Individual life insurance agents lose as their market stagnates; Gen Z and millennials aren't buying because they either can't afford it or think it costs ten times more than it does. Insurance companies' private credit bets lose if the credit cycle turns and defaults spike—they're holding 31.4% of portfolios in private credit, a high allocation in a slowing economy.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that the long-term care insurance market collapses under the weight of rate increases that policyholders simply won't pay. If Genworth and Prudential try to implement their $31.2 billion in approved rate hikes all at once, customers will flee to Medicaid or drop coverage entirely, which will shrink the market further and push the remaining carriers toward insolvency. The Federal Long-Term Care Insurance Program's April 2026 suspension is a warning: even government can't make the math work. If carriers can't raise rates fast enough to cover claims, they'll either exit the market or demand state bailouts. A second worry is mortality risk in the annuity market: if recession drives unemployment and mortality spikes in vulnerable populations, or if long-term longevity assumptions prove wrong in the opposite direction (people live even longer than expected), carriers holding $464 billion in annuity liabilities could face margin compression or worse. The private credit bet—31.4% of insurance portfolios—is also dangerous: if the credit cycle turns, defaults spike, and liquidity freezes, insurers could face capital calls and forced asset sales.

In one sentence

Insuring a longer life in a nutshell

Boomers demand longer-life insurance, but carriers priced wrong twenty years ago and now face a choice: raise rates to unsustainable levels and lose customers to Medicaid, or absorb losses and exit the market.
