A · The body wears out

Joint Repair & Surgery

Demand for knee and hip replacements floods in, but robotics become standard while Medicare cuts 26% of reimbursement in a decade.

Joints wear out. A 65-year-old body is not a 35-year-old body. The flood of aging Americans—10,000 turning 65 every single day—means orthopedic surgeries, eye surgeries, and the devices that go inside people's knees and hips are no longer niche. The market for orthopedic products hit $60.6 billion in 2025. But here's what changed: reimbursement cratered, robotics became table stakes, and consolidation crushed independents.

What changed

The orthopedic products business ($60.6 billion in 2025, growing only 4.3% annually through 2035) is being torn between explosive demand and a reimbursement wall. Ten years ago, a hip replacement or knee surgery was a specialty procedure. Now 34% of all inpatient procedures are people over 65, and Medicare's payment for hip arthroplasty has collapsed 39% since 2017. Not 2% cuts year after year—a 39% cliff. Meanwhile, the robot-assisted surgery piece exploded to $2.6 billion in 2026 and is growing at 16-17% annually, far outpacing the sluggish 4.3% of the traditional implant business. Stryker, which makes MAKO, now runs 67% of US knee replacements and 33% of hips on robotic platforms by end-2025. That's not a nice-to-have. That's the standard of care. Hospitals and surgery centers adopted the robot because it improves outcomes and reduces complications—but Stryker locked the market, and smaller implant makers have nowhere to hide. J&J spun off its DePuy Synthes orthopedic unit (valued north of $20 billion) because the core business of making knee implants can no longer justify the manufacturing footprint or R&D expense at acceptable margins.

Ambulatory surgery centers, which now handle many of these procedures outside hospitals, grew to $46.5 billion in 2026 and are consolidating violently. Ascension bought AmSurg for $3.9 billion in 2024 and went from 58 to 300+ centers overnight. Meanwhile, reimbursement grew only 2.4-2.9% annually—nowhere near wage inflation—so smaller independents got crushed. Large platforms like USPI hold 38.6% EBITDA margins by leveraging scale; smaller doctors' practices are down to the low 30s. CMS also threw a 2.5% efficiency adjustment at the industry in 2026 and added 560 new procedures to the ASC Covered Procedures List—cardiac ablations, lumbar fusion—but here's the catch: those complex procedures pay less at ASCs than at hospital outpatient departments. The reimbursement tables are deliberately tilted to keep complex, high-acuity work in hospitals. So centers have the permission to do the work but not the payment to make it attractive.

Eye surgery clinics ($2.0 billion in 2025, only 1.7% growth that year) are being squeezed from both ends. LASIK was the growth story for decades—elective surgery, cash pay, no insurance. Prices fell from $2,350 per eye in 2015 to $2,000-$5,000 today, which means younger, price-sensitive customers got priced in after waiting for deals, but the pool isn't growing. Cataract surgeries are covered by Medicare, so reimbursement is the limit. Meanwhile, there is a catastrophic shortage of ophthalmologists: 46% of eye practices report understaffing in 2025, and supply will fall 12% by 2035 even as demand grows 24%. That's a 36-point supply-demand gap. Hospital systems and private equity are acquiring regional clinics (Alcon bought STAAR Surgical for $1.5 billion in 2024; Zeiss acquired DORC) and bundling AI diagnostics to automate patient screening and reduce per-procedure costs. Independents can't match that. Roughly 40% of developers are now integrating AI—not as novelty but as a table-stakes move to compete. The result is a bifurcated market: large integrated platforms absorb the robot cost, the staff shortage, the reimbursement decline, and win through scale. Everyone else loses margin and volume.

The evidence

  • Orthopedic products: $60.6 billion market in 2025 with 26% Medicare reimbursement decline from 2016 to 2024, while robot-assisted surgery jumped to $2.6 billion in 2026 (16-17% CAGR).
  • Ambulatory surgery centers: $46.5 billion in 2026 growing only 2.4-2.9% annually, but large platforms like USPI maintaining 38.6% EBITDA margins while independents compress to low-30s.
  • Eye surgery: $2.0 billion market in 2025 with 46% of practices understaffed; ophthalmologist supply projected to fall 12% by 2035 despite 24% demand growth.
  • Robotic surgery dominance: Stryker's MAKO performs 67% of US knee replacements and 33% of hips by end-2025, making robotic assistance table stakes for competitive orthopedic practices.
  • Consolidation wave: J&J spun DePuy Synthes ($20 billion+) in October 2025; Ascension acquired AmSurg ($3.9 billion, 2024); Alcon bought STAAR Surgical ($1.5 billion, 2024).

Who rides it

Large hospital systems and PE-backed platforms win through scale and integration. They absorb the robot cost by spreading it across hundreds of cases, negotiate better Medicare rates by owning multiple sites, and deploy AI diagnostics to cut per-procedure costs. Stryker wins because MAKO is non-negotiable—surgeons switched because the robot genuinely works, then Stryker locked the market. Consolidated ambulatory surgery center networks like USPI and Ascension's expanded footprint can weather 2.4% reimbursement growth because they're not dependent on a single procedure or geography. They also have the capital to buy up stragglers at fire-sale prices. In eye surgery, hospital systems bundling cataract care with geriatric eye disease management and AI screening can underprice independents and defend market share through payer relationships.

Who's squeezed

Independent orthopedic surgeons and smaller implant makers get squeezed out. If you built your practice around a single implant line and can't afford Stryker's MAKO system or don't have capital to acquire one, you're losing surgeons to hospitals and surgery centers that have the robot. Small orthopedic device companies selling specialized knee or hip implants face a market where consolidation (J&J spinning DePuy Synthes, Globus buying Nevro for $250M in April 2025) is the only exit; organic growth at 4.3% doesn't justify standalone operations anymore. In ambulatory surgery centers, the 67% of independents are bleeding margin because reimbursement grows at 2.4-2.9% while their wage and supply costs rise faster. They're being acquired at multiples that reflect their distress. Independent eye surgery clinics can't match bundled platforms on cost, can't afford AI integration, and face a 12% ophthalmologist shortage—they lose volume and talent to hospital systems that own multiple practices, have recruitment power, and can cross-refer complex cases.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that Medicare reimbursement for orthopedic services collapses further and accelerates the erasure of independents before any new business model fills the gap. We've seen 39% cuts to hip arthroplasty reimbursement in a decade. CMS just applied a 2.5% efficiency adjustment in 2026 and is advancing site-neutral payment rules that could obliterate the ASC economics that depend on procedure price differentials. If Medicare goes after orthopedic services the way it went after cardiology a decade ago—capitated bundles, site-neutral pay, low utilization caps—the volume upside from an aging population evaporates into pricing pressure. Large platforms can survive on margin compression because they have scale and can cross-subsidize; independents cannot. The second worry is the robot lock: Stryker's 67% market share in knee replacement means surgeons who defect to MAKO have a hard time going back. If Stryker raises prices or restricts supply (as large players do when they dominate), the entire orthopedic supply chain is hostage. And the ophthalmologist shortage is existential—12% decline by 2035 against 24% demand growth means tens of millions of aging Americans cannot get routine cataract surgery, let alone advanced retinal work. No amount of AI screening or ASC efficiency gains that labor shortage away.
