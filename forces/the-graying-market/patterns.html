The synthesis · what repeats across all 12

The patterns

The boomers hit their care years — demand floods into everything age-related, but reimbursement cuts and a caregiver shortage cap the upside.

The aging wave is making orthopedic surgery, senior housing, home care, and long-term care insurance scarce resources in the most literal sense: there aren't enough beds, enough workers, or enough willingness from payers to fund care at prices operators need. Medicare cuts have been surgical and relentless. From 2016 to 2024, Medicare reimbursement for orthopedic procedures fell 26%, with hip arthroplasty pay down 39% since 2017; in January 2026, CMS tightened further with a 2.5% efficiency adjustment. That is happening in an industry where orthopedic product manufacturing brought in $60.6 billion in 2025 and is supposed to grow at steady 4.3% annually through 2035, but real margin growth is strangled by pricing pressure of 1-2% per year. The only segment escaping this trap is robotic-assisted surgery: MAKO knee replacements exploded to $2.6 billion in 2026 and are growing at 16-17% annually—far outpacing the rest of the market—because hospitals can charge premium fees and Stryker now dominates with 67% of US knee procedures and 33% of hip procedures running on their platform by late 2025.\n\nThe senior care franchises face the opposite problem: demand is roaring, but they cannot find bodies to fill the beds. The residential senior care market hit $415 billion total in 2026, with non-medical home care franchises alone accounting for $110 billion, up from $5.967 billion in 2021. Growth is accelerating toward high-single-digit rates as boomers enter their 80s starting 2027, but wages are eating profits. Home care worker turnover runs at 40% and half of agencies report ongoing staff shortages. Since 2019, wages in the sector have climbed 31% cumulatively to pull bodies into the work, forcing operators into a brutal choice: lose margin or ration service. In 2025 alone, the industry saw 871 M&A transactions—20.8% above the prior record—with private equity firms betting that demographic tailwinds justify any acquisition price. Assisted living made up 44% of those deals. National occupancy hit 88.7% by Q3 2025, the 17th consecutive quarterly increase, showing demand is real and staying strong.\n\nLong-term care insurance carriers are caught in their own squeeze: they underpriced policies in the 1990s and 2000s, assuming claim frequency and longevity that proved wildly optimistic. Genworth now faces $31.2 billion in cumulative rate increases it has already secured from state regulators, and yet new customers still balk at premiums. A 55-year-old woman in 2025 pays roughly $3,750 per year for individual coverage—double what a man pays at $2,200—and faces rate hikes of 10-25% on renewal. Carriers are trying to escape the legacy trap by rewriting underwriting from scratch. Genworth launched CareScout in 2025 with a curated network of 500-plus providers by 2024, aiming for sustainable pricing on new business. Connecticut's 2026 legislation requiring public hearings for any rate hike above 10% signals that regulators are tightening oversight. The Federal Long-Term Care Insurance Program remains suspended as of April 2026 because even the government could not price it sustainably. This market brought in $25.6 billion in 2025 but is growing just 2.4% to 4.0% annually—far slower than the underlying aging population—because price-sensitive customers are either going without insurance or relying on Medicaid, which now has partnership programs in 40 states.\n\nWeight loss services tell the story in miniature. The industry pulled in $2.6 billion in 2026 but is shrinking at 2.4% annually from its 2022 peak of $3.8 billion because GLP-1 medications have displaced diet programs almost overnight. From 5.8% of adults using GLP-1s in February 2024, adoption soared to 18% by 2026, directly cannibalizing demand for traditional coaching. Weight Watchers filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in May 2025 after 60 years in business. The future belongs to vertically integrated telehealth platforms like Hims and Ro that can secure steady GLP-1 supply and deliver it via apps and clinicians, not to coaching boutiques. The FDA crackdown on compounded GLP-1s in February 2026 accelerated this shift by favoring well-capitalized players with access to brand-name medications.

The through-line

The graying market is not a shortage of demand—it is a shortage of margin, labor, and political will to pay for what aging actually costs.

Who wins across the board

The winners are consolidators with scale and deep pockets to absorb labor cost inflation and reimbursement cuts. In orthopedics, Stryker locked in dominance with its MAKO platform—67% of knees, 33% of hips—and can command premium pricing. In senior care, private equity firms like KKR (backing BrightSpring's 2024 IPO) are rolling up fragmented home and community-based care operators into unified staffing and procurement engines that can weather wage inflation. In telehealth weight loss, platforms like Hims and Ro with their own supply chains and digital infrastructure are buying up legacy assets and funneling everything through GLP-1 delivery. Vertically integrated, capital-heavy, late-consolidation businesses with pricing power over payers win. Regional and single-location operators lose.

Who's squeezed across the board

The squeezed middle includes independent orthopedic device companies (J&J is spinning off DePuy Synthes as a $20B+ business because it is too small to survive alone), regional home care franchises unable to absorb 31% wage growth since 2019, and legacy long-term care insurers (Genworth and Prudential) burning through premium dollars on claims they underpriced decades ago. Independent weight loss clinics and diet programs that failed to integrate GLP-1 prescribing are being forced to shutter. The patients themselves get squeezed too: a 55-year-old woman buying long-term care insurance pays double what a man pays, faces double-digit renewals, and still may not be covered for cognitive decline. Regional Medicare Advantage plans face margin compression from CMS cuts. State Medicaid programs face spiraling costs as boomers enter, forcing them to slow eligibility or tighten benefit caps.

The biggest tension

The one to watch

The single biggest risk is that reimbursement cuts and labor cost inflation spiral into a feedback loop that erodes care quality. Medicare is cutting orthopedic reimbursement by 2.5% efficiency adjustments on top of existing 39% hip arthroplasty cuts since 2017. At the same time, nursing assistants and home care aides face burnout and exit the profession because wages are not keeping pace with the cost of living despite 31% cumulative raises since 2019. When staffing is already 40% turnover and half of agencies report shortages, further margin compression could force operators to hire even less qualified staff or reduce hours per patient. The risk is not that the aging wave goes away—it doesn't—but that we end up rationing care to people who can afford it while pushing low-income seniors into understaffed, under-trained networks, creating a two-tier system. Long-term care insurance already prices out most middle-class workers. Medicaid is the fallback but is moving toward managed-care capitation that rewards plans for denying coverage. If that tightens much further, seniors and their adult children will face genuine gaps in coverage.
