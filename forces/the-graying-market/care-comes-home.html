Care comes home

Home care is booming as seniors flee hospitals, but razor-thin margins and a caregiver shortage have agencies turning away one in four patients.

The money now strongly favors keeping seniors out of hospitals and nursing homes. Medicare, Medicaid, and insurers all discovered that a home care aide costs less than a facility bed. Home care exploded from $109.6 billion in 2021 to $191.6 billion in 2026 — a 75% jump in five years. But the agencies running this boom are drowning in a simple problem: not enough workers, not enough pay, and payment cuts that get deeper every year.

What changed

The government made home care suddenly *necessary* by cutting what they pay for facilities and long-term institutional care. Medicare slashed reimbursement to hospitals and nursing homes so aggressively that shifting seniors to home-based services became the only way to make the math work. Home care is now growing at 6.7% annually (2021-2026), and in 2026 alone the market grew 3.9% while reaching $191.6 billion — that's real demand, not hype. The shift made home care *cheap* relative to the alternative, and suddenly valuable to every insurance company and government program trying to manage costs. It also made caregiver labor desperately *scarce*. Over 2.4 million home health aides work in the US in 2026, and that number is projected to grow 17% by 2034 — but the problem is turnover. Annual caregiver turnover sits at 75%, meaning half the workforce quit and needs replacing every 18 months. Wages climbed from $13.07 per hour in 2014 to $16.77 in 2024, but that's still low compared to other healthcare work, and it's not enough to fix the shortage. Ninety-five percent of agencies report moderate-to-severe staffing shortages as of 2026, and they're now turning away up to 25% of patient requests. That's capacity they can't fill and money they can't capture. Meanwhile, payment cuts keep coming: Medicare slashed home health reimbursement for four straight years, with 2026 seeing a 1.3% aggregate reduction worth $220 million — after CMS originally proposed a 6.4% cut in November 2025. These cuts are *risky* to smaller agencies, forcing closures in rural and underserved areas where margins were already near zero. Thirty-four percent of agencies reported profit concerns in 2025-2026, and those agencies are competing for the same scarce caregivers with bigger, better-capitalized platforms. The profit opportunity is still there — the best agencies target 15-20% SDE margins — but you have to scale to reach them, which means you probably need private equity backing or a tech platform that lets you manage caregivers and patients more efficiently. Private equity figured this out fast. Fifty-five home health and hospice deals closed in the first half of 2026 alone, with 41% backed by PE firms. General Atlantic paid ~$3 billion for TEAM Services Group in Q2 2026 — the largest home care deal on record. Kinderhook took Enhabit private for $1.1 billion in Q1 2026. Humana snapped up the remaining stake in Kindred Healthcare for $5.7 billion in 2024 to fully own the home care giant. The fragmented industry — 437,000+ independent agencies — is consolidating fast, with PE-backed platforms building scale, technology, and caregiver networks that smaller operators can't match. That consolidation is making the industry slightly *new* — value-based care models are shifting financial risk to lower-performing agencies through CMS rebalancing of quality scorecards, and cloud-based EMRs and remote patient monitoring are becoming mainstream. But integration is incomplete, and these technologies add administrative burden to agencies that are already stretched thin.

The evidence

  • Home care providers hit 2.4 million workers in 2026 but face 75% annual turnover despite caregiver wages rising from $13.07/hr (2014) to $16.77/hr (2024).
  • Medicare cut home health reimbursement 1.3% in 2026, the fourth year of cuts, forcing closure waves in rural areas where 34% of agencies reported profit concerns (2025-2026).
  • General Atlantic's $3 billion acquisition of TEAM Services Group (Q2 2026) was the largest home care deal on record as PE consolidates the fragmented 437,000-agency market.
  • 95% of home care agencies report moderate-to-severe staffing shortages in 2026, turning away up to 25% of patient requests as demand outpaces workforce growth.
  • The home care market jumped from $109.6 billion (2021) to $191.6 billion (2026) at 6.7% CAGR, but the best agencies only target 15-20% SDE margins while 34% struggle with profitability (2025-2026).

Who rides it

Private equity platforms and large integrated health systems that can scale. The best home care players now have technology platforms (cloud EMRs, remote monitoring), national caregiver networks, and enough volume to absorb margin cuts and ride out workforce volatility. General Atlantic, Kinderhook, and Humana are buying scale and consolidation power. Smaller PE-backed platforms that own home care assets directly benefit from rising demand and can squeeze out independent agencies. Technology vendors selling EMRs, patient monitoring, and caregiver scheduling software also win — agencies are forced to adopt these tools to manage stretched teams and improve reimbursement through value-based care scorecards.

Who's squeezed

Independent home care agencies, especially in rural and underserved areas where margins are already thin and caregivers are even harder to find. Closure waves are already underway. Smaller agencies can't compete for scarce caregiver talent — they can't match wages or offer career paths like bigger platforms do. Caregivers themselves lose too, despite wage growth: $16.77 per hour (2024) is still low for healthcare work, and 75% annual turnover means burnout and instability for the workforce. Patients in rural areas lose access as agencies shutter and agencies in other regions turn away 25% of requests.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The caregiver shortage is the single biggest risk to the whole model. If agencies can't hire fast enough, they can't take on more patients, which means demand stays unmet and the upside gets capped. Home care is growing 6.7% annually, but home health aide jobs are only projected to grow 17% by 2034 — that's real growth, but not enough to match demand if baby boomers age faster or migrate to underserved regions. Wages would have to spike much higher to attract workers from other fields, which would crush margins further and force more closures. Alternatively, agencies shift to lower-acuity, easier-to-staff services, which means higher-need seniors (the most expensive and complex patients) get pushed back into hospitals — defeating the whole purpose of the home care boom.

In one sentence

Care comes home in a nutshell

Home care is booming as payers shift seniors out of hospitals, but a caregiver shortage and four years of Medicare cuts mean agencies can only capture the upside if they scale fast or face closure.
