C · Even diagnosis & advice

Medical Diagnostics

AI reads scans and labs faster and cheaper than humans; volume rises, per-read price falls, and the physician's gate remains the only moat.

AI is flooding medical diagnostics with speed and accuracy. Radiologists can screen ten times as many scans per day. Labs can process results faster and cheaper. The machinery works—but the business is getting squeezed from both ends. Imaging reimbursements have fallen two-thirds since 2003. Lab margins are stable only for the giants. And smaller players are getting pinned between machines and consolidation.

What changed

AI made routine reading cheap and fast. A machine can flag anomalies in a chest X-ray or spot cancer in a microscope slide in seconds. That automation is real: it works. But automation didn't make diagnostics more valuable—it made them more abundant. Hospitals and health systems now run their own labs instead of sending samples out. Clinics buy point-of-care testing machines. Consumers order tests online and read results at home. The gate where radiologists and pathologists sat—reading rare, valuable images for high fees—has become a funnel. More volume, lower price per read, and the human is still liable for the miss. Imaging centers face 2% growth in a 2026 market of $26.3 billion. Labs are flat at 0.9% growth from 2021–2026 in a $90.9 billion market. Both are consolidating to survive. Both are buying AI to keep up.

The evidence

  • Diagnostic imaging centers: Radiology Partners raised $720 million in fresh equity in February 2026 to reduce debt after financial distress; GE HealthCare acquired Intelerad for $2.3 billion in November 2025 to bolt on AI and cloud platforms.
  • Diagnostic labs: Quest Diagnostics raised 2026 revenue guidance to $11.95–12.05 billion and acquired Fresenius's Spectra Laboratories clinical testing assets in August 2025 to consolidate volume.
  • Dermatology: FDA approved DermaSensor, an AI skin-cancer diagnostic device for primary-care settings in January 2024. Thirty-five-plus private equity platforms are active in dermatology consolidation as of 2025, and dermatologists see 3–5% reimbursement gains 18 months post-acquisition.
  • Cardiology: Medicare slashed amyloid PET imaging by 57% and LAA closure by 27% work RVUs in the 2026 fee schedule. Six cardiology practice acquisitions were recorded in H1 2025 alone, matching the entire 2024 volume—consolidation is accelerating as cardiologist shortages worsen.

Who rides it

The big diagnostic networks and hospital systems win. Quest and LabCorp survive through scale, acquisition, and direct integration into hospitals. Imaging chains like Radiology Partners and Envision Healthcare weather the cuts by buying AI and bundling services. PE-backed dermatology platforms thrive by consolidating fragmented solos and cosmetic-aesthetics clinics; a 1–4 location group trades at 4–9x EBITDA, and the math still works for PE because AI lets them standardize workflows and staffing across acquired practices. Hospitals win outright: they now own 41.5% of outpatient imaging claims and can build in-house labs, testing, and diagnostic dashboards at lower per-unit cost. The physicians who survive are those inside large groups or hospitals with negotiating power and AI on staff. Patients win on speed and access—results come back faster, home testing is cheap, and AI is genuinely good at spotting things humans miss.

Who's squeezed

Independent radiologists and pathologists are squeezed. Radiology practices with fewer than 10 providers can't afford AI platforms ($1–5 million in software, infrastructure, and training). Freestanding diagnostic imaging centers lose market share to hospitals and are forced to consolidate or sell. Small labs can't survive Medicare's flat rates and the coming 15% annual cuts starting 2027; they're being acquired or shut down. Dermatologists in solo and small-group practices face 9% Medicare cuts in 2027 and can't compete on cosmetics alone (where the margins are 30–50% versus 10–15% for medical dermatology). They're being rolled up by PE platforms at 3–5x multiples for control. Cardiologists outside large groups or hospitals are in crisis: burnout is driving 547 early retirements annually, the cardiologist-to-patient ratio will worsen from 1:1,087 (2025) to 1:1,700 (2035), and Medicare cuts on stress tests ($91 per procedure in 2026) and imaging make small practices unviable. Radiologist residency spots went unfilled—the next generation is opting out.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Automation of routine diagnosis breaks the economics of small practices before it ever creates new value. The moat protecting physicians—legal liability and the need for human judgment on edge cases—stays intact. But the volume game is already lost to machines and consolidation. In five years, most independent diagnostic practices will be absorbed or closed. The real risk is that AI creates an illusion of cheaper diagnostics while making physicians rarer and more overworked inside large systems, and then one AI miss in a critical case creates a liability cascade that reshapes the whole ecosystem. The other risk: consolidation into a handful of national lab and imaging networks gives them pricing power over hospitals and patients, while the physician shortage means those networks can understaff and push harder on AI, cutting corners on quality for a volume play that no one can sue because the liability stays with the hospital, not the machine.

In one sentence

Medical diagnostics in a nutshell

AI made diagnosis cheap and fast; now the value flows to whoever owns the scale and the data—hospitals, networks, and private equity—while independent physicians and small practices face consolidation or extinction.
