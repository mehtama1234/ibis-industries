Law & consulting

AI does the draft; the pyramid cracks.

Law firms and consulting shops built their money machine on a simple stack: juniors do the routine work at low cost, partners handle the judgment, and the firm keeps the spread. AI is automating the routine. Juniors don't disappear—but the math that justifies hiring a college graduate to write his hundredth contract memo is broken. The firms that win are the ones that capture AI's gains before their clients demand the savings.

What changed

For decades, a law firm's or consulting shop's profit came from leverage. A senior partner charged $500 an hour; a junior associate cost $30 an hour but did 80 percent of the work—drafting contracts, running comps, writing memos. The firm pocketed the spread. AI upended the ratio. A language model now drafts a contract in 10 minutes for near-zero cost. A consultant no longer has to spend three weeks gathering industry data and formatting a PowerPoint deck; an AI agent does it in a day. The work isn't gone—it's cheaper and faster. That's a disaster for the model that priced junior labor at a markup.

The law industry felt this first. Firms jacked up their rates—fees per lawyer were up 16.8% in 2025—and landed record profits. But the bedrock numbers were weak: demand grew only 1.9 percent while expenses jumped 9.1 percent. They're spending big on AI infrastructure (budgets up 10% in 2025) and still seeing no client savings. Fifty-nine percent of corporate clients say they see zero benefit. Firms are merging frantically (59 deals in 2025, at least 16 more announced for 2026) because the math requires scale now. Consulting hit the same wall harder and faster. By 2025, AI roles outnumbered entry-level consultants for the first time in the industry's modern history. Hiring of junior consultants crashed 40 percent from 2023 peaks. The federal government cut $5 billion in consulting spend in early 2025. Clients stopped accepting hourly billing and started demanding outcome-based pricing or contingency fees. Consulting margins are under siege: AI specialists earn 56 percent wage premiums, but junior consultant salaries have flatlined.

The evidence

  • Law: 59 law firm mergers in 2025 (up 18% from 2024) and at least 16 more deals announced for 2026, driven by the mismatch between demand growth (1.9%) and expense growth (9.1%) after firms spent 10% more on AI infrastructure with no measurable client ROI.
  • Consulting: Consultant hiring down 40% from 2023 peaks by 2025; federal consulting cuts of $5B in early 2025 forced layoffs at Deloitte, Accenture, and IBM as clients rejected hourly billing and demanded outcome-based pricing.
  • HR consulting: Market growth decelerated from 6.5% (2024) to 1.4% (2026) as software platforms (Rippling, Paychex, TriNet) eroded the advisory moat and AI spending jumped 50% YoY in 2025.
  • Expert networks: AlphaSense's $930M acquisition of Tegus (June 2024) triggered consolidation as AI synthesis from transcript libraries replaces pay-per-call consulting, with research project turnaround collapsing from 2-5 days to 2 hours.

Who rides it

Big firms with capital win. Scale lets you absorb AI infrastructure costs, hire enough specialists to use it well, and keep existing clients even if margins compress. The Hogan Lovells–Cadwalader merger (2026) creates a $3.6 billion firm that can negotiate AI-powered efficiency gains directly into client contracts. Accenture doubled its AI specialist headcount to 80,000 and grew AI bookings 120 percent in 2025. Firms and consultancies that own data—especially transcript libraries and past project work—win harder. AlphaSense's consolidation play works because they own Tegus's 100,000-call library; they can resell the same insights to dozens of clients at near-zero marginal cost. Niche specialists who do judgment work—a trial lawyer, a complex M&A advisor, a human-centered change management consultant—stay valuable. Partners and senior people who own client relationships keep their place. What collapses is the middle: the junior associate who was a cheap source of routine drafting, and the mid-market consultant who sold "analysis and PowerPoints."

Who's squeezed

Junior associates and entry-level consultants get squeezed hardest. Consultant hiring fell 40 percent from 2023 peaks. A law firm no longer needs five juniors to draft contracts if one AI system does it at 1 percent of the cost. Salaries for junior consultants have flatlined while AI specialists command 56 percent premiums—the firm won't pay to develop your judgment if the machine already does your current job. Mid-market law and consulting firms—the ones too small to afford top-tier AI infrastructure and too specialized to win on pure scale—get trapped. They can't compete on price; they can't compete on judgment. Clients who've been paying for leverage-arbitrage lose their discount. A corporate legal team that used to outsource everything to BigLaw because it was cheaper than hiring in-house now sees AI reduce the advisory moat and questions whether they need BigLaw at all. Firms burning cash on AI without seeing client savings (59 percent of corporate clients report no clear benefit) are running out of runway. The margins are gone and the revenue is flat.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that firms invest heavily in AI and still can't pass savings to clients fast enough to matter. Right now, law firms hiked rates 16.8 percent in 2025 while clients see zero benefit from AI. If clients keep rejecting those prices, firms face a cascade: falling demand, rising costs per unit of work, and no way to cut head count fast enough to match. The stack of mergers already announced (16+ in 2026 alone) signals firms are betting consolidation will help—but there's no guarantee it will. A $3.6 billion merged firm still has to solve the problem that AI broke the junior-leverage model; you can't make that math work just by getting bigger. The second worry: clients defect. A corporation that sees "we hired AI and cut our consulting bill in half" succeeds; they internalize the work. Law firms and consultancies have no switching cost once clients realize the routine judgment has become commoditized. The firms that survive are betting they can move upmarket to pure judgment work fast enough. But the supply of "pure judgment" clients is smaller and more price-sensitive than the supply of "routine advisory" clients was.

In one sentence

Law & consulting in a nutshell

AI automated the junior associate's job, broke the leverage model that financed law and consulting, and now only the biggest firms with scale and specialist judges survive.
