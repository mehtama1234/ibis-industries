D · The new scarcities

The deploy dividend

The wins go to whoever sits between the AI and the enterprise—not the ones being disrupted.

AI is automating the thinking work. But someone has to deploy it, train it, feed it data, and defend it from the rest. That's where the money is moving. The IT consultants charging $300–$500 an hour for AI strategy, the data centers building hyperscale farms, the vendors locking in contracts—they're capturing the lion's share of the rewiring, while the corporations that actually need AI are caught between hype and reality, and the junior knowledge workers are getting squeezed out.

What changed

AI pushed four things to become newly scarce or valuable: the power to deploy the systems, access to clean data, the skills to operate the infrastructure, and the judgment to know when an AI's answer is right. Capital poured in—the five largest hyperscalers will spend $630 billion on infrastructure in 2026, up 36% from 2025. But electric capacity became the hard constraint, not money. US data center electricity demand tripled from 23 GW in 2023 to 42 GW in 2026. Wholesale power prices near major data centers rose 267%. That scarcity rewires who wins.\n\nFor IT consulting firms, AI became a pricing lever. Narrow, high-touch services—AI strategy, cloud migration, cybersecurity—command $300–$500 per hour in 2026. But the firms discovered they could also shed junior staff faster than they could sell AI work. Accenture booked $5.9 billion in gen-AI work in FY2025; IBM landed $12.5 billion in gen-AI business. McKinsey cut 10% of its global workforce (3,000–4,000 people) in 2026 as AI productivity squeezed internal capacity needs. Clients hired consultants to *avoid* hiring consultants—a paradox that compressed margins and accelerated M&A. The industry grew to $821.2 billion in 2026, but at 2.9% CAGR since 2021—slower than cloud alone ten years before.

The evidence

  • IT Consulting (Technology & Digital): Accenture's gen-AI backlog hit $5.9 billion in FY2025; IBM's gen-AI business reached $12.5 billion in the same period, yet overall consulting market growth slowed to 2.9% CAGR 2021–2026, with margins compressed and 52,000+ tech jobs cut in Q1 2026 alone (up 40% from Q1 2025).
  • Data Processing & Hosting (Technology & Digital): Hyperscalers will spend $630 billion on infrastructure in 2026 (up 36% from 2025), with $450 billion explicitly targeting AI; wholesale power prices near major data centers rose 267% in 2025–2026, and 90% of operators report staffing constraints as the critical bottleneck.
  • Enterprise AI Adoption: McKinsey's 2025 survey found only 39% of organizations claim any EBIT impact from AI, with most reporting impacts below 5%, despite record consulting backlogs and the tripling of US data center electricity demand from 23 GW (2023) to 42 GW (2026).
  • Infrastructure M&A (2025–2026): CoreWeave secured a $200 MW lease in Charlotte from Digital Realty (March 2026); Q1 2026 data center M&A topped $21+ billion in a single quarter; Equinix closed a $15 billion joint venture to build hyperscale facilities.

Who rides it

The IT consulting firms and infrastructure vendors win—at least for now. Accenture and IBM have locked clients into multibillion-dollar AI backlogs. Specialized hosting platforms like CoreWeave are booking massive capacity commitments. Data center operators who own power-rich land or long-term electricity contracts are becoming bottleneck choke points. M&A accelerated; consolidation is concentrating the picks-and-shovels business. The rule: if you *deploy* AI or *sell* the infrastructure layer underneath, you capture value. The consultant charges $300–$500 per hour. The hyperscaler owns the margin on GPU capacity. The power supplier holds the scarcity card.

Who's squeezed

Junior and mid-level consultants, software engineers, and routine knowledge workers get squeezed hardest. Hourly rates for junior and mid-level staff ($90–$150) face automation pressure from AI-coding tools, while consulting firms cut headcount faster than they redeploy it. McKinsey cut 10% of its global workforce; tech sector shed 52,000 jobs in Q1 2026 alone. Mid-market enterprises also get pressured—they can't afford the $300–$500/hour AI strategy consultants, yet they're told they must adopt AI or fall behind. Smaller data center operators and regional hosting vendors are being consolidated or priced out by hyperscalers' buying power and access to capital and power. The real loser is the company that *buys* the AI solution, hoping for ROI—because 61% of enterprises report no meaningful EBIT impact (inverse of the 39% claiming any impact).

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Power becomes the hard limit. Hyperscalers can spend $630 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026, but the US grid can't sustain 42 GW of data center demand. Wholesale power prices spiked 267% near major data centers, and capacity prices in PJM jumped 833% in a single year. If power constraints tighten further—or if electricity becomes politically contested—the entire AI deployment engine stalls. The second risk: the consulting bubble pops when clients realize 61% of AI projects deliver no measurable EBIT impact, and they stop paying $5.9B–$12.5B backlogs. Capgemini, IBM, Accenture, and others could face a sudden reckoning if enterprise confidence in AI ROI collapses. The third risk: the talent shortage never resolves. 90% of data center operators report staffing bottlenecks; demand for AI infrastructure specialists, high-voltage engineers, and cooling technicians vastly outpaces supply. That wage pressure spirals and further compresses infrastructure margins.

In one sentence

The deploy dividend in a nutshell

The deploy dividend flows to consultants, hyperscalers, and infrastructure vendors—while the enterprises buying AI solutions and the junior workers getting automated out find themselves squeezed between hype and a power-constrained grid.
