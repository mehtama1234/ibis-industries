Education & credentials: the degree's new job

<em>AI tutors are free and good. What a college degree actually sells now is not what it used to.</em>

AI changed what you need a class for. In 2026, ChatGPT and specialized tutors can teach you calculus, coding, or compliance frameworks for free or nearly free. The colleges and certification schools that thrived by selling "here's what you need to know" are now competing on something they forgot they were selling: who you know, what doors open, and—for employers—that you passed a gate they trust.

What changed

AI made the transmission of routine knowledge cheap and on-demand. Before 2024, you needed a professor for calculus, a bootcamp for Python, a compliance course for Azure. Those gatekeepers had pricing power because knowledge transfer was expensive to scale. Now a large language model teaches you better, faster, and asks fewer embarrassing questions. The cost of producing and distributing that content approached zero overnight.

This hollowed out the middle: commodity bootcamps that promised "learn coding in 12 weeks" face free competition from AI tutorials, corporate LLMs, and open MOOCs. Entry-level IT training bootcamps shut down in waves (Epicodus 2023, SNHU 2024, 2U entire bootcamp division 2024). Generic certification prep collapsed. But the top end moved the other direction. Cybersecurity training surged because it's locked to hiring requirements and insurance discounts. Enterprise cloud certifications command premium pricing because they're not about what you know—they're about proving to your employer that you passed someone's gate. That proof, that signaling, became more valuable as the underlying knowledge became free.

The evidence

  • Colleges & Universities: US sector reached $605.1B (2026), contracting 7.5% CAGR through pandemic recovery, now stabilizing but losing international graduate students (-12%, fall 2025-2026)
  • Business Certification & IT Schools: $6.4B market (2026), bifurcating sharply—cybersecurity training at 16.6% CAGR to $6.66B (2025), cloud bootcamps 12.7% CAGR, while commodity bootcamps shut down en masse
  • Consolidation: Elon acquired Queens (2026), Russell Sage merged Albany, Ursuline joined Gannon, Villanova absorbed Cabrini and Rosemont—wave of forced small-school mergers
  • Platform consolidation: Coursera acquired Udemy (April 2026, $2.5B equity value) to control 290M learners and 95,000 instructors, consolidating online-learning oligopoly as free alternatives displace entry-level training

Who rides it

Employers and the credentialing gatekeepers. Employers win because they can now outsource technical training to free AI and hire on credentialed proof. Cybersecurity and cloud certifications command premium pricing because they're not teaching knowledge—they're pre-screening: you paid someone to test you so I don't have to. Big platforms (Coursera, Udemy, now unified) win because they control the gates: 290 million learners and 95,000 instructors funnel through one checkout. Elite universities survive because their networks never went digital and their research keeps them a brand. The accreditors win because the Trump administration is weakening regulatory barriers and streamlining approval, letting more credential players enter the market.

Who's squeezed

Small regional colleges, bootcamp operators, and anyone selling knowledge transfer as their core product. Community colleges in rural areas are growing enrollment but losing revenue per student. Mid-tier private colleges can't compete with free tutorials and consolidated platforms, so they're consolidating or closing (Siena Heights closed after 2025-26 AY, Union Institute filed Chapter 7 bankruptcy). Bootcamps that built business models on "we teach you this rare skill fast" can't underprice free and are exiting or pivoting. The junior developer and entry-level IT hire loses negotiating power when the barrier to entry evaporated—now employers want pre-screened, credentialed juniors, and the credential is expensive, making that first job harder to reach even if the knowledge is free.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The accreditation market is starting to look like a toll booth. As AI makes content cheap, the degree's residual value collapses to "I passed their test" and "I met their people." If accreditors can set arbitrary gates and employers trust them uncritically, colleges and bootcamps become subcontractors, not institutions. The Trump administration's push to weaken accreditor oversight and streamline approval could flood the market with new credential players, each claiming to test you, all collecting fees, none actually teaching anything new. Meanwhile, AI itself is getting embedded into hiring systems—so the credential might start mattering less than raw performance data. The real risk is that education becomes a pure compliance layer: mandatory testing, zero learning, all margin capture.

In one sentence

Education & credentials in a nutshell

AI made knowledge free, so colleges now sell networks and gates instead of education—and the gatekeepers are consolidating into oligopolies while small schools shut down.
