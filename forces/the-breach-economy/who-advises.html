Who Sells the Compliance

Consultants and IT services firms monetize the rising security and compliance burden as cybersecurity skills become a premium, scarce resource.

Every company now needs someone to keep them secure and compliant. They hired consultants. The IT consulting industry hit $821.2 billion in 2026 and captured that demand—but they're caught in a painful squeeze between scarcity of talent, margin collapse from AI automation, and clients increasingly doing the work in-house instead.

What changed

Cybersecurity and compliance work created a new tier of expensive consulting. In 2026, consultants commanded $300-$500 per hour for AI, cybersecurity, and cloud work—triple the rate for junior and mid-level roles, which fell to $90-$150 and faced compression from AI-assisted coding tools. This pricing power created a vortex: firms like Accenture booked $5.9 billion in generative-AI work in FY2025, and IBM claimed $12.5 billion in gen-AI business, but delivery remained immature. Clients wanted solutions but were skeptical—only 39 percent of organizations in McKinsey's 2025 survey claimed any meaningful business impact from AI consulting, and most saw returns below 5 percent. The abundance of hype masked a scarcity of talent. Tech firms cut 52,000 jobs in Q1 2026 alone—up 40 percent from a year earlier—citing AI productivity gains. Simultaneously, 87 percent of tech leaders reported struggling to find skilled workers. The contradiction was brutal: firms needed fewer junior staff but desperately wanted more specialists. Displaced workers from Big Tech (IBM laid off 15,000+ since September 2024; McKinsey cut 10 percent of its global workforce, an estimated 3,000 to 4,000 jobs, in 2026) were snapped up within weeks by mid-market consulting firms hungry for talent. The consolidation wave accelerated to prove it: 21 IT consulting deals closed in Q1 2026 alone, the strongest quarter in recent history. Capgemini paid $3.3 billion for WNS Holdings in July 2025 to bulk up on AI, data, and process services. IBM closed its $11 billion acquisition of Confluent in December 2025, the largest IT services-adjacent deal, and simultaneously bought Neudesic, Cognitus, Hakkoda, and Txture in the same quarter—all to grab data, cloud, and AI talent. The result was a bifurcated market: large firms with strong brand moats and existing compliance relationships kept pricing power and kept margins thin through sheer volume, while smaller shops could command premium rates on niche expertise but faced constant client pushback on cost and persistent uncertainty about whether the consulting would actually work.

The evidence

  • IT consulting: $821.2B US market in 2026 with 2.9% CAGR since 2021, driven by cloud migration and compliance demands (2026)
  • Cybersecurity consulting commands $300-$500+ per hour vs. $90-$150 for junior roles facing AI automation (2026)
  • Tech job losses hit 52,000+ in Q1 2026 alone (up 40% YoY), yet 87% of tech leaders struggle to find skilled workers—contradiction forcing mid-market firms to snap up displaced talent (2026)
  • Major consolidation wave: Capgemini-WNS ($3.3B, July 2025) and IBM-Confluent ($11B, Dec 2025) targeting AI, data, and cloud competencies (2025-2026)
  • Only 39% of organizations claim business impact from AI consulting, with most below 5% ROI, yet consultants booked record backlogs (Accenture $5.9B gen-AI, IBM $12.5B gen-AI) (2025)

Who rides it

Big consulting firms with existing client relationships and enough scale to absorb the talent wars—Accenture, IBM, Capgemini, Deloitte—win because they can promise delivery and have the firepower to acquire missing capabilities. Mid-market and specialized firms win on niche expertise: boutiques with deep cybersecurity, compliance, or cloud chops can command premium rates because they solve specific, urgent problems that generalists can't. Displaced workers from Big Tech win too, absorbed within weeks by firms desperate for skilled hands. The consolidation itself signals that acquisition-driven growth is the survival strategy.

Who's squeezed

Junior and mid-level consultants lose as AI automation compresses their hourly rates and firms cut staff even as they claim to need talent. The talent paradox deepens the squeeze on them. Large enterprises building internal consulting teams also lose because they now face a binary choice: pay premium rates to external advisors or hire scarce talent and train them from scratch. Clients face the worst squeeze: consulting bills rise as premium skills get scarcer, but most consulting doesn't deliver measurable returns (only 39% see any EBIT impact), yet they can't ignore cybersecurity and compliance demands.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that large consulting firms will consolidate so aggressively that client choice collapses and pricing power becomes purely extractive rather than value-based. If three or four mega-firms control most of the compliance and security consulting market, they can raise rates without regard to actual ROI, knowing clients have nowhere else to go. The second risk is that the mismatch between hype and delivery—only 39% of organizations see business impact from AI consulting—triggers a correction where clients cut budgets precisely when compliance and security demands are rising. This creates an opening for pure-play security and compliance specialists, but the consolidation wave suggests the big firms are already buying them to prevent that outcome.

In one sentence

Who sells the compliance in a nutshell

Cybersecurity and compliance work created a premium consulting tier, but the contradiction between job cuts and talent scarcity forced aggressive consolidation and left only the largest firms and the most specialized shops with pricing power.
