The Security Software Layer

Biometric and identity detection systems became structural infrastructure, not a luxury feature.

For decades, biometric technology sat on the edge of business operations—nice to have, but not critical. Then two things happened at once: criminals figured out how to forge faces using AI, and the federal government started writing checks. By 2026, it's now table stakes to detect whether a fingerprint or face on your screen is real or synthetically generated.

What changed

The US biometrics scan software market hit $12.3 billion in 2026, growing at an 8.95% annual clip—but that number is deceptive. Only five years earlier, industry forecasters predicted it would barely reach $9.8 billion. The jump from forecast to reality tells the real story: demand exploded faster than anyone expected. What changed was the nature of the threat. Between January and August 2025 alone, one financial institution blocked 8,065 biometric injection-attack attempts using AI-generated deepfakes. In the first quarter of 2025, Sumsub documented a 1,100% surge in deepfake fraud and a 311% rise in fake identity documents—the kind of numbers that make executives stop arguing about ROI and start signing checks. This forced a complete rethinking of how identity verification works. Banks and fintech companies could no longer use a single biometric signal—a fingerprint reader or a facial scan—and trust it was real. Instead, they began consolidating vendors, buying bundled platforms that layer liveness detection (software that confirms the face or fingerprint is actually alive, not a photograph or mask), document verification (confirming that a driver's license isn't forged), and behavioral fraud signals (checking if someone types, moves, or clicks the way they normally do) all in one system. Meanwhile, the federal government was rewriting the playbook for border and immigration operations. The Secure America Act, signed in June 2026, allocated $69.5 billion to the Department of Homeland Security through fiscal 2029, with $673 million specifically earmarked for biometric entry-exit systems and $5.2 billion for real-time facial and fingerprint checks in ICE operations. By September 2026, DHS was required to deploy biometric exit capability at every major land port of entry or lose funding. That's not a suggestion—it's a regulatory trigger. This flooding of federal capital hit an industry composed of 377 companies, but dominated by a handful of global players: NEC, IDEMIA, Thales, HID Global, Entrust, Fujitsu, IBM, and Cisco Duo. The entry of a giant market signal—federal mandate plus fraud epidemic—started consolidating the vendors and the solutions. No single company could build the entire stack, so the winners became platforms that could integrate liveness detection, document verification, and behavioral signals into a unified stack that worked across mobile, web, and physical entry points. On the consumer side, the shift became invisible but total. By 2026, 80% of smartphones shipped with fingerprint technology, and facial recognition, though only 28% of mobile biometric deployments, was growing at 27.1% annually. What had been a luxury feature on premium phones became standard issue.

The evidence

  • Financial services: One institution blocked 8,065 biometric injection-attack attempts in 2025, forcing consolidation to integrated liveness-detection platforms
  • Border and immigration: The Secure America Act (June 2026) allocated $5.2 billion for ICE biometric checks and required DHS biometric exit deployment at all major land ports by September 2026
  • Mobile handsets: 80% of smartphones by 2026 shipped with fingerprint technology; facial recognition growing 27.1% annually despite being only 28% of current deployments
  • Document verification: Sumsub documented 1,100% surge in deepfake fraud and 311% rise in synthetic identity documents in Q1 2025
  • Biometric technology: 377 US companies operate in the scan software space, dominated by NEC, IDEMIA, Thales, HID Global, Entrust, Fujitsu, IBM, and Cisco Duo

Who rides it

The platform players that bundled liveness detection, document verification, and behavioral analytics won big. Banks and fintechs suddenly needed integrated solutions, not point products, because standing up a fingerprint reader or facial camera alone was too risky. Consolidators like IDEMIA, NEC, and Thales could command premium pricing because they offered coverage across the entire attack surface. Federal spending also created a protected revenue stream—government contracts for border biometrics don't shrink when economic growth slows. Consumer-facing identity verification players that could speak both to banks and fintech also benefited from the shift toward mobile-first authentication, since 80% of phones now shipped with biometric hardware. The deepfake fraud epidemic made these companies indispensable.

Who's squeezed

Standalone point-solution vendors got squeezed out. If you sold only facial recognition or only liveness detection, you became a module inside someone else's platform rather than the decision-maker. Small regional identity verification startups that hadn't yet built out the full stack found themselves either acquired for a discount or shut out of deals. Banks and fintechs absorbed the rising cost of integrated platforms through higher fees—ID verification that used to cost a dollar or two per transaction started costing more as vendors bundled in better fraud detection and liveness checks. That cost trickles down: fintech customers pay more in friction, businesses processing identity verification face higher operational expenses, and consumers see new identity-verification holds when opening accounts or transferring money. The regulatory mandate also created a compliance floor: every company operating in financial services or handling sensitive ID data had to implement these systems, turning what was once a competitive advantage into a table-stakes cost of doing business.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that biometric systems are only as good as their training data—and training data for facial recognition and liveness detection is notorious for bias and false positives. A facial recognition system trained primarily on light-skinned faces will fail more often on darker-skinned faces. A liveness detection system trained in one climate may struggle in another. When the entire border security infrastructure or financial system depends on these systems working without error, a systematic bias or adversarial attack (like the deepfake injection attacks already surging in 2025) could lock legitimate people out of critical services or let dangerous people through. The deepfake attacks documented in early 2025 show that adversaries are already winning a few rounds—8,065 injection attacks against a single institution in less than a year means the arms race is real and accelerating. If the sophistication of synthetic faces and forged documents keeps growing faster than liveness-detection technology improves, the entire integrated-platform model starts to fail.
