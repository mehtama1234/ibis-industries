Insuring the breach

Ransomware and identity fraud are flooding the cyber insurance market with unpredictable claims, forcing a harder underwriting line and bifurcating the risk pool.

Every business now expects to be breached. That expectation spawned a $7 billion cyber liability insurance market in the US alone that's already breaking at the seams. Ransomware claims exploded in 2025, identity theft is getting weirder and more expensive, and the insurers who once priced risk by formula are now gatekeeping who gets covered at all.

What changed

Cyber liability insurance used to be a soft market—cheap premiums, broad coverage, happy underwriters. That era ended hard in 2025. Ransomware, which was always a risk, became the risk: it drove more than 90 percent of all cyber insurance claim value in the first half of 2025 alone. Businesses that got hit faced ransom demands that jumped 47 percent year-over-year, though to insurers' relief, 86 percent of targets refused to pay. But the claims that did land were massive. Technology companies averaged $875,000 per ransomware incident, finance and insurance hit $731,000, and healthcare $675,000. Third-party liability claims—lawsuits from customers and partners blaming your breach for their losses—surged 70 percent in a single year, the biggest jump the industry tracked. That's not a trend; that's a phase shift. By mid-2025, Beazley (one of the big players) posted a 48.5 percent loss ratio, and the sector as a whole saw the first half of 2025 rack up more claim severity than most full years prior.

The market is physically fragmenting. For the first time in years, the combined ratio tightened to 65.4 percent in 2025—which sounds good on paper (breakeven is 100 percent), but the tightness came entirely from underwriters culling risk, not from premiums rising. Two years of rate cuts have ended; S&P Global now forecasts 15 to 20 percent premium increases for 2026. But here's the brutal part: those increases won't be uniform. Businesses with strong cybersecurity posture will get cheap rates. Laggards will be denied outright or shunted to the surplus market at triple premiums. The gap between a protected firm and a vulnerable one is widening fast. California's new CCPA cybersecurity audit mandate (effective January 2026) is already driving demand for cyber insurance among tech, retail, and financial firms, but it's also raising the floor for underwriting—you either have clean audits or you don't get a desk at the table. Insurers like AIG are now running 700-plus cybersecurity variables through AI platforms just to decide whether to offer a quote. This is not boutique risk management anymore; it's a hard gate.

Identity theft insurance is seeing a different crisis: synthetic identity fraud. Real identity theft—someone steals your Social Security number and opens a credit card in your name—is common and expensive. But synthetic identity fraud, where criminals fabricate entire personas from fragments of stolen data, is now 25 percent of all fraudulent claims and jumped eightfold globally in 2025. Life insurers faced $3.3 billion in synthetic ID exposure through 2024 and saw instances explode in the following year. The claims data looks like traditional fraud (it's fraud), but the forensics are alien—there's no victim in the classic sense, just a fiction that made money before it vanished. That forces underwriters to rethink loss reserves, claims assessment, and who even bears the cost. Simultaneously, the competitive field is sorting itself by technology. Aura's dark-web monitoring catches 260 distinct signals; competitors like Norton LifeLock track 60. Aura alerts triggered in 3 minutes in Q1 2026 tests; LifeLock's came 9.2 hours later. Biometric liveness detection with AI deepfake defense is the 2026 standard—you can't sell identity protection without it anymore. Credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) are bundling monitoring and insurance to lock in wallet share, and consolidators like Kingswood Capital are scooping up smaller players like IDX to stitch together breach response and insurance into one package. The industry is consolidating toward full-stack protection vendors, not bare-bones insurers.

Regulatory tightening is the third pressure. California's CCPA audit mandate took effect in January 2026, and the FTC updated identity theft rules in August 2026. The FTC's 2024 data already showed $12.5 billion in fraud losses—up 25 percent year-over-year—with identity theft now 18 percent of all Consumer Sentinel complaints. That metric alone tells you the margin for error is vanishing. Underwriters used to assume a certain baseline of fraud in the pool and price accordingly. Now they're pricing for volatility and regulatory penalty. A single class-action lawsuit over a major breach can cost millions; cyber insurers are pricing that in. Identity insurers face tighter claim standards and have to justify every denial to regulators. The whole market is shifting from loose-and-broad to tight-and-selective.

The evidence

  • Cyber Liability Insurance: US market $7.02B in 2024 (19.3% CAGR through 2026), North America $9.56B in 2025; ransomware drove 90%+ of claim severity in H1 2025.
  • Finance and Insurance: Average ransomware claims of $731,000 in 2025; Beazley's loss ratio at 48.5% (H1 2025) signals rising claim severity crushing profitability margins.
  • Technology: Highest average ransomware severity at $875,000 per incident in 2025; AIG deployed AI underwriting screening 700+ cybersecurity variables per policy in late 2025.
  • Healthcare: Ransomware claims average $675,000; third-party liability surged 70% YoY, forcing hospitals and health systems to face lawsuits from patients over breach damages.
  • Identity Theft Insurance: $6.8B market in 2024 growing 8–15% CAGR through 2035; synthetic identity fraud (25% of claims, 8x growth in 2025) forces rethinking of loss reserves and claim assessment.

Who rides it

Insurers with AI-powered underwriting platforms and data science teams are winning. AIG, Chubb, and Beazley are using algorithmic screening to cherry-pick the lowest-risk clients and price aggressively against their peers. Credit bureaus—Equifax, Experian, TransUnion—control 50–60% of monitoring services and are locking in customers by bundling insurance, breach response, and identity protection into integrated packages they can cross-sell endlessly. Consolidators like Kingswood Capital (which acquired IDX in November 2024) and tech vendors like Aura (now leading on dark-web monitoring speed and depth) are capturing the identity-protection niche by offering full-stack forensics, monitoring, and insurance under one roof. Well-secured enterprises with clean audit trails and strong cybersecurity postures are getting cheaper rates in a bifurcated market; they represent the profitable tail of underwriting.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market and lower-tier businesses without sophisticated security programs are getting squeezed. Insurers are tightening underwriting, denying policies outright to laggards, or forcing them into the surplus market at triple premiums. Small retailers, healthcare practices, and local governments face a choice: invest millions in security infrastructure to qualify for standard-market rates, or pay punitive premiums or go uninsured. Traditional identity-theft insurers like Norton LifeLock, whose dark-web monitoring (60 pieces) lags competitors like Aura (260 pieces) and whose alert times lag by orders of magnitude (9.2 hours vs. 3 minutes), are losing wallet share to full-stack consolidators. Businesses facing ransomware, third-party liability claims, and identity fraud are bearing more of the cost themselves as insurers narrow coverage and raise deductibles.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that claims keep accelerating faster than premiums can catch up, triggering a hard market or a cascade of insolvencies. Ransomware claims reached record highs in H1 2025, third-party liability surged 70 percent in a single year, and synthetic identity fraud is exploding unpredictably. S&P Global predicts 15–20 percent premium increases for 2026, but if claims losses surge 40 percent year-over-year (as they did in 2025), even 20 percent premium hikes won't cover the gap. That sets up either a sudden market hardening (renewals denied, rates spike, small businesses go dark) or a reckoning where one or more carriers blows through loss reserves and fails. Regulatory tightening—California's CCPA audits, the FTC's updated identity rules—is also raising compliance costs and litigation exposure, compounding the margin compression that underwriters are already facing.

In one sentence

Insuring the breach in a nutshell

Ransomware and synthetic identity fraud are making cyber risk harder to price and easier to deny, bifurcating the market into the protected-and-cheap and the vulnerable-and-priced-out.
