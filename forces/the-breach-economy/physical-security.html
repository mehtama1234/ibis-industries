Guarding the Physical Door

Physical and digital security converge into one spend as breach economics make every entry point a regulatory requirement.

Every door—to an office, a data center, a warehouse—is now a breach risk. The cost of guarding physical access has merged with the cost of guarding data access. Manufacturers and building operators are being squeezed from two directions: regulators demanding encryption and monitoring, and commodity costs spiking on tariffs and supply shocks.

What changed

The physical security hardware market is consolidating fast around digital credentials and biometric access. The electronic access control system market grew from USD 9.96 billion in 2025 to USD 12.65–13.76 billion in 2026, a 7.2% to 8.4% annual climb through 2033. But that top-line growth masks a sharp profit margin squeeze. The top five manufacturers now control 70–80% of the market (2024–2025), locking out smaller vendors. Meanwhile, the juicy new money is in software: biometric access control software alone is projected to grow 15% annually through 2033, reaching $7.8 billion by then. What made this shift urgent? Two things converged. First, regulatory mandates made old card-based systems obsolete. NYDFS cybersecurity rules hit in May 2025. CISA's critical infrastructure reporting final rule landed May 2026. Sweden's new Cybersecurity Act kicked in January 2026. All of them demand encryption, continuous monitoring, and access logs—things a swipe card cannot do. Second, digital standards broke vendor lock-in. Apple Wallet NFC integration went live in February 2026, and Aliro 1.0 standardization released that same month by the Connectivity Standards Alliance. Suddenly you could use your phone to unlock a door, and the data didn't belong to the lock vendor anymore.\n\nAt the same time, commodity costs exploded. Steel and aluminum tariffs hit 50% in mid-2025. Baseline global tariffs added another 10% to imports. Polymer supply fractured: 31 force majeure declarations in Q1 2026 alone, with price spikes of 30%. Manufacturers faced a choice: swallow the margin hit or move production closer to customers. Many chose nearshoring. Honeywell's USD 4.95 billion acquisition of Carrier's Global Access Solutions in June 2024 signals consolidation, but also desperation—bigger is the only way to survive tariff walls and scale a cloud-centric software business. ASSA ABLOY, the Swedish giant, keeps acquiring: Kentix GmbH in October 2025 for data center access control, then hit their 400th acquisition milestone in April 2026. Each deal chases either specialized software, regulatory compliance tech, or geographic footprint.\n\nMeanwhile, residential is now eating commercial's lunch. Smart locks jumped from $2.98 billion in 2025 to $3.43 billion in 2026, and residential captured 71% of that volume. Three years ago, the market was commercial-driven. Now homeowners are buying smart locks because their insurance companies are demanding it, or their mortgage lender is requiring it, or they're just done losing keys. That shift forces manufacturers to build two stacks at once: enterprise-grade encryption for data centers and banks, and consumer-friendly interfaces for homes and small offices. It's expensive. Margins are expected to stabilize around 5.9% in 2026 (according to projections from 2022 reports), which is thin for hardware and forces the whole industry toward recurring software revenue.

The evidence

  • Manufacturing: Electronic access control system market grew 7.2%–8.4% annually from USD 9.96B (2025) to USD 12.65–13.76B (2026).
  • Manufacturing: Biometric access control software projected to grow 15% annually through 2033, reaching USD 7.8 billion by then.
  • Manufacturing: Top five firms hold 70–80% market share (2024–2025), consolidating vendor landscape.
  • Manufacturing: Tariff shock—50% steel/aluminum duties (mid-2025) plus 10% baseline global tariffs (2025) force margin compression or nearshoring.
  • Retail/Residential: Smart locks jumped from USD 2.98B (2025) to USD 3.43B (2026), with residential now 71% of volume—a reversal from prior commercial dominance.

Who rides it

Large manufacturers like Honeywell and ASSA ABLOY win by consolidating the market and pivoting to cloud software and biometric credentials. They have the capital to absorb tariff costs, fund nearshoring, and build the encryption stacks regulators now demand. Smart lock vendors win by tapping the residential boom—insurance companies and mortgage lenders are now pushing adoption, creating a tailwind that commercial markets never had. Cybersecurity software vendors embedded in access control win by riding the 15% annual growth in biometric software.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market and smaller lock manufacturers get squeezed out—70–80% market share in the hands of five firms leaves almost no room for regional players or legacy card-based vendors. They can't afford the tariff hit, can't fund the cloud transition, and can't meet regulatory compliance. Building operators and enterprise IT teams lose pricing power; they now have to buy into consolidated vendor ecosystems and recurring software fees. Margins are collapsing (expected 5.9% in 2026) so the cost savings that used to come from commodity competition evaporate.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that Apple Wallet and Aliro 1.0 standardization (February 2026) promise interoperability but haven't yet proven they work reliably at enterprise scale. If lock vendors can't make NFC mobile credentials as secure and auditable as regulators demand, we're looking at a decade of hybrid systems—some locks taking cards, some NFC, some biometric—all running different encryption standards. That fragmentation makes incident response and forensics a nightmare for enterprises, and it delays the margin recovery manufacturers need. Meanwhile, tariff and supply shocks may not stop at 2026; another round of duties could force a whole category of vendors out of business within 18 months.

In one sentence

Guarding the physical door in a nutshell

Physical and digital security are merging into one regulated, consolidated market where tariffs and software growth rates matter more than hardware innovation.
