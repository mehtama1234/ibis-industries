B · Detecting & cleaning up

Catching the fraud

When you get breached, a whole forensics-and-detection industry springs into action—and it's growing fast.

Breaches used to be a one-time catastrophe. Now they're a regular business expense, which means fraud detection, forensic investigation, and data recovery have become their own industries. Three things converge: AI-powered fraud is exploding (deepfakes, impersonation), AI-powered detection is racing to catch up, and the legal and financial pressure to investigate every breach has created constant demand.

What changed

Three detection and recovery markets are growing in tandem, each addressing a different piece of the breach response.

Fraud detection software is the fastest-growing piece. The market hit $40.4 billion in 2026, with 18–24% annual growth through 2033—up from $18.4 billion in 2022. This is the edge of the battle: companies trying to stop fraud before it happens, not after. The industry has shifted from simple rule-based systems (flag this transaction if X happens) to AI-powered prediction that analyzes billions of data points in milliseconds. The trigger is deepfakes. In 2024, deepfake fraud attempts jumped 1,300%—from about one per month to seven per day. By 2026, 30% of enterprises no longer trust face-biometric-only systems to verify identity. So vendors are layering in behavioral biometrics (how you type, move the mouse, hold the phone), liveness detection (is this a real face or a recording), and multi-modal authentication (face plus voice plus behavior). Meanwhile, 71% of US companies experienced an uptick in AI-powered fraud attempts in 2025, and over 50% of modern fraud now involves AI-generated manipulation. Margins are compressing a little—vendors make 8–12% profit now, up from 5.9% in 2021—because scale lets them amortize R&D across more customers, but wage and tool costs are rising fast.

Digital forensics is the investigation piece, where specialists dig through servers, phones, and cloud logs after a breach to figure out what was stolen and how. This market was $3.74 billion in 2025 and is growing at 15.9% annually. The industry is consolidating: top 5 firms hold 38% of the market. Consolidation is driven by regulatory pressure—GDPR fines exceeded EUR 5.8 billion by early 2025, and 21 US states now have comprehensive privacy laws that all demand proof of what happened—and by the sheer volume of data to investigate. Ransomware and insider threats (which hit 83% of companies in 2024) have exploded the caseload. Forensic firms are moving from reactive (you get hacked, then we investigate) to proactive (we monitor continuously, preserve evidence in real time, and hunt for threats before a breach). AI is changing the work too: machine learning can now detect deepfakes with 92% accuracy and can sift through petabytes of logs in hours. But courts and regulators still demand transparent, defensible evidence, which creates friction—firms can't just feed everything to a black-box AI and call it done. Margins are tight: about 9% profit, with wage pressure pushing costs up. Recent moves signal consolidation: Cellebrite bought ARM virtualization software (Corellium) for $170M in December 2025, then acquired a UAV forensics firm in February 2026. OpenText spent $185M on litigation-workflow software (ADF Solutions). Magnet Forensics integrated with Microsoft Sentinel in February 2026 to automate evidence capture from cloud systems.

Data recovery services are the last-resort piece, for when ransomware or equipment failure destroys data and you have to physically recover it. This market was $5.2 billion in 2025 and grew to $6.04 billion in 2026—16.1% growth. But this market tells a story of economic pain. Ransomware demand has saved the industry: Q1 2025 saw 2,063 victim organizations (double the year before), and 77% of intrusions now include data exfiltration, making recovery a critical part of incident response. Mean recovery costs hit $1.53 million globally (not counting ransom). However, the industry faces brutal structural headwinds. SSDs now comprise 80%+ of enterprise storage (up from 36% in 2020), but SSD recovery is far more complex and risky than old hard-drive recovery—it's also way more expensive. Cloud backup is growing at 19.6% annually (projected $9.25 billion in 2025), so more enterprises can avoid on-premise data loss entirely. And profitability is crushed: consumables (parts, tools, supplies) now eat 50% of gross revenue in 2026, leaving only 50% margin before you pay anyone or run an office. Recovery firms are still fragmented—DriveSavers and Ontrack are the biggest independent players, but Seagate, Western Digital, and Dell all run authorized repair shops—and consolidation is slower here than in software. Still, growth is real: Ontrack raised $50M in Series B funding in 2025 specifically to expand labs and R&D, and it partnered with AWS in 2024 to offer hybrid cloud recovery, signaling a bet that the recovery business will last.

These three markets are woven together. When a company gets breached, fraud-detection tools might spot something odd first. Then forensic investigators move in to gather evidence and understand scope. Then data recovery teams extract files if encryption or deletion is involved. The regulatory clock is ticking through it all—GDPR, CCPA, state privacy laws, PCI-DSS requirements—which makes every step defensible and documented. The result is a tightly coupled industrial supply chain, where each player's growth is the other player's justification.

The evidence

  • Fraud detection software: market hit $40.4 billion in 2026, growing 18–24% annually; deepfake fraud attempts surged 1,300% in 2024, forcing enterprises to layer behavioral biometrics and liveness detection
  • Digital forensics: $3.74 billion market in 2025, growing 15.9% annually; top 5 firms hold 38% share; GDPR fines exceeded EUR 5.8 billion by early 2025, driving demand for forensic investigation and evidence preservation
  • Data recovery services: $6.04 billion in 2026, up 16.1% from 2025; ransomware doubled victim count year-over-year in Q1 2025, with 77% of intrusions now including data exfiltration
  • AI-powered fraud escalation: 71% of US companies experienced an uptick in AI-powered fraud attempts in 2025; over 50% of modern fraud now involves AI-generated manipulation
  • Ransomware and insider threat surge: 83% of companies faced insider threats in 2024; Q1 2025 ransomware victim count doubled to 2,063 organizations; mean recovery cost around $1.53 million globally (excluding ransom)

Who rides it

Fraud-detection software vendors are the clear winners. They own the fastest-growing market ($40.4B in 2026, 18–24% growth), and demand is structural—every company needs to catch AI-powered fraud faster than the attackers can launch it. Digital forensics firms win too, especially consolidators like Cellebrite and OpenText, because regulatory pressure (GDPR, CCPA, 21 state privacy laws) makes every breach investigation mandatory and defensible. Data recovery firms benefit from ransomware surge but face margin compression. Cloud backup providers are the long-term winner because they let enterprises avoid recovery needs altogether.

Who's squeezed

Small and mid-market companies without detection budgets get squeezed. They can't afford AI-powered fraud systems (priced for enterprise), so they rely on older rule-based tools that miss AI-generated attacks. Data recovery shops on the low end get squeezed hardest: 50% of revenue now goes to consumables (parts), leaving almost nothing for profit, which forces consolidation and cuts. Companies using on-premise storage instead of cloud backup face higher recovery costs and longer downtime. Enterprises with legacy payment systems (pre-PCI-DSS compliance) face steeper fines and forensic costs when breached.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is regulatory lag. Deepfakes and AI-powered fraud are evolving faster than detection can adapt. 30% of enterprises already distrust face-biometric systems, and 50% of fraud now uses AI manipulation. But courts and regulators are still catching up to what "defensible evidence" means in an AI-powered world. A black-box fraud-detection model might catch 95% of attacks, but if a court rejects it as untransparent, the whole system breaks. Also, detection vendors are racing to add liveness detection and behavioral biometrics, but these create privacy and user-friction issues that could trigger backlash. If enterprises start rejecting multi-modal auth as too invasive, the arms race stalls.

In one sentence

Catching the fraud in a nutshell

Fraud detection, digital forensics, and data recovery have become industrial supply chains, each tier growing 15–24% annually to keep pace with AI-powered breaches and the regulatory bills that follow.
