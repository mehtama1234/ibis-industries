C · The compliance-industrial complex

Who Profits from the Rules

Lawyers, auditors, and accountants are the new tax on compliance—they capture the windfall, even as their own model cracks.

When the rules multiply, three industries profit: law firms, audit shops, and accounting services. In 2025 and 2026, they celebrated record revenues even as demand growth stalled and their talent pools collapsed. The pattern is clear: compliance rules have made expert advisors scarce and indispensable, at least for now.

What changed

The US law firms industry hit a historic moment in 2025. The market reached $426.7 billion, up from $367.9 billion in 2022, but the growth masks something ugly underneath. Demand—the actual legal work companies need done—grew only 1.9 percent in 2025. Expenses, though, jumped 9.1 percent. Law firms made record profits not by doing more work, but by raising rates. Fees worked per lawyer climbed 16.8 percent in 2025 alone. Clients noticed. Fifty-nine percent of corporate clients surveyed said they see no savings from outside counsel using generative AI, yet law firms burned through 9.7 to 11 percent more on tech infrastructure in 2025 just trying to stay ahead. The industry is consolidating fast: 59 law firm mergers happened in 2025, up 18 percent from 2024, with at least 16 more deals announced for completion in 2026. Hogan Lovells and Cadwalader combined to create a $3.6 billion revenue firm. The squeeze is real. In-house legal departments, squeezed on budget, are moving work away from premium Am Law 100 firms to cheaper alternatives. Milbank's June 2026 salary scale—$235,000 for starting associates, $455,000 for eighth-year associates—signals desperation for talent.\n\nThe audit industry faces a different but equally stark paradox. The market is worth $56.3 billion in 2025, but talent is fleeing. Over 300,000 accounting professionals have exited the field in recent years. CPA exam takers are down 30 percent since 2016. Open audit roles now take 73 days to fill, 41 percent longer than comparable positions. The Big Four (PwC, Deloitte, EY, KPMG) audit 97 percent of the S&P 500, which means they own the compliance monopoly. So they are pouring money into AI. PwC announced end-to-end AI automation of audits by the end of 2026. EY deployed AI to 80,000 tax staff and rolled out 150 AI agents for data collection, document review, and compliance tasks. Yet accounting firm M&A hit a record 194 transactions in 2025, up 26 percent year-over-year, with financial acquirers leading at 54.8 percent of deals. The regulatory environment shifted sharply in 2025: SEC audit enforcement hit an eight-year low with only 2 actions in 2025 versus 58 in 2024. PCAOB enforcement dropped 33 percent year-over-year to 39 combined actions. Lighter-touch regulation means less pressure to tighten scope, and scope keeps expanding. Sixty-four percent of companies now expect auditors to assess AI use in financial reporting. ESG and sustainability assurance are mandatory or expected across most public companies and many private ones. Cybersecurity ranks as auditors' number one global concern.\n\nAccounting services paint the starkest picture of all. The US market grew from $141.8 billion in 2022 to $145-155 billion in 2025, projected to reach $187.58 billion in 2026. But this growth is not coming from more junior accountants doing tax returns. AI adoption quadrupled in 2024-2025, with LLM-based audit tools reducing document analysis time by roughly 50 percent. That shrinks junior labor demand fast. Eighty-seven percent of finance leaders report talent shortages. CPA exam participation has dropped 30 percent since 2016. Mid-size accounting firms are trapped: too small to invest in the tech and talent infrastructure of private-equity-backed platforms, but losing solo and small-practice client bases to self-service software and automation. Private equity consolidators (Blackstone, Cherry Bekaert, Aprio, Baker Tilly, Citrin Cooperman) are buying smaller and mid-market firms at 4 to 7 times EBITDA multiples, creating mega-platforms that chase scale and efficiency. KPMG received approval to launch KPMG Law US in 2025, the first Big Four law firm in the US under Arizona regulatory reform. KPMG's tax revenue grew 7.5 percent in 2025, outpacing competitors. The Big Four expanded workforces in 2025 while smaller firms struggled to hire.

The evidence

  • Law firms: 59 mergers in 2025 (up 18% YoY), rate hikes of 16.8% per lawyer despite 1.9% demand growth, yet 59% of corporate clients see no savings from AI spending by outside counsel (2025).
  • Audit services: Big Four audit 97% of S&P 500; PwC expecting end-to-end AI automation by Q4 2026; EY deployed 150 AI agents to 80,000 tax staff; PCAOB enforcement down 33% YoY to 39 actions (2025-2026).
  • Accounting services: AI adoption quadrupled in 2024-2025, reducing document analysis time by 50%; yet 87% of finance leaders report talent shortages and 194 M&A deals in 2025 (up 26% YoY) consolidate mid-market (2025).
  • Talent crisis: CPA exam takers down 30% since 2016; open audit roles take 73 days to fill (41% longer than comparable); over 300,000 accounting professionals exited the field; positions once taking 30-45 days remain open months (2025-2026).
  • Regulatory expansion: 64% of companies expect auditors to assess AI in financial reporting; ESG/sustainability assurance now mandatory or expected; cybersecurity is auditors' #1 global concern; SEC audit enforcement at 8-year low of 2 actions in 2025 vs. 58 in 2024 (2025-2026).

Who rides it

The Big Four (PwC, Deloitte, EY, KPMG) and mega-platform accounting firms backed by private equity profit most. They have scale to invest in AI and talent, control 97 percent of S&P 500 audit work, and face lighter-touch regulation. Premium law firms capture rate premiums from corporate clients stuck paying for regulatory complexity. Consolidators acquire mid-market practices at cheap multiples and integrate them into efficiency machines. These players turn compliance scarcity into pricing power.

Who's squeezed

Solo practitioners, small accounting firms, and mid-market law firms are squeezed out. They cannot afford the tech stack or talent costs of scaled rivals. Their client bases shrink as self-service software and automation handle routine work, yet they still face the same compliance burden. In-house legal departments, starved for budget, are forced to move work to cheaper alternatives or junior staff, eroding the traditional pyramid of billable-hour labor. Small businesses that once hired a local accountant now get pushed toward DIY software or AI tools they half-understand.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The model for advisor firms—selling scarce expertise at premium margins—breaks the moment the expertise becomes automated or abundant. PwC, EY, and Deloitte are investing billions in AI expecting to cut costs and grow profits. But if AI actually succeeds at automating audit work, demand for human auditors collapses, pricing power vanishes, and the industry consolidates further into a handful of mega-platforms. Law firms are already seeing this: rate hikes are running ahead of demand, clients are walking away, and mergers are accelerating. The real risk is that advisors have gotten so expensive that clients finally decide the compliance itself is the luxury they can no longer afford—and then the entire industry faces a demand cliff.

In one sentence

Who profits from the rules in a nutshell

Compliance rules made lawyers, auditors, and accountants indispensable, so they raised rates even as demand stalled, but now AI and consolidation threaten to automate the very scarcity they profited from.
