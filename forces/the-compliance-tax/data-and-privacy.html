Data, Privacy & Cyber

Privacy and cyber rules raised the cost of holding data — and built a new profit machine for scale players.

Starting around 2023, the cost of keeping data safe went vertical. Privacy laws—GDPR fines exceeding €7.1 billion, California's CPRA, LGPD in Brazil, HIPAA in healthcare—stopped being legal abstractions. They became operational reality: compliance requirements embedded in hosting contracts, mandatory security audits, breach notification overhead, and staff time burned on paperwork. The infrastructure cost to hold data went from a commodity to a competitive moat.

What changed

Privacy rules made data expensive to hold. Start with the numbers: data processing and hosting services hit $485.7 billion in 2026, growing at 5.7% that year but forecast to grow at 16.8% CAGR through 2032. But there's a catch. Profit margins on basic hosting compressed from 6.7% in 2021 to near zero today because compliance and capex don't scale linearly—a small operator needs auditors, redundancy, and legal review almost as much as a big one. At the same time, GDPR fines exceeded €7.1 billion, and the EU AI Act hit full effect August 2, 2026, layering new obligations on any company processing data from Europeans. These rules don't cost nothing—they're sunk costs that a solo-operator host can't spread across enough customers.

The big players absorbed this differently. Hyperscalers—Amazon, Google, Microsoft—built compliance into the fiber of their infrastructure. AWS revenue hit $37.59 billion in Q1 2026 (up 28% year-over-year), and the company can afford to hire compliance teams, maintain HIPAA-certified zones, and build carbon-aware data centers because they have 31% market share and billions in revenue. For healthcare providers, research institutions, and mid-market firms, the path narrowed: either move to a big cloud vendor (Azure, AWS, Google Cloud—now with compliance baked in), or hire your own compliance staff and security engineers. Internet hosting services—13,817 establishments as of 2022, mostly under 10 people—faced a guillotine. Consolidation sped up: Newfold Digital (Clearlake Capital) folded Web.com into Network Solutions in June 2025, sunsetting the brand entirely. Technician wages rose 7%+ annually, driven by demand for people who understand both security and regulatory requirements. By 2025-2026, 53% of hosting providers said AI-driven automation was critical to survival, and 65% prioritized AI-powered malware detection—automation became table stakes just to compete.

The data processing layer felt the squeeze even harder. Seventy thousand enterprises operate in data processing, but consolidation is hollowing out the middle. The five largest hyperscalers spent $630 billion on infrastructure in 2026—up 36% from 2025—with $450 billion explicitly targeting AI. That money went to power, chips, and redundancy, not labor-saving automation. US data center electricity demand jumped from 23 GW in 2023 to 42 GW in 2026, and wholesale power prices near major data centers rose 267%. Meanwhile, 90% of data center operators cited staffing constraints as a bottleneck: demand for AI infrastructure specialists, high-voltage engineers, and cooling technicians vastly outpaced supply. Smaller data processing shops—the ones that might handle a hospital's imaging archive or a research lab's genomic data—couldn't compete for talent or power. The industry workforce grew from 636,786 employees in 2021 to a projected 753,671 by 2026, but those jobs concentrated at hyperscalers and tier-one operators. Tier-two and tier-three operators either specialized (edge compute, niche verticals) or exited.

What changed: privacy and cyber rules made the baseline cost of operating data infrastructure rise sharply, but scale masking that cost. Big players built compliance into their margins and hired domain experts. Smaller operators couldn't. At the same time, AI demand created a parallel crisis—power and talent became scarce, both expensive. For a healthcare provider or research institution looking to store sensitive data, the economic choice inverted: cloud giants with compliance teams and surplus power won, and the mid-tier hosting operator—the one that used to serve regional health systems and universities—disappeared.

  • Internet Hosting Services: 13,817 establishments (2022), highly fragmented with <10 person average; market slowed to 4.9% growth (2025) from 11.9% pandemic boom; consolidation accelerated (Newfold Digital folded Web.com into Network Solutions, June 2025); technician wages rose 7%+ annually

Who rides it

Big cloud and hosting players. AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, and hyperscalers like Equinix, Digital Realty, and CoreWeave saw compliance become a competitive weapon—they could afford to build HIPAA zones, maintain GDPR certifications, hire security experts, and publish compliance roadmaps. Their scale let them spread compliance costs across billions in revenue. Meanwhile, consolidation rewarded aggressive M&A: Verizon paid $20 billion for Frontier (March 2026), Charter announced a deal for Cox, CoreWeave and Digital Realty formed a $15 billion joint venture—big players got bigger, smaller competitors' options narrowed to acquisition or exit. Data privacy regulation, meant to protect users, ended up being a moat for incumbents.

Who's squeezed

Regional and mid-market hosting and data processing shops. The compliance tax is a fixed cost: auditing, breach notification, legal review, security staff time. A solo-operator host or regional data processor pays nearly the same compliance overhead as a cloud giant, but spread across 10x or 100x fewer customers. Technician wages shot up 7%+ annually because everyone needed security experts at once. Power became scarce and expensive as hyperscalers monopolized available capacity. Smaller operators couldn't outbid for talent or power, couldn't spread compliance costs, and couldn't maintain the margin to reinvest in automation. Consolidation accelerated—Newfold folded Web.com, regional ISPs disappeared into Charter and Verizon deals—and independent operators exited entirely. For healthcare providers and research institutions, this meant fewer options: move to a cloud giant's compliance-ready infrastructure, or pay a regional operator's higher prices while they liquidate.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Compliance becomes a compliance moat, not user protection. The thicket of GDPR, CPRA, HIPAA, AI Act, and cyber mandates was meant to protect privacy. Instead, it created a high barrier to entry—only scale players can afford compliance teams, audits, and redundancy. Smaller operators, regional providers, and specialty hosts disappeared. Now healthcare systems, research institutions, and SMBs have fewer real choices: use an AWS zone or move to a hyperscaler. Compliance concentration mirrors infrastructure concentration. The irony: stricter privacy rules aimed at protecting data may have narrowed the competitive landscape enough to make data protection less competitive and more expensive. When there are fewer independent operators, competition for compliance excellence weakens, and prices rise for everyone except the scale winners.

In one sentence

Data, privacy & cyber in a nutshell

A thicket of GDPR, CPRA, HIPAA, and cyber mandates turned data infrastructure into a capital-and-compliance arms race where big wins by consolidating, and small operators get priced out.
