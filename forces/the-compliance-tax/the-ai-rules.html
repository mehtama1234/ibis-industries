D · The tensions

Governing the Algorithms

New AI-governance rules land first on data, credit, and software—and they cost more to comply with than most midmarket firms can absorb.

The SEC is overhauling how rating agencies govern AI decisions. Europe already requires explainability of automated lending. Regulators want to know what the algorithm decided and why. That audit trail used to be optional. Now it is becoming the cost of doing business—and small players are bleeding out from it.

What changed

The compliance infrastructure around AI governance has turned explanation into a new operational burden. When a credit algorithm denies a loan or flags a transaction, the borrower can now demand to know why. That means the firm has to preserve the model's logic, document its training data, version its weights, and prove it didn't discriminate. Equifax, TransUnion, and Experian have all raised their compliance spending, and the SEC's proposed overhaul of rating agency rules (2026) will spike that further by requiring governance oversight, analyst independence audits, and methodology disclosure. This is not a one-time fix. Every model update triggers a new compliance cycle. Every breach—and the industry has suffered major ones: TransUnion exposed 4.4 million Americans' names and Social Security numbers in August 2025, and 700Credit exposed 5.8 million between July and October 2025—pushes regulators to demand tighter data handling, forcing bureaus and lenders to invest in security infrastructure that was considered optional five years ago.

What makes this worse is that the compliance tax compounds with scale. A startup credit bureau can hire a single compliance officer. A regional lender can buy off-the-shelf audit software. But when the SEC, FTC, and state attorneys general all want different documentation formats, different retention periods, and different explainability standards, the cost of coordination grows exponentially. Moody's reported $7.7 billion in revenue for 2025 with 9% growth in both analytics and core investor services, and crucially, operating income jumped 17% year-over-year (2025)—the big player absorbs the burden and passes it to customers. TransUnion's compliance costs are baked into their margins (averaging around 29% industry-wide in 2026), but they can spread those costs across millions of credit reports. A regional credit union or a fintech lender trying to build an AI lending system? They hit the same SEC requirements, the same explainability standards, the same data retention rules. They just can't amortize the cost across as many transactions.

Meanwhile, the software and AI-services layer is consolidating at breakneck speed. Salesforce spent billions acquiring Informatica (November 2025) for data integration and AI pipeline capabilities, Qualified.com (April 2026) for AI-driven sales acceleration, and Fin (June 2026) for AI customer support automation. These aren't feature acquisitions—they're acquisitions of compliance infrastructure. Building an AI agent that handles customer support requires not just the model but the governance layer: logging every decision, proving it doesn't discriminate, explaining why a customer was routed to a human, auditing for data lineage. A small software vendor can't afford that stack. So they get acquired, or they disappear. Seventy-two percent of SaaS M&A targets pitched AI as their core value in 2025, but what they were really offering was the compliance machinery already baked in. The enterprise software market is growing to $317.5 billion in 2026 (13.8% year-over-year growth), but that growth is concentrated at the top: big platforms expanding their compliance infrastructure, not new entrants building novel solutions.

The compliance tax here is turning simplicity into a luxury. A firm with established governance practices, existing audit trails, and deep regulatory relationships can layer AI into their products. They can afford the explainability audits, the annual security updates, the data-retention protocol changes. Smaller competitors, regional banks, independent lenders, and software vendors without enterprise backing—they face the same regulatory demands but can't spread the cost. Some will consolidate. Others will retreat into niches too small or too specialized for regulators to scrutinize heavily. The middle is being crushed.

The evidence

  • Credit bureaus: TransUnion exposed 4.4 million Americans in August 2025; Moody's reported 9% revenue growth and 17% operating income increase in 2025.
  • Enterprise software: $317.5 billion market in 2026 growing 13.8% year-over-year; Salesforce acquired Informatica (Nov 2025), Qualified.com (April 2026), and Fin (June 2026) for AI-governance infrastructure.
  • SaaS consolidation: 72% of M&A targets in 2025 pitched AI as core value, revealing that compliance-ready infrastructure is the new acquisition driver.
  • Alternative scoring: Zest AI demonstrated its model could approve 25% more loans while holding risk constant, disrupting the traditional credit-bureau moat.
  • Regulatory pressure: SEC's proposed overhaul of rating agency rules (2026) mandates governance oversight, analyst independence audits, and methodology disclosure.

Who rides it

Big platforms with existing compliance infrastructure win. Moody's, Salesforce, and other scale players can absorb the regulatory burden and amortize it across customers. Acquisition targets win temporarily—they get bought before the compliance tax makes them unviable. Consultants win. Every new regulation spawns demand for compliance auditing, governance frameworks, and integration work.

Who's squeezed

Regional credit unions, independent fintech lenders, and mid-market software vendors lose. They face the same SEC requirements, the same explainability audits, the same breach-response protocols as Moody's or Salesforce. They just can't spread the cost. Some will fold. Others will get acquired, shedding independence. The margin for a regional competitor shrinks faster than the market grows.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The consolidation cascade. As compliance costs kill mid-market players, the industry becomes more concentrated. Three credit bureaus, a handful of rating firms, a few software giants controlling the core infrastructure. That concentration makes regulatory capture easier and consumer choice nearly nonexistent. When one firm dominates and faces minimal competition, regulators lose their most powerful lever: the threat of market disruption if they overreach.

In one sentence

Governing the algorithms in a nutshell

New AI-governance rules make explaining algorithmic decisions mandatory, and that compliance infrastructure costs more per dollar of revenue for small firms than for scale incumbents—so the market is consolidating into a few big platforms.
