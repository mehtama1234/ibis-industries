B · Healthcare's paperwork

Healthcare's Paperwork Machine

Hospitals bleed money on reimbursement shortfalls while drowning in regulatory overhead, forcing consolidation and pricing out independents.

US hospitals face a structural crisis: Medicare reimburses at only 82-87 cents per dollar of care, while operating margins have collapsed from 7.6% in 2022 to just 1-2% in 2025. At the same time, a thickening web of regulatory requirements—Medicare rules, AI governance, supply chain traceability, quality management systems—has made administration itself a massive, growing cost center. The result is ruthless consolidation, labor conflict, and the extinction of independent hospital networks.

What changed

The reimbursement math broke in 2024-2025. Medicare's shortfall—paying 13-18 cents less than the actual cost of care—combined with labor costs that jumped 42% since 2020, left hospitals with nowhere to hide. A hospital system can't cut patient care to fix the math. What it can do is consolidate, automate, and outsource. But doing any of that requires money and compliance expertise. Small regional systems don't have it. In January 2025, Prospect Medical Holdings filed Chapter 11 and shed 10 of its 16 hospitals. Steward Health Care's bankruptcy became the largest in the hospital sector in 30 years. Twenty hospital bankruptcies in 2025 alone, up from 15 in 2024.

Simultaneously, the regulatory load exploded. The FDA's Quality Management System Regulation, which took effect February 2, 2026, harmonized US device rules with ISO 13485:2016 and required new levels of supplier traceability and control. For device manufacturers, this meant restructuring decades of offshoring. Tariffs added muscle to the rule: a 10% global tariff on medical devices (April 2025), plus 50-100% tariffs on syringes and needles, forced major OEMs like J&J (facing $400 million in added cost for 2025) to rethink supply chains almost overnight. Smaller manufacturers couldn't absorb the hit. AI adoption accelerated in hospitals—75% of health systems now use at least one AI tool (up from 59% in 2025)—but almost entirely for tasks that don't directly generate revenue (note-taking, image analysis). The systems buying the tech, however, are the large consolidated ones with IT infrastructure and compliance staff to implement and defend these tools.

Scale became survival. In Q1 2026, hospital M&A rebounded to 22 transactions worth 14.5 billion—the highest Q1 in more than 5 years. Ascension spent 3.9 billion to acquire AmSurg, betting that owning ambulatory surgery centers (which now do 9% more procedures than they did in 2023) would give it control over profitable procedures that used to happen inside hospitals. Larger systems negotiate tariff-resilient supply chains. They absorb FDA compliance overhead. They can afford the IT teams to deploy AI and track its outputs. Regional hospitals and independents can't. Labor power shifted too: the Kaiser strike in January 2026, involving over 30,000 workers, exposed how labor unions now have leverage in an industry where staffing is the largest cost center and consolidation has reduced employer options. But unions only protect workers at large systems. Independent practices and small hospitals get crushed.

The FTC's new Healthcare Task Force, active in 2026, is blocking cross-market mergers and scrutinizing private equity deals to slow this wave. But the structural forces are stronger than any regulator. Medicare payment rules, FDA device rules, supply chain tariffs, and the automation threshold (it costs less to buy AI tools than hire staff at union rates) all point in one direction: hospitals must be big or disappear. The 5,875 hospital establishments in the US employed 5.8 million people in December 2025. That number will shrink.

The evidence

  • Hospitals: US hospital margins collapsed to 1-2% in 2025 from 7.6% in 2022, while Medicare shortfall widened to 13-18 cents per dollar of care
  • Device makers: Medical device tariffs (10% global, 50-100% on syringes/needles, April 2025) added $400M+ in annual costs to J&J alone
  • Labor: Over 30,000 Kaiser Permanente workers struck in January 2026; hospital bankruptcies rose to 20 in 2025, highest in years
  • Consolidation: Hospital M&A rebounded to $14.5 billion across 22 deals in Q1 2026, highest Q1 in 5+ years; Ascension acquired AmSurg for $3.9 billion
  • Regulation: FDA Quality Management System Regulation enforcement began February 2, 2026, tightening supplier controls; 75% of health systems now use AI tools (up from 59% in 2025)

Who rides it

Large hospital systems and medical device OEMs (Medtronic, Abbott, Boston Scientific, J&J, Edwards Lifesciences) win by absorbing compliance overhead and negotiating tariff-resilient supply chains. Ascension, buying AmSurg and ambulatory centers, captured profitable outpatient procedures. Health systems with IT infrastructure can deploy AI for automation and reduce reliance on union labor. Private equity and larger regional networks consolidate at fire-sale prices. Consultants selling compliance, supply chain restructuring, and AI implementation solutions capture a growing slice of hospital budgets.

Who's squeezed

Independent hospitals and regional chains disappear. Prospect Medical, Steward Health Care, and dozens of mid-sized systems can't survive the reimbursement shortfall or absorb tariff shocks and FDA compliance costs. Smaller medical device suppliers and contract manufacturers face impossible margins when tariffs hit 50-100% and they lack the scale to nearshore. Hospital workers at smaller systems lose leverage; labor unions protect workers at big systems, but independents are gone. Communities lose local control of healthcare infrastructure. Patients at remaining hospitals face fewer options as consolidation proceeds.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that consolidation accelerates healthcare inflation. Large hospital systems, freed from regional competitors, can raise prices and capture insurance reimbursement more aggressively. Meanwhile, the FTC's Healthcare Task Force is blocking cross-market mergers but can't stop within-market consolidation (one big system swallowing two smaller ones in the same region). The result: higher prices, fewer choices, lower quality. Simultaneously, regulatory pressure keeps pushing automation and AI, which large systems can afford but small ones cannot, deepening the divide. If consolidation continues at the current pace, within five years most Americans may have access to only one or two dominant health systems in their region, each facing no competitive pressure to keep costs down.</worry> </invoke>

Healthcare's paperwork machine in a nutshell

Compliance and reimbursement rules have turned healthcare administration into such a costly burden that only large systems can afford to stay in business.
