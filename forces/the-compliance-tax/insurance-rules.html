Insurance & the regulators

State rules and new tech risks turn insurance into a compliance burden—and a margin game rigged for scale.

Rules are making insurance more expensive and more complicated. Health insurers are hiking premiums faster than anytime in 15 years. Cyber insurers are tightening underwriting and dumping risky firms to specialty carriers. Workers' comp carriers in California are exiting the market entirely. What changed is that compliance—to state data laws, to cybersecurity standards, to medical-cost regulations—has become inseparable from risk pricing itself.

What changed

Health insurance entered 2025-2026 in a state of violent repricing. Employer-sponsored premiums jumped 9 percent—the highest in 15 years—while ACA marketplace premiums for 2026 climbed 18 percent, with some benchmark plans jumping 26 percent beyond what insurers had even proposed. The real shock arrives December 31, 2025: enhanced ACA premium tax credits expired. That means an unsubsidized individual paying $888 annually in 2025 will face a $1,904 bill in 2026—more than double. Medicaid is shrinking for the first time since COVID, down 7.6 percent in 2025, as new work and reporting requirements in the 2025 reconciliation bill kicked in. What made this repricing possible is that the regulatory ground shifted. Subsidies vanished. Work requirements returned. Medicaid gates tightened. The largest threat to traditional insurers isn't regulation—it's that Amazon, GoodRx, and TrumpRx.gov are now siphoning off the most profitable segments. Amazon launched a GLP-1 management program through One Medical that charges as low as $25 per month in 3,000 cities (expanding to 4,500 by year-end 2026), undercutting traditional insurance entirely.

Cyber insurance shows the compliance tax in its starkest form. The market hit $7.02 billion in direct written premiums in the US in 2024, growing at 19.3 percent annually through 2026—still rapid, but slowed from the 25 percent-plus surge of earlier years as the market matured and competition squeezed rates. But then ransomware exploded. In the first half of 2025 alone, ransomware accounted for more than 90 percent of all cyber insurance claim severity. Initial ransom demands surged 47 percent year-over-year. Technology companies posted the highest average ransomware severity at $875,000, followed by finance and insurance at $731,000 and healthcare at $675,000. Even as claims accelerated, the market's combined ratio sat at 65.4 percent in 2025—still profitable, since breakeven is 100—but underwriters are signaling alarm. Third-party liability claims jumped 70 percent, the largest single-year increase on record. California's CCPA cybersecurity audit mandate took effect January 2026, requiring regular audits. AIG launched an AI-enhanced underwriting platform evaluating 700-plus cybersecurity variables per policy. The message is clear: compliance infrastructure—audits, penetration testing, staff credentials, incident response plans—is now built into pricing. Firms with loose security get denied outright or shunted to specialty carriers charging 2-3 times standard rates.

Workers' compensation insurance is caught in a different bind: regulation at the state level has fractured the market. Premiums nationally fell 3 percent to $41.6 billion in 2024, and the industry maintained an 86 percent combined ratio—the 11th straight year of underwriting gain. But claim severity jumped 6 percent in 2024, and medical costs are accelerating in certain states. Florida saw a 41 percent rate spike in 2025 after fee-schedule changes. California, home to 10 percent of US employment, posted a 127 percent combined loss ratio in 2026, forcing carriers to reduce capacity or exit entirely. This isn't a soft-market problem—it's a bifurcated market. Carriers with strong capital and predictive analytics are profiting. Smaller players, and those lacking technology to model state-specific medical inflation and presumption laws, are being pressured out. The compliance tax here isn't a single rule; it's a patchwork. Each state's medical-fee schedules differ. Each state's definition of compensable injury expands differently. Cumulative trauma and presumption-law claims are expanding the definition of injury faster than carriers can reserve for it. A carrier without 50-state medical compliance and claims analytics cannot survive.

The evidence

  • Health insurance market: $1.6 trillion (2026) with 9 percent employer premium increases (highest in 15 years) and unsubsidized ACA premiums doubling from $888 to $1,904 annually after December 31, 2025 subsidy expiration.
  • Medicaid shrinking 7.6 percent in 2025 (first decline since COVID) as new work and reporting requirements in 2025 reconciliation bill took effect.
  • Amazon launching GLP-1 management at $25 per month (April 2026) through One Medical, expanding from 3,000 to 4,500 cities by year-end, undercutting traditional insurance for high-margin segments.
  • Cyber insurance at $7.02 billion (2024) with ransomware driving 90 percent of claim severity in H1 2025; third-party liability claims surging 70 percent year-over-year, the largest single-year increase on record.
  • California CCPA cybersecurity audit mandate (effective January 2026) driving demand for cyber insurance; AIG deploying AI platform evaluating 700-plus variables per policy to tighten underwriting.
  • Workers' compensation bifurcating by state: Florida up 41 percent (2025), California posting 127 percent loss ratio (2026) and forcing carrier exits; claims severity up 6 percent in 2024 amid cumulative-trauma and presumption-law expansion.

Who rides it

Large, well-capitalized insurers with 50-state compliance infrastructure and advanced data analytics win. Aetna, UnitedHealth, and Cigna control health insurance and can absorb premium volatility and Medicaid shrinkage through scale. In cyber, Chubb, Travelers, and AIG are investing heavily in AI underwriting and can retain profitable segments by pricing in compliance overhead. In workers' comp, carriers like Zurich and Liberty Mutual with state-by-state medical expertise and machine-learning claims modeling are gaining share. Consultants, auditors, and security vendors win too—as compliance becomes pricing, demand for CCPA audits, penetration testing, and cyber-incident response planning explodes.

Who's squeezed

Small employers, small insurers, and startups get squeezed. A mid-market employer (100-500 employees) faces the full weight of a 9 percent premium increase in 2025-2026 while losing access to cloud-based compliance platforms that larger rivals subsidize. Regional workers' comp carriers in high-cost states like California face exit or absorption. The self-insured small business—once a competitive escape valve—now must hire compliance staff, run CCPA audits, and maintain incident response procedures, or lose insurability. For individual health insurance buyers, the subsidy cliff on December 31, 2025 is catastrophic: a $1,004 annual cliff pushes millions off the individual market entirely, reducing insurers' customer base but increasing claims concentration among the remaining enrollees.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The largest risk is a cascade failure in state-regulated markets. If medical cost inflation in Florida, California, or New York outpaces state fee-schedule adjustments, carriers will exit those states entirely, leaving self-insurance or high-cost specialty carriers as the only options. Simultaneously, if ransomware and third-party liability claims continue surging (third-party up 70 percent in a single year), the cyber market's 65.4 percent combined ratio will evaporate, triggering a hard market and denial of coverage for mid-market firms. The compliance tax then becomes a lock-in: only large firms with in-house security, actuarial, and legal teams can obtain insurance at any price, while smaller firms face uninsurable risk or massive premium shocks that force self-insurance or exit.

In one sentence

Insurance & the regulators in a nutshell

Compliance rules, state-level medical fees, and ransomware claims are turning insurance into a scaled game where big carriers win, consultants profit, and small operators face denial or triple-digit rate increases.
