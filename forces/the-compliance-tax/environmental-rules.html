C · The compliance-industrial complex

Environmental & climate rules

EPA standards and climate mandates are turning environmental compliance into a $27.4 billion consulting market—feeding the Big Four while squeezing smaller operators with tariff costs and engineer shortages.

The EPA's 2024 PFAS drinking-water standards, CERCLA hazardous-substance designations, and methane regulations are creating multi-year remediation pipelines that flow directly through corporate and government budgets. Environmental consulting has grown into a $27.4 billion market in 2025, but the work is mostly expensive: site assessments, remediation planning, and compliance audits. The real story is who pays the bill and who makes the margin.

What changed

Environmental rules have flipped from an occasional cost to a permanent tax on operations. The EPA's April 2024 drinking-water PFAS standard—covering six forever-chemicals—triggered multi-year testing and remediation pipelines that flow through 2026 budgets and beyond. Three months later, in July 2024, PFOA and PFOS were designated as CERCLA hazardous substances, meaning any facility that touched them now needs legal support and site cleanup planning. These aren't one-time expenses. They're cascading: the PFAS rule alone created demand for environmental assessments, GIS mapping, ground-testing, treatment design, and litigation support. An updated methane rule taking effect in August 2025 will push 705 additional landfills toward capture systems, adding another wave of site-specific engineering work.

The cost advantage has shifted decisively to scale. Environmental consulting is a $27.4 billion market in the US (2025), growing at 1.6%, but that headline masks violent consolidation. In February 2025, WSP paid $1.78 billion to acquire Power Engineers, instantly adding depth in renewable-energy infrastructure and environmental design. Jacobs spun off its Cyber and Intelligence units to focus fire on infrastructure and environmental work. Montrose absorbed multiple boutique firms in 2024 and early 2025. The Big Four—Jacobs, WSP, Tetra Tech, and Stantec—are scooping up smaller regional competitors at valuations that reward size, client relationships, and the ability to staff 50-person teams on a three-month notice. Mid-sized firms operate at 8-9% EBITDA margins (2025), but consolidators are paying for 12-15% margin upside within 18 months.

Labor is now the bottleneck and the cost driver. Licensed environmental engineers are "the tightest in a generation" to hire (ACEC 2025). GIS specialists command noticeable premiums. Project managers with water remediation experience can name their price. This labor scarcity is compressing smaller firms harder than large ones: a firm with five engineers needs each one; a firm with 500 can rotate, cross-train, and absorb a departure without a client emergency. Simultaneously, tariffs are tightening the procurement side. US tariffs on steel and aluminum doubled to 50% in 2025, pushing the effective tariff rate toward 13% by early 2026. For site remediation, this means equipment costs, pipe, drilling rigs, and analytical instruments all cost more. Smaller contractors can't absorb the hit and pass it to clients; larger ones have supplier relationships and can negotiate. The result is that costs are rising faster than margins can expand, and only scale survives.

Data center demand for renewable baseload power is creating new contract opportunities, but they're captured almost entirely by established players. In 2025, data center electricity consumption surged 17%, and AI-focused data centers are tripling their power demand. Tech companies signed 40% of all new corporate renewable energy PPAs in 2025, and they're contracts with utility-scale operators, not local environmental consultants. Waste-to-energy plants, a $1.4 billion industry operating 64 facilities across 21 states, are seeing 18-25% EBITDA margins on this new demand. But the industry has only nine major operators, and consolidation is accelerating: Covanta rebranded to Reworld (April 2024) under EQT Infrastructure and shifted to regionalized service lines; FCC Environmental, a global operator with 12 international facilities, entered the US market in June 2025 by acquiring the Wheelabrator South Broward facility. Small regional waste operators face a choice: sell to a consolidator now or watch margin compression as commodity power prices fall.

The evidence

  • Environmental Consulting (Business Services): $27.4B market in 2025, 65,000 establishments, 165,000 employees; consolidators like WSP ($1.78B Power Engineers deal, Feb 2025) and Jacobs (spun off Cyber/Intelligence units, Dec 2024) hunting scale; PFAS and methane rules creating multi-year pipelines.
  • Waste-to-Energy Plant Operation: $1.4B market in 2025, nine operators, 64 plants; 18-25% EBITDA margins; Covanta rebranded to Reworld (April 2024), FCC Environmental entered US market (June 2025); data center demand surged 17% in 2025.
  • Landfill Gas & Methane Regulation: 598 active systems producing 64% of renewable natural gas (2025); 705 additional landfills now mandatory capture candidates under August 2025 EPA rule; 20 new projects in 2025 added 39.9 Bcf capacity.
  • Labor Scarcity & Tariff Squeeze: Licensed environmental engineers 'tightest in a generation' (ACEC 2025); steel/aluminum tariffs doubled to 50% in 2025; GIS specialists command premiums; mid-sized firms at 8-9% EBITDA, compressed versus large-scale consolidators.
  • Corporate Renewable Energy: Tech sector signed 40% of all new corporate renewable energy PPAs in 2025; data center power demand tripling with AI workloads; contracts captured by utility-scale operators, not regional environmental service providers.

Who rides it

Big consulting firms, especially Jacobs, WSP, Tetra Tech, and Stantec, are consolidating the industry at $1+ billion acquisition prices. They have the labor depth to staff multi-year remediation projects, the supplier relationships to absorb tariff hits, and the client trust to win government and Fortune 500 contracts. Waste-to-energy operators with scale—Reworld, FCC Environmental, EQT-backed players—are capturing data center power contracts worth 18-25% EBITDA margins. Tech companies building data centers are locking in long-term renewable energy PPAs at favorable rates, trading compliance risk for contract certainty.

Who's squeezed

Mid-sized environmental consulting firms (8-9% EBITDA margins) can't compete on project scale or labor bench strength. Small regional waste operators face margin compression from commodity power prices and consolidator entry. Facilities that touched PFAS—manufacturers, treatment plants, defense contractors, airports—now carry permanent remediation costs with no clear exit. Local contractors and engineering practices lose work to out-of-state consolidators who can underbid on the back of scale and spread overhead. Labor-constrained firms (especially those hunting licensed engineers and GIS specialists) either pay up and compress margins or miss contracts entirely.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The permanent shift in regulatory overhead has no off switch. PFAS remediation pipelines flow through 2026 and beyond; methane rules will keep expanding; climate and ESG consulting is eating into base environmental-assessment margins. Small operators can't exit compliance costs by doing less—they're pinned between statutory obligations and scale-driven competitors who can absorb multi-million-dollar bids and wait three years for ROI. The real risk is consolidation-driven monoculture: as Big Four consulting firms dominate, they standardize methods, raise prices for captive clients (especially mid-market and government customers), and lobby for rules that favor complexity. And if tariffs stay elevated or rise, procurement costs on remediation projects will keep compressing margin for everyone except the largest operators.

In one sentence

Environmental & climate rules in a nutshell

Climate and environmental rules have made compliance consulting the faster-growing cost of doing business than the compliance itself.
