The Compliance Tax

A patchwork of AI, privacy, climate, and cyber rules turns compliance into a rising cost that favors scale and feeds consultants.

Compliance used to be a cost of doing business. Now it's becoming a business model unto itself—and it's rewarding scale while crushing the small operator. Every new rule, from AI governance to data privacy to climate disclosure, requires legal expertise, IT infrastructure, and specialized talent that only large organizations can afford to maintain. The result is a form of regulatory tax that transfers wealth from small businesses to consultants, law firms, and the biggest players who can absorb the cost.

What changed

Compliance costs have become the new barrier to entry in nearly every sector. In finance, the 2023 bank failures (SVB in March, Signature Bank in March, First Republic in May 2023) triggered a regulatory backlash that has hit smaller banks hardest—they lack the compliance infrastructure that the big four now rely on to stay ahead of regulators. Commercial banking profit margins compressed to 19.7% in 2025 (down from higher historical levels before the rate-cut cycle), but the pain fell unevenly: large banks absorbed technology spending while smaller players watched deposit flows shrink and regulatory scrutiny intensify. This is not a temporary disruption—it's baked in. The $1.26 trillion commercial real estate maturity wall coming by 2027, with loans refinancing from 4.3% to 6.2%, will demand sophisticated compliance and risk management tools that only the largest institutions can build or purchase.

Healthcare is a living laboratory of compliance tax at work. US hospitals face reimbursement that covers only 82-87 cents per dollar of care, but they must still comply with Medicare's quality reporting, privacy rules, and increasingly, AI governance for diagnostic tools. AI adoption accelerated to 75% of health systems in 2026 (up from 59% in 2025), not because it improved margins but because regulators and payers started demanding it—and the compliance overhead of implementing, auditing, and documenting AI systems is enormous. Yet only large hospital systems can justify the investment. Steward Health Care's bankruptcy in 2025 (the largest hospital-sector bankruptcy in 30 years) and Prospect Medical Holdings' Chapter 11 filing (shedding 10 of 16 hospitals in January 2025) both point to the same story: regional operators with 5-50 hospitals could not scale compliance and operations fast enough to keep up. Meanwhile, consolidation surged in Q1 2026 with 22 transactions worth $14.5 billion—the highest Q1 in five years—because size now confers survival.

Law firms are the compliance tax's beneficiaries. The $426.7 billion legal services market posted record 2025 profits, but the underlying growth was only 1.9 percent while expenses surged 9.1 percent. That gap is pure compliance tax: firms raised rates (per-lawyer billing capacity up 16.8%) to pay for regulatory work, AI infrastructure (tech budgets up 9.7-11 percent in 2025 alone), and the merged-firm overhead needed to handle multinational regulation. Sixty-three law firm mergers occurred in 2025 (59 tracked by Fairfax Associates, up 18 percent from 2024), and at least 16 deals already announced for 2026 completion, because regulatory chaos forces corporates to hire bigger firms with deeper benches. But here's the tax: 59 percent of corporate clients surveyed in 2025 report no clear savings from outside counsel using AI, yet they still paid for that AI buildout. The rate hike was not about efficiency—it was about compliance volume.

In data processing and hosting, compliance has become a scarce resource itself. The EU AI Act reached full effect on August 2, 2026, triggering a global compliance architecture. US data center electricity demand surged from 23 GW in 2023 to 42 GW in 2026 as AI hyperscalers compete for power and cooling—but every new data center needs environmental compliance, grid approval, and labor certifications that tie up years of project timeline. Smaller hosting operators simply cannot navigate this. Consolidation accelerated in Q1 2026 with $21+ billion in data center M&A (the most active quarter on record), and the five largest hyperscalers will spend $630 billion on infrastructure in 2026, up 36 percent from 2025. Ninety percent of data center operators cite staffing constraints as a bottleneck, and demand for AI infrastructure specialists, high-voltage engineers, and cooling technicians far outpaces supply—another compliance tax: you must hire certified talent or you cannot legally operate.

The evidence

  • Banking: Commercial banking margins compressed to 19.7% (2025) as regulatory costs from 2023 bank failures (SVB, Signature, First Republic) hit smaller banks disproportionately hard relative to the big four's infrastructure.
  • Healthcare: US hospitals reimburse at 82-87 cents per dollar (2025-2026) yet must comply with AI governance; Steward Health Care bankruptcy (2025, largest in 30 years) and Prospect Medical Holdings Chapter 11 (January 2025) exemplify regional operators unable to scale compliance fast enough.
  • Legal Services: Law firms posted record 2025 profits on rate hikes (per-lawyer billing +16.8%), not efficiency gains; legal demand grew only 1.9% while expenses surged 9.1%—the gap is compliance and AI infrastructure costs passed to clients; 59% of corporate clients report no savings from AI tools (2025).
  • Data Centers: Five hyperscalers spending $630 billion on infrastructure in 2026 (+36% from 2025), with $450 billion targeting AI; EU AI Act effective August 2, 2026, forces compliance architecture; 90% of operators cite staffing constraints as bottleneck.
  • Consolidation: Hospital M&A hit 22 deals ($14.5B in Q1 2026, highest Q1 in 5+ years); 63 law firm mergers in 2025 (+18% from 2024); data center M&A reached $21+ billion in Q1 2026 (most active quarter on record); size is the survival strategy under compliance tax.

Who rides it

The big platforms win. JPMorgan absorbed First Republic in May 2023 and emerged stronger relative to smaller competitors. Larger hospital systems (like Ascension, buying AmSurg for $3.9 billion) grab market share from regional operators who cannot afford compliance overhead. The largest law firms (Hogan Lovells acquired Cadwalader to create a $3.6 billion revenue firm) consolidate regulatory work and raise rates because corporates have no alternative—and they pass the compliance tax to their smaller clients. In data processing, the five hyperscalers capture 90 percent of AI infrastructure investment because they alone can navigate power allocation, environmental compliance, and talent acquisition across multiple geographies. Consultants, advisors, and service providers also win: law firms, compliance technologists, and audit firms all benefit from the rising cost of rule-keeping.

Who's squeezed

Small and mid-sized operators get squeezed. Community banks cannot afford the compliance infrastructure that the big four now rely on, so deposits flow to larger institutions (uninsured deposits surged $214.7 billion in Q4 2025 alone, suggesting clients are shopping rates toward bigger names). Regional hospital systems cannot justify AI infrastructure and compliance audits, forcing bankruptcies (20 healthcare bankruptcies in 2025, up from 15 in 2024) or acquisition at fire-sale prices. Small law firms lose corporate clients to Am Law 100 firms because multinationals need consolidated regulatory expertise under one roof. Mid-tier data center operators cannot compete for power, land, or talent in the hyperscaler arms race, leading to specialization or exit. The compliance tax is a wealth transfer from small business to scale, from operator to advisor, and from Main Street to Wall Street.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that compliance tax becomes self-reinforcing. As rules accumulate (AI governance, climate disclosure, privacy, cybersecurity, labor), the cost of staying compliant grows exponentially, not linearly. Small operators reach a tipping point where they cannot afford entry anymore—not because the rule is individually too expensive, but because the total compliance surface is too broad. This creates a permanent moat for the already-large and locks out new entrants. In healthcare, this already happened: Steward and Prospect bankruptcies were not caused by bad clinical care but by the inability to afford consolidated compliance, IT infrastructure, and regulatory staffing across dozens of facilities. The risk is systemic concentration—in every sector, compliance becomes an invisible tax that transfers market share from the distributed to the consolidated. If this continues unabated, we end up with a smaller number of larger operators, each one a more complex regulatory animal but with no real efficiency gain. We trade agility and competition for compliance theater and consolidation.

In one sentence

The compliance tax in a nutshell

Compliance costs are becoming invisible taxes that reward scale, feed consultants, and lock out small operators—concentrating markets without delivering efficiency.
