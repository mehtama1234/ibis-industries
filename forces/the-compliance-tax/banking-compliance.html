The bank compliance load

How capital rules, KYC, and consumer regs turned compliance into a moat for the big four.

Banks used to be simple: take deposits, make loans, collect spread. But since 2008—and especially after the 2023 SVB collapse—the compliance engine has become industrial. A thousand regulatory demands (Basel III capital tiers, CCAR stress tests, AML/KYC databases, consumer disclosure rules) cost small banks millions to staff and audit. Big banks absorbed the cost decades ago. Small banks are still paying.

What changed

The compliance tax made capital itself scarce. After SVB failed in March 2023 and Signature shut down two days later, regulators tightened every capital and liquidity rule. The banking sector had to hold more—and more expensive—equity. The big four (JP Morgan, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citigroup) already had the infrastructure: lawyers, compliance teams, automated reporting. When the Fed cut rates 1.75 percentage points between September 2024 and Q4 2025, net interest margins compressed. But here's the squeeze: a regional bank with $50 billion in assets pays 30+ people in compliance full-time. A community bank with $2 billion in assets can't afford that, so it outsources to consultants at $200–$400 per hour. That's a fixed cost per dollar of assets that kills ROA for small institutions. By Q4 2025, uninsured deposits surged $214.7 billion in a single quarter, meaning depositors moved money to safer names—the known big four. Small bank deposit costs didn't fall with Fed rate cuts; they stayed sticky because confidence evaporated. The result: net interest margins for community banks stayed compressed while big banks recovered to 3.39% by year-end 2025, highest since 2019.

Alongside deposit flight, capital rules made expansion impossible for small banks. Basel III capital minimums, stress tests that predict losses in a recession, and liquidity coverage ratios all require quarterly recalculation and board reporting. For a $10 billion bank, one mistake in the model can trigger a regulatory request for capital raises that costs 2–3% in dilution. For JPMorgan, the same mistake is a rounding error in a $2 trillion portfolio. Community banks faced another hidden cost: due diligence on climate risk, cybersecurity, third-party vendor management, and AI bias all rolled out between 2024 and 2026. These aren't optional; regulators issue cease-and-desist orders to banks that can't show controls. A small bank had to buy software, hire auditors, and train staff on frameworks that didn't exist five years ago. Big banks had already spent $1 billion building these systems and wrote the standards themselves through industry groups.

Compliance also made scale the only way to compete. The credit card issuing market is dominated: the top five issuers (Chase, Amex, Citi, Capital One, Bank of America) controlled over 50% of the $178.9 billion market in 2026. When Capital One closed its $35 billion acquisition of Discover in May 2025, it didn't shrink the market; it just consolidated it. Small issuers can't afford KYC infrastructure that screens for synthetic identity fraud (now the fastest-growing financial crime, costing $3.1 billion annually as of 2026). The Visa-Mastercard settlement pending regulatory approval threatened to cap interchange at 1.25%, which doesn't hurt JPMorgan (whose network effect and fee base can absorb it) but devastates a smaller player whose entire model depends on higher interchange. Fintech rivals (Affirm, Klarna) stole $70 billion in 2025 transactions by targeting BNPL, but they also sidestepped most traditional banking compliance—at least until regulators catch up.

The real cost lies hidden: commercial real estate. Banks hold $2.7 trillion in CRE exposure. By 2027, $1.26 trillion in CRE loans will mature, many originated at 4.3% now needing refinancing at 6.2%. Delinquencies are near decade highs. A bank regulator will demand proof that you stress-tested this portfolio, that you have a workout plan, that you can cover losses. JPMorgan has a 50-person CRE workout team; a small bank has one person wearing three hats. The compliance cost to hold, model, and resolve that risk is the same whether you're $2 trillion or $10 billion. It's a fixed cost of being a bank. Small banks are pricing out.

The evidence

  • Commercial banking: $1.5 trillion market (2026), but margins compressed as Fed rate cuts (1.75pp, Sept 2024–Q4 2025) eased deposit costs and depositors fled small banks post-SVB.
  • Credit card issuing: Top 5 control 50%+ of $178.9B market (2026); Capital One–Discover merger (closed May 2025) consolidated power as smaller issuers face $3.1B+ synthetic-ID fraud and pending Visa–Mastercard interchange cap.
  • CRE crisis: $1.26T in loans mature by 2027 at 6.2% (from 4.3% originals); banks hold $2.7T exposure with delinquencies at decade highs; compliance modeling cost is fixed regardless of bank size.
  • Deposit flight: Uninsured deposits surged $214.7B in Q4 2025 alone; SVB/Signature failures (March 2023) triggered regulatory tightening that small banks can't afford to staff.
  • Fintech disruption: BNPL stole $70B in 2025 transactions; synthetic-ID fraud costs $3.1B+ yearly (2026); small issuers lack KYC infrastructure to compete.

Who rides it

The big four banks (JPMorgan, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citi) win because they already had the compliance machines running. They absorbed the cost of building capital models, AML databases, and stress-test frameworks over decades. When the 2023 failures triggered regulatory tightening, they didn't build anew; they tuned what existed. Their deposit bases also benefit from a confidence moat: uninsured depositors fled smaller banks and moved to the known names, giving big banks cheap funding even as Fed rates fell. SVB's collapse was a gift that removed mid-size competitors (First Republic was sold to JPMorgan in May 2023) and reinforced that size equals safety in the eyes of regulators and the market.

Who's squeezed

Regional and community banks get squeezed. A $10 billion bank pays 30+ people full-time in compliance; a $2 billion bank outsources to consultants at $200–$400 per hour, pushing ROA below 1%. Their deposit costs didn't fall with the Fed's rate cuts because depositors—especially business owners—moved uninsured money to JPMorgan or Bank of America. Compliance costs for capital modeling, CRE stress-testing, climate risk, and cybersecurity are fixed regardless of asset size; they penalize small institutions on a per-dollar basis. Credit card issuers outside the top five face a similar wall: $3.1 billion in annual synthetic-ID fraud means KYC must be bulletproof, but building that system costs tens of millions that only big issuers can amortize. Small card programs are being acquired or shut down.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest risk is that compliance costs will become so high that the bottom 50% of banks (by asset size) consolidate or fail in the next five years, leaving only the big four and a handful of regional oligopolies. The $1.26 trillion CRE maturity wall means banks must hold more capital and model credit losses in unprecedented detail. Regulators will demand proof of controls on every emerging risk (AI bias, climate, third-party vendor cybersecurity). A small bank can't staff these functions; it also can't hire the consulting firms that big banks use because consultants prioritize large, profitable accounts. This creates a vicious cycle: small banks fall further behind, their ROA shrinks, they can't raise capital, and they become acquisition targets or failures. The market won't be more competitive; it will be more concentrated. Consumers will have fewer choices and face higher costs because the remaining players face no price competition.

In one sentence

The bank compliance load in a nutshell

Compliance rules that cost $50 million to implement at scale kill small banks on a per-dollar basis, handing deposits and market share to the big four.
