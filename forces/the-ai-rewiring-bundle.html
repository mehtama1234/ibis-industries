Design & the web: the fork

AI builders made commodity website design free. Strategy and brand just became worth more.

Five years ago, hiring a web designer meant hiring an agency. Today, a founder can build a professional site in hours with no code and AI doing the heavy lifting. This didn't kill the design industry—it split it in half. The glue work, the stuff you can template and automate, is now cheap and accessible. The hard work—positioning, brand, personalization—suddenly commands 55 to 80 percent margins and is the only thing agencies are competing on.

What changed

AI website builders went from a niche to a market force in three years. The global AI-powered website builder market hit $6.3 billion in 2026 and grew 26 percent year-over-year—with Gartner estimating that AI will reshape 70 percent of design and development effort for new web apps. Sixty percent of new websites are now built partly with AI.

What changed is not that designers disappeared. It's that template-based, execution-level design work collapsed in price and got absorbed into platforms. Wix, Squarespace, Webflow, and newer entrants like Bolt and Lovable all raced to add AI assistance—turning website builders from "template + drag drop" into "natural language to deployed site." Wix's Base44 acquisition (June 2025) generated $100 million in annual revenue in a single year and hit $150 million by May 2026. The message: AI code generation for websites is real, fast, and expensive to compete against.

Meanwhile, the design services industry—$61.23 billion in the US alone in 2025—is stratifying. Top-tier agencies are surviving by selling strategy, brand positioning, and personalization. Mid-market and small firms are racing to the bottom on price and losing. Talent is bifurcating: the best designers are moving up the value chain (brand, UX strategy, accessibility compliance), and the rest are being priced out by cheaper labor globally or displaced by AI builders.

The evidence

  • Web Design Services: US market $61.23 billion in 2025 with 8.5% CAGR (2025-2030), but stratifying into high-margin strategy tier (55-80%) and shrinking commodity tier
  • AI Website Builders: $6.3 billion market in 2026 with 26% YoY growth; 60% of new websites now built partly with AI
  • Wix Base44: $150 million ARR by May 2026, just nine months after acquisition, signaling how fast AI code generation is scaling
  • Website Creation Software: growth slowing to 2% annually in a 20-year-old commodity market, with traditional builders losing share to AI-native platforms

Who rides it

Agencies that sell brand strategy and personalization are winning. They've moved upmarket and are commanding 55 to 80 percent gross margins—real money. The companies acquiring design studios (Accenture bought Yumemi in early 2025) and the platform giants (Wix, Squarespace, Webflow) are winning because they own the distribution and the AI layer. AI-native builders like Bolt are winning because they don't have a legacy template business to protect; they can be pure AI-first. And founders, small businesses, and anyone who doesn't need bespoke positioning—they're winning too. They get professional-looking sites for a tenth of what they would have paid five years ago.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market and small design agencies are getting squeezed. They competed on execution—HTML, CSS, maybe a plugin or two—and that work just got commodified. Freelance web designers in developed markets are losing pricing power because they're competing against AI builders and global freelancers on a level field. And junior designers? The apprenticeship is drying up. The work that used to teach you the craft—the repetitive templating and basic execution—is gone. There's no low-risk entry point anymore.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The accessibility regulation tailwind (European Accessibility Act enforcement started June 28, 2025, and WCAG mandates are tightening in the US) created a temporary moat for compliance-focused agencies. But that moat is already eroding. AI website builders are baking accessibility compliance into their platform defaults and charging a premium, just like the agencies do. When automation eats the next layer up—personalization, conversion optimization, A/B testing logic—there's nowhere left to hide except pure brand and strategy work. That's real and scarce. But it also means the number of people who can charge premium fees for this work stays small.

Video & content: the automation squeeze

Generative video makes content cheap. Distribution and judgment become the real asset.

AI video tools (Sora, Runway, Kling) can now generate and edit footage at scale. This made video production cheaper and faster, which sounds great until you realize it eroded the value of the routine work that funded the entire industry. The scarce thing isn't content anymore—it's distribution, taste, and the rights to sell it.

What changed

Generative AI video and automated editing compression made the labor-intensive middle of video production redundant. A decade ago, every commercial, short film, or streaming show required dozens of editors, colorists, and effects artists doing months of careful work. Now a studio can generate lower-tier shots in minutes and let AI handle basic cuts and color grading. Sora and Runway didn't have to be perfect—they just had to be cheap enough that studios stopped outsourcing that work to humans.

This squeezed the post-production sector first. The video post-production market is $6.8 billion (2025), growing 5.9% annually, but that surface recovery hides the real damage: studios are now automating away 20-30% of routine editing work inside their own walls rather than paying freelancers and small shops. The Writers and Actors strikes of 2023-2024 already cut demand by halting production on 47 projects. Streaming giants like Netflix and Disney then slashed content budgets by $2-5 billion combined to chase profitability. Now the same studios are replacing the remaining work with software. Smaller editing shops are caught between shrinking job volume and per-project budgets that no longer cover their overhead.

The evidence

  • Post-production Services: US market $6.8B (2025), growing 5.9% but facing 20-30% automation of routine editing work by decade's end
  • Advertising Agencies: US market $88.7B (2026) with only 1.8% growth in 2026 and 4.7% CAGR 2021-2026, as AI automates ad copy, visuals, and strategy work previously done by human teams
  • Motion Picture & Video Employment: down 21% from 2016 to 2024 (405,400 employees); Writers and Actors strikes (May-September 2023) halted production on 47 projects; Netflix cut content spend then raised it back to $18B in 2025 while automating internal post-production
  • Agency Consolidation as Weakness: Omnicom-IPG merger ($13.5B, November 2025) created $25B revenue entity but reflects market dysfunction—clients flee to smaller shops and AI tools rather than traditional full-service model

Who rides it

AI tools let smaller, leaner creative shops compete with legacy holding companies. A 10-person production studio with Runway and a good pitch can now do work that once required a 50-person agency. Companies like Publicis (winning the Mars account) bet that specialist creatives plus AI automation outcompete the WPP model. Similarly, studios that own AI video and editing tools internally keep that margin instead of paying external shops. The winners are (1) studios with the capital to invest in in-house AI tooling, and (2) boutique agencies or production companies agile enough to build new kinds of judgment—narrative strategy, brand psychology, distribution insight—that a machine still can't do.

Who's squeezed

Traditional advertising holding companies and mid-market post-production shops. WPP's market cap fell from £24B to £3.1B in 2025, losing Coca-Cola and Mars to smaller competitors who deployed AI and cut overhead faster. Motion picture and video employment dropped 21% from 2016 to 2024; even as streaming budgets recover (Netflix now $18B in 2025), studios cut the freelancer and outsourcing budgets first. Post-production shops that did routine cutting, color, or effects work face margin compression from both angles: fewer projects from streaming consolidation, and lower budgets because studios now handle that work in-house with software.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Downstream creative atrophy. If AI handles routine editing and basic effects, fewer humans learn those skills at entry level. In a decade, the people who know how to cut film by hand or grade color competently may be rare. This creates a bottleneck: you need human judgment for the hard aesthetic calls, but you may not have a pipeline of editors trained to recognize them. The second risk is concentration: only large studios and well-funded boutiques can afford the AI tooling and still afford to pay human creatives to do the judgment work. Mid-market production shops disappear, and talent becomes dependent on a handful of big players.

Words & Publishing

AI drafting makes words cheap. The money follows brand, curation, and control of the catalog.

AI can draft faster than humans now. That floods the market with cheap text. But publishing was already hollowed out by the internet—what's left is whoever owns readers' trust, the data, and the rights to sell later. The winners own the attention and the archive. The losers write for nothing.

What changed

AI made routine text production nearly free. Audiobook narration, self-published titles, backend copywriting—all got automated and cheap fast. The economics inverted: supply of words exploded, but demand did not. Self-published ISBN titles hit 3.5 million in 2025, up 39% in a single year. Half of self-published authors now use AI tools. That abundance killed gatekeeping but also killed middlemen who used to live off scarcity.

What got scarce instead: reader attention, verified information, and brand trust. The Big Five publishers shifted from acquiring *more* titles to refining their portfolio—they stopped trying to buy everything and started picking winners. Amazon still controls 71% of reader purchases; indie bookstores unexpectedly opened 422 new locations in 2025, a 24% jump, because they offer curation humans actually want. Newspapers collapsed because advertising died and digital subscriptions stayed cheap. But the New York Times added 250,000 subscribers in Q1 2025 alone by making readers pay for judgment and access.

The evidence

  • Book Publishing: self-published titles hit 3.5 million in 2025, up 38.7% YoY, with audiobook sales at $2.43 billion (2025)
  • Book Publishing: Amazon Print-on-Demand revenue jumped from 24% to 30% of PublishDrive sales in 2025, but indie bookstores opened 422 new locations (24% increase, 2025)
  • Newspapers: print advertising revenue crashed from $73.2 billion in 2000 to $407.7 million in 2024; digital advertising only $315.1 million in 2024
  • Newspapers: News Media Corporation closed 14 newspapers in August 2025; New Jersey Star-Ledger and Times of Trenton ended print editions in February 2025

Who rides it

Publishers who own a brand readers trust and pay for. The New York Times, Wall Street Journal, Bloomberg—national outlets with subscriber walls that actually stick. Amazon, which controls the catalog and takes a cut of everything sold through it. Indie bookstores and niche publishers, who survived by offering curation that algorithms can't replicate. Self-published authors in romance and graphic novels, where social media drives discovery and AI tools made production affordable.

Who's squeezed

Small newspapers and local news outlets. Advertising revenue evaporated—print dropped 99% from its 2000 peak to $407.7 million in 2024. Digital ads never replaced it. Mid-market publishers without a paywall strategy or brand moat got squeezed. Freelance writers and editors who sold commodity work—copy editing, routine drafting, fact-checking structure—now compete with free models. Newspapers owned by private equity got bought and stripped of costs, like Lee Enterprises under Berkshire's refinance.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

Misinformation at scale. When drafting text is free and publishing is frictionless, the signal-to-noise ratio collapses. The same AI that let indie authors flourish also floods Amazon with SEO spam and scam books. Trust gets weaponized: if readers can't tell AI-generated content from human reporting, the few outlets that *do* earn trust monopolize the market. That concentrates power and leaves everyone else invisible.

Accounting & Audit: The Close Gets Hollowed

The machines are taking the grunt work, the liability is staying human, and the junior accountant has nowhere left to hide.

AI is automating the work that used to be the entry point into accounting: client bookkeeping, expense categorization, tax return assembly, even the audit sample selection. The machines are fast and accurate. But the sign-off stays human—because liability does. The result is a junior accountant squeeze that's reshaping the whole profession.

What changed

AI automation cut the time to analyze financial documents in half and now handles 80% or more of routine tax return preparation. Compliance tasks, data collection, and document review that used to burn hours of junior labor now run through AI agents—PwC's pushing end-to-end audit automation by late 2026, EY deployed 150 AI agents onto 80,000 tax staff in 2025-26, and Deloitte's Zora AI handles autonomous work.

But here's the catch: a machine can categorize an expense. A machine cannot sign the audit. Compliance judgment, the decision to flag a risky line, the call about what the auditor thinks is really going on—those stay human. So do the client relationships. The value isn't in the routine cognitive work anymore. It moved to whoever owns the technology, whoever has the data at scale, and whoever the client trusts to say yes or no.

That shift is hollowing out the middle. The Big Four are investing billions in AI—6.4 billion dollars in 2025-26 alone—and hiring people with tech skills. Mid-market and smaller firms are getting squeezed between AI tool costs and a collapsed talent pipeline. There are fewer junior roles. The roles that exist demand tech fluency that didn't used to be part of the job.

The evidence

  • Accounting Services: Tax revenue growth diverged sharply—KPMG's tax revenues grew 7.5% in 2025 while EY and Deloitte each posted ~5.5% and PwC only 1%, reflecting uneven positioning on AI-assisted offerings
  • Audit Services: PwC announced end-to-end AI automation of audits expected by Q4 2026 (April 2026 announcement); EY deployed 150 AI agents to 80,000 tax staff simultaneously (2025-2026)
  • Private Equity Consolidation: Accounting firm M&A hit record 194 transactions in 2025, up 26% YoY, with financial acquirers driving 54.8% of deals (up from 38.9% prior year), targeting 4-7x EBITDA multiples on mid-market and smaller firms
  • Talent Pipeline Collapse: CPA exam participation down 30% since 2016; 300,000+ accounting professionals exited the field; open audit roles now take 73 days to fill (41% longer than other positions); 87% of finance leaders report talent shortages

Who rides it

The Big Four win. KPMG launched a law firm in 2025 and is expanding capacity while rivals consolidated—Deloitte, EY, and KPMG all grew headcount in 2025. They own the AI, the data, the client relationships, and the ability to absorb the tech investment. Mid-market and smaller firms that own their niche and can keep clients close also hold their own—but only if they don't get rolled up into a PE consolidator chasing scale. Private equity financial buyers are betting that pooling smaller firms and running AI across them will unlock margin. They're likely right, but the fee pressure will be real.

Who's squeezed

Junior accountants get squeezed. The entry-level work that used to be rote but reliable—bookkeeping, tax prep assembly, expense categorization—is now mostly automated. The roles that remain demand coding, data literacy, or compliance expertise. For anyone who wanted to learn the craft before moving up, the path just got narrower and longer. Solo practitioners and small local firms lose because they can't afford the tech and can't compete on scale. A one-person CPA shop in a small town can still serve local clients, but the competition from AI-assisted online services and mid-market roll-ups keeps the fees compressed.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The junior accountant exodus could flip into an undersupply crisis faster than firms can retrain. CPA participation is already down 30% since 2016, and the remaining pipeline is drying up. If AI automation accelerates faster than the profession can rebrand itself as "judgment and strategy," not "data entry," the shortage will get acute. Firms can automate faster than they can hire. And if mid-market firms get rolled up by PE at a record pace (194 transactions in 2025, up 26% YoY), the consolidators might optimize the junior roles out of existence entirely, leaving fewer on-ramps for new talent to enter the field. That narrows the future talent pool even further.

Insurance claims and broking: The adjuster vanishes

AI triages claims and prices risk faster than humans; the middle layer of adjusters and brokers thins out as the work moves in-house or consolidates upward.

For decades, insurance adjusters and brokers were the connective tissue—the human judgment layer between carrier and customer. AI is automating the routine parts (claim triage, document sorting, risk quotes) so fast that the entire staffing model is breaking. In 2025-2026, the claims adjusting market shrank even as AI adoption exploded, and insurance employment collapsed.

What changed

AI made routine claims triage cheap and fast. Instead of a human adjuster reviewing a storm-damage photo and opening a file, a machine now does it in seconds, flags outliers for fraud, and routes the claim to the right endpoint. Carriers tested this in 2024 (8% deployed full-scale AI) and punched it in 2025 (34% deployed). By end-2026, 91% of insurers committed to production deployment. The speed gains are real: 50–70% cycle-time cuts, 20–50% cost drops per claim.

But the second part is what breaks the industry: once a carrier can automate straight-through processing in-house, they don't need the third-party adjuster network anymore. They just need better fraud detection and claims data. Meanwhile, climate rewired the volume pattern. Catastrophe frequency doubled (one billion-dollar event every 10 days now vs. every 82 days in the 1980s), but the composition flipped. Secondary perils—hail, flooding, wildfire—now dwarf hurricanes, creating year-round grinding volume instead of seasonal spikes. Adjusters can't hire fast enough to absorb the new shape of risk.

The evidence

  • Claims adjusting: Carrier deployment of AI claims automation jumped from 8% (full-scale) in 2024 to 34% in 2025, with 91% committed to production by end-2026 (2025-2026 carrier surveys); cycle-time cuts of 50–70% and cost reductions of 20–50% per claim (industry reports, 2025-2026).
  • Broking: Insurance employment shed 70,000 jobs in the 12 months through June 2026 despite GDP growth (BLS payroll data); 400,000 retirements projected by end-2026 across the sector (Bureau of Labor Statistics); 854 M&A transactions announced in 2025, the third-highest on record, concentrating market power among five giants (InsuranceJournal, 2025).
  • Climate pattern shift: Catastrophe frequency doubled (one billion-dollar insured event every 10 days vs. every 82 days in the 1980s); secondary perils (hail, flooding, wildfire) became costliest insured peril of the 21st century in September 2025, surpassing tropical cyclones (Aon, January 2026).
  • Market contraction: Claims adjusting market $10.8B in 2026, growing only 1.5% CAGR (2020–2025) and contracting in 2025 due to interest rates; insurance brokers and agencies $216B in 2025, growing only 0.6–2% annually vs. historical 2.6% CAGR (2025-2026 industry data).

Who rides it

Carriers and big brokers win. Carriers automate claims triage in-house, kill the third-party adjuster margin, and keep the spread. Big brokers (Gallagher, Brown & Brown, Willis Towers Watson) vacuum up mid-market rivals at record pace ($13.5B Gallagher-AssuredPartners deal in August 2025; $9.8B Brown & Brown-Accession in August 2025), consolidate tech, and capture the 14–17% margin that small players can't defend. Data and fraud-detection vendors win too—once claims are automated, the competitive edge moves to whoever spots the patterns first. Tech-first platforms (like Newfront, acquired for $1.45B by Willis in December 2025) capture value by wrapping the machine layer in better UX.

Who's squeezed

Independent adjusters and small brokers get squeezed out. The 25% of adjusters retiring by 2027 won't be replaced; carriers need fewer humans once AI can triage 50–70% faster. The 30,000 small independent brokers generating under $1.25M annually have no succession plan and no capital to buy tech. They can't compete on data or automation, so they get consolidated or disappear. The labor cliff is real: 70,000 jobs shed in 12 months (June 2025–June 2026), and the Bureau of Labor Statistics expects 400,000 retirements by end-2026. The industry is shrinking its workforce faster than it's adding new talent.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The biggest risk is that AI automates the easy 80% of claims (routine triage, straightforward indemnity) and leaves the hard 20% (fraud, subrogation, complex liability) to humans—but there won't be humans. Adjusters are retiring at 25% by 2027; new entrants aren't filling the gap. If a catastrophic year hits (and climate models suggest secondary perils will keep events frequent), carriers will have automation infrastructure but no human surge capacity to handle edge cases. Result: claims backlog, customer rage, and pressure on carriers to lower premiums or deny coverage. The second worry is that consolidation kills optionality. Five giant brokers now control most of the middle market; smaller carriers and niche underwriters lose negotiating power and distribution access. Independents can't start new brokers because the tech cost ($5.76B agentic AI market in 2025, $7.26B in 2026) is no longer DIY.

Law & consulting

AI does the draft; the pyramid cracks.

Law firms and consulting shops built their money machine on a simple stack: juniors do the routine work at low cost, partners handle the judgment, and the firm keeps the spread. AI is automating the routine. Juniors don't disappear—but the math that justifies hiring a college graduate to write his hundredth contract memo is broken. The firms that win are the ones that capture AI's gains before their clients demand the savings.

What changed

For decades, a law firm's or consulting shop's profit came from leverage. A senior partner charged $500 an hour; a junior associate cost $30 an hour but did 80 percent of the work—drafting contracts, running comps, writing memos. The firm pocketed the spread. AI upended the ratio. A language model now drafts a contract in 10 minutes for near-zero cost. A consultant no longer has to spend three weeks gathering industry data and formatting a PowerPoint deck; an AI agent does it in a day. The work isn't gone—it's cheaper and faster. That's a disaster for the model that priced junior labor at a markup.

The law industry felt this first. Firms jacked up their rates—fees per lawyer were up 16.8% in 2025—and landed record profits. But the bedrock numbers were weak: demand grew only 1.9 percent while expenses jumped 9.1 percent. They're spending big on AI infrastructure (budgets up 10% in 2025) and still seeing no client savings. Fifty-nine percent of corporate clients say they see zero benefit. Firms are merging frantically (59 deals in 2025, at least 16 more announced for 2026) because the math requires scale now. Consulting hit the same wall harder and faster. By 2025, AI roles outnumbered entry-level consultants for the first time in the industry's modern history. Hiring of junior consultants crashed 40 percent from 2023 peaks. The federal government cut $5 billion in consulting spend in early 2025. Clients stopped accepting hourly billing and started demanding outcome-based pricing or contingency fees. Consulting margins are under siege: AI specialists earn 56 percent wage premiums, but junior consultant salaries have flatlined.

The evidence

  • Law: 59 law firm mergers in 2025 (up 18% from 2024) and at least 16 more deals announced for 2026, driven by the mismatch between demand growth (1.9%) and expense growth (9.1%) after firms spent 10% more on AI infrastructure with no measurable client ROI.
  • Consulting: Consultant hiring down 40% from 2023 peaks by 2025; federal consulting cuts of $5B in early 2025 forced layoffs at Deloitte, Accenture, and IBM as clients rejected hourly billing and demanded outcome-based pricing.
  • HR consulting: Market growth decelerated from 6.5% (2024) to 1.4% (2026) as software platforms (Rippling, Paychex, TriNet) eroded the advisory moat and AI spending jumped 50% YoY in 2025.

The human premium

AI is making taste, judgment, and accountability scarce — and whoever can prove a person stands behind the work is winning.

The middle of every service industry is hollowing out. On one side, AI handles the routine: the boilerplate contracts, the commodity websites, the junior consultant hours. What changed

When AI handles the routine, the real value concentrates at the top and bottom — either you need genuine judgment, or you need to trust someone's name is on it. Everything in the middle gets compressed.

Clients no longer pay for hours. They pay for outcomes, or they buy the robot. That shifts who wins. Design firms with strong brands and personalization are commanding 55-80% margins. The judgment work is becoming a luxury product. The labor is disappearing.

The Junior-Role Collapse

AI eats the training work, and nobody has answered who becomes the senior of tomorrow.

For fifty years, junior roles were the entry ticket. A college grad worked their way up: entry-level tax returns taught you accounting, junior associate work taught you law, junior designer work taught you how brands think. AI has automated exactly that work. And now the pipeline is broken.

What changed

AI ate the routine cognitive grind that paid junior wages. Individual tax returns that took a junior four hours now take AI thirty minutes. Contract review that took a junior a day now takes generative AI an hour. Design mockups that a junior prepared now come from an AI website builder.

The work that got automated wasn't the hard stuff. It was the stuff that taught you how to think like a professional. And it paid. A junior accountant in 2025 is cheaper to replace with software than to hire. Law firms burned through record profits in 2025 not by getting more efficient, but by squeezing associates and raising rates. Web design agencies are splitting: the top tier offering strategy commands 55-80% margins, while the commodity tier competes with AI and is vanishing.

The people who used to be squeezed upstream—the training staff who had to take on juniors—are now getting squeezed out. But the people who are hit hardest are the juniors themselves, and the future senior cohort that has no way in.

The evidence

  • Accounting: 87% of finance leaders report talent shortages; CPA exam participation down 30% since 2016; AI now handles 80%+ of individual tax returns (2025).
  • Law: US law firms posted record 2025 profits on rate hikes (fees up 16.8%), not efficiency; but 1.9% demand growth and 9.1% expense growth force consolidation.
  • Web design: AI website builder market reached $6.3B in 2026 (26% growth YoY); 60% of new websites built partly with AI; design labor compressed 20-40% (2026).

Who rides it

The big firms and the software companies. Deloitte, EY, and KPMG expanded in 2025 while smaller accounting firms got rolled up. KPMG tax revenue grew 7.5% in 2025, outpacing competitors. And the design firms that pivoted to strategy and compliance—not commodity delivery—are capturing 55-80% margins. The winners are whoever owns the technology or can scale to invest in it. Smaller practices cannot compete.

Who's squeezed

The junior cohort and the firms that can't consolidate. CPA exam participation dropped 30% since 2016 because the junior role no longer teaches—and no longer pays to learn. Web design juniors are gone; they're replaced by AI builders. Law firm associates are hired at the top ($235K starting pay in July 2026) because the firm no longer wants juniors to train; it wants experienced talent to bill out at premium rates. Mid-market accounting and legal practices are caught: too small to invest in AI infrastructure, too large to compete with freelancers or AI builders on price. And the pipeline of people who could become tomorrow's partners is closing.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

No one is training the next generation of professionals. Accounting firms hiring at the top and compressing the middle. Law firms burning money on AI tools with no measurable client savings, squeezing associate headcount instead. Design shops either going high-margin or vanishing. In five years, who becomes the managing partner, the partner counsel, the principal of the design firm? You cannot jump from college to partner. You have to work up. And the work that used to do that—the junior work—is not coming back.

E · The big picture

The AI Rewiring

Routine cognitive work got cheap; judgment, data, and control got scarce and valuable.

Artificial intelligence is automating the cognitive grunt work that used to justify a salary. Web designers now compete with AI builders. Claims adjusters face machines that process cases 50-70% faster. Radiologists watch algorithms read scans. Portfolio managers discover that their decisions don't beat a $10 ETF. The pattern is everywhere: the routine got cheap, and the value migrated to whoever owns the data, deploys the system, or makes the judgment a machine still can't.

What changed

AI didn't kill these professions. It crushed the middle. Web design used to be a ladder: junior designer to senior strategist. Now it splits into two industries. The commodity tier—websites built by Wix, Shopify, and AI builders reaching $6.3 billion in 2026 with 26% yearly growth—handles 60% of new sites. The premium tier, commanding 55-80% margins, survives by selling brand strategy and personalization to clients who can pay for it. Everyone else goes away.

The same wedge splits every industry in this collection. Claims adjusters faced a dual shock: AI adoption jumped from 8% to 34% of carriers in 2025, with 91% committed to full deployment by end of 2026—cutting cycle time by 50-70% and eliminating the middle-market adjuster. Meanwhile, radiologists watch as hospitals consolidate imaging and integrate AI screening, while independent imaging centers see margins compress and struggle to afford the software they now need. Portfolio management became arithmetic: passive index funds hold 55% of US mutual fund assets as of 2025, up from 53% a year earlier and 34% a decade ago. Only 38% of active managers beat their benchmarks after fees. The math was already bad. AI made it obvious.

What got cheap: routine labor and commodity decisions. What got scarce: proprietary data, deployment infrastructure, and the judgment that can't be automated yet. What got valuable: owning the algorithm, controlling the data it trains on, and having the capital to build scale. Consolidation followed—Accenture acquiring Tokyo design studios, BlackRock buying Preqin for £2.5 billion and HPS for $12 billion to control private-market data and infrastructure, GE HealthCare paying $2.3 billion for Intelerad to embed AI into imaging workflows.

The evidence

  • Web design: AI builders reached $6.3 billion market in 2026 with 26% YoY growth, capturing 60% of new websites while mid-market agencies race to the bottom on price.
  • Claims adjusting: AI adoption by carriers jumped from 8% in 2024 to 91% committed to deployment by end of 2026, promising 50-70% cycle-time cuts that threaten third-party adjusters.
  • Diagnostic imaging: Radiologist shortage reached crisis (961 unfilled residency spots in 2025; 130-day hiring cycle), while hospital consolidation and AI adoption squeeze independent centers facing 2.83% Medicare reimbursement cuts.
  • Portfolio management: Passive funds hold 55% of US mutual fund assets as of 2025; only 38% of active managers beat their benchmarks after fees, driving $1+ trillion in outflows and industry consolidation.

Who rides it

Winners own infrastructure or data. BlackRock controls both—they bought Preqin's private-market data (£2.5 billion in 2025) and HPS's platform ($12 billion in 2024) to dominate alternatives. Large web agencies survive by selling strategy to brands that won't commoditize. Hospitals consolidate imaging and capture 41.5% of outpatient claims. Carriers deploy claims automation in-house and disintermediate the adjuster middle. The pattern holds: scale matters. Consolidators win. Specialists with a judgment moat win. Everyone else competes on price in a market where price keeps falling.

Who's squeezed

Mid-market and solo professionals. The claims adjuster who isn't backed by a carrier or major network faces a choice: retrain as a complex-case specialist or leave. Independent diagnostic imaging centers struggle with 2.83% Medicare cuts and lack the capital to buy AI software they now need to compete. Mid-market web agencies can't afford to match the premium tier's strategy offerings or the AI tier's speed. Active portfolio managers managing conventional stocks and bonds watch their AUM leak away—$1+ trillion in 2025 alone—to passive funds. Radiologists don't disappear; they shift to hospitals and get salaried. The freelancer and small partnership, for now, still exist. They just stopped being the default path.

The main worry

The single biggest risk

The single biggest tension is labor replacement speed exceeding retraining speed. Claims adjusters face 25% retirement by 2027 at the exact moment carriers are automating their jobs. Radiology training takes 5-6 years; the shortage is now, and AI can screen images today. Web design junior roles have collapsed (AI builders + platforms did that), so the pipeline to senior designer dries up. Portfolio managers in their 40s can't easily become alternatives specialists overnight. The worry isn't slow displacement—it's whiplash displacement, where the retraining ladder collapses before new rungs are built. Policy, schools, and firms haven't kept pace with the speed of automation.
