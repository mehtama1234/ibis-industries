Regulated Software and the Admin State

One of the most durable growth engines in the corpus is the need to manage complexity that customers cannot simply opt out of.

A growing slice of economic value comes from software and services that manage mandatory complexity: compliance, identity verification, healthcare documentation, fraud prevention, and audit trails that customers cannot skip. Regulatory burden keeps rising, which creates durable demand that is only loosely tied to end-market growth cycles. Scale and product maturity in this space create defensible, recurring revenue.

Clearest signal

Healthcare administrative overhead, compliance software spending, and identity/fraud verification solutions expand faster than core industry volumes.

The bet

Build or acquire regulatory compliance, audit, identity verification, or reimbursement administration software targeted at regulated industries where enforcement is tightening.

A surprising amount of economic value now comes from mandatory workflows. Compliance, identity, fraud control, reimbursement, testing, reporting, privacy, cybersecurity, audit trails, and certification all create demand that is only partly tied to macro growth. Customers often pay not because they want to, but because they must. That makes regulated workflow businesses structurally different from ordinary discretionary software or services.

This layer matters because the administrative burden of the economy keeps thickening. Healthcare billing and coding grow more complex. Financial identity and fraud systems become more critical as scams industrialize. Cyber controls, documentation, and audit readiness move downmarket. Environmental, safety, and technical standards keep expanding the zones where formal compliance becomes a gate to participation.

The important strategic divide is between businesses that can turn this complexity into repeatable infrastructure and those that still deliver it through expensive bespoke labor. The first category can become a toll-taking layer with recurring economics. The second may still benefit from demand, but it remains vulnerable to margin pressure. In other words, the admin state creates a lot of work, but the highest-value positions are the ones that productize that work.

Deep theme read

A vast section of modern business is now devoted to managing mandatory complexity that customers cannot escape: compliance frameworks, identity verification, reimbursement protocols, audit trails, fraud detection, and privacy controls. These workflows are not innovation or differentiation—they are table stakes. That shift creates durable, non-cyclical demand for software, services, and infrastructure that turns regulatory burden into revenue. Firms that can embed compliance and verification into their operations, or sell those capabilities to others, now occupy a more stable economic layer than those purely competing on product features.

Structural shifts
  • Compliance demand is acting more like a permanent revenue layer than a cyclical add-on.
  • Identity, verification, and fraud control are becoming deeper infrastructure markets.
  • Healthcare and other regulated sectors are monetizing administrative throughput, not just core service delivery.
  • Testing and certification bodies are quietly gaining gatekeeper power.
Core mechanisms
  • Regulatory bodies and payers (Medicare, insurance, agencies) continuously expand documentation, reporting, and verification requirements, creating recurring administrative workloads that cannot be outsourced or avoided.
  • Synthetic fraud, identity theft, and cyber risk are rising faster than law enforcement can address them, making third-party verification, scoring, and monitoring services economically essential across finance, identity, and commerce.
  • Healthcare's reimbursement and claims machinery generates enormous administrative value capture separate from clinical care—coding, prior authorization, eligibility checking, and payment reconciliation alone are billion-dollar workflow categories.
Tensions
  • Customers resent compliance spending even as they cannot avoid it.
  • Manual service demand can be strong while still producing mediocre margins.
  • AI can compress administrative work, but regulated customers still need auditability and trust.
  • Regulatory compliance is a rising cost that hurts smaller, less-integrated players more than scaled incumbents, but it is also a cost that cannot be wished away or automated into nothing—it remains a permanent, defensive business.
  • The more firms and regulators rely on software and automation to manage compliance, the higher the stakes if that software fails, is breached, or becomes a single point of concentration risk.
